If you are new and wondering how to build trust with customers, here is the plain answer. You cannot buy trust, and you cannot fake a track record. What you can do is make small promises and keep them in a big way, give trust before you ask for it, and treat your first mistake as your best chance to prove something.

On September 29, 1982, in the Chicago area, someone put cyanide in Tylenol capsules, and seven people died. Johnson and Johnson, the company that made Tylenol, had done nothing wrong. But its name was on the bottles. Under its chairman, James Burke, the company pulled 31 million bottles, about $100 million of product, and destroyed it. Nobody required them to do it. Tylenol's market share fell from 35 percent to 8 percent in weeks. Within about a year it was back near where it started, and in time it became the market leader again. The crisis also led to the tamper-evident seals we now take for granted.

You are not Johnson and Johnson. You have no 35 percent to lose. That is actually the point. Burke had decades of trust stored up before he ever had to spend it. A new founder starts at zero.

## What trust actually is

Trust is the belief that the other person will do what they said they would do, even when nobody is watching, and even when it costs them something. The economist Kenneth Arrow wrote that virtually every commercial transaction has an element of trust in it.

Think of trust as a tax, or a dividend, on every deal. Where trust is high, business is cheap. You shake hands, you sign a short contract, and the work starts Monday. Few checks, because they are not needed. Where trust is low, everything gets expensive: long contracts, lawyers, inspectors, deposits up front, audits, escrow, and weeks of delay while everyone checks on everyone else. The work is the same. The price is double. Those extras are the bill for missing trust.

When you are new, every customer quietly charges you that tax. It shows up as "let me think about it," as a request for references you do not have yet, as a smaller first order than you hoped for. They are pricing a risk they cannot yet measure.

## Why a new founder cannot buy it

Advertising buys attention. A famous spokesperson buys awareness. Bought reviews buy suspicion. The moment a customer suspects the money bought the message, the trust evaporates, and it takes the money with it.

So do not fake proof. No padded testimonials. No "trusted by" strip built on one free trial. Trust goes up slowly and comes down instantly, and a new founder's account is small, so one bad withdrawal is not a dip. It is the whole balance. Small and real beats impressive and fake.

The good news about having no reputation is that you have not ruined it yet. Every kept promise is a deposit, and early deposits matter most, because they are the entire history your customer has of you.

## Start with promises small enough to beat

The first rule: make promises small, and keep them large. Not small promises kept small. Small promises kept with room to spare.

The plumber who says Thursday and shows up Wednesday morning. The freelancer who quotes Friday and delivers Thursday. The restaurant that says 20 minutes and seats you in 10. Every overshoot is a deposit.

Here is an invented example, a composite, not a real company. Call her Dana, a solo bookkeeper three weeks into her own practice, with no reviews at all. A landscaping business with two trucks is interested, but wary. Its last bookkeeper vanished mid-month, so the owner applies the trust tax: small job first, payment after he checks everything himself.

Dana thinks she can clean up a quarter of messy books in about 4 days. She quotes 7 days and $600. She delivers on day 5, with a one-page note flagging a $340 duplicate vendor payment the owner had missed. Then she gives trust first: "Pay once you have checked the reconciliation yourself."

He pays. He takes her on for monthly books at $250 a month, and mentions her to one other business owner. Nothing dramatic happened. A small promise, kept large, became the first deposit, and the first deposit became [a first paying customer](/blog/value-created/how-to-get-first-paying-customers) who stayed.

When you are new, the promise is the product sample. People cannot judge your whole business yet. They can judge whether Tuesday meant Tuesday. Pick one thing per customer, a date, the scope, how fast you reply, and beat it.

## Give trust first

The rule from the chapter that feels backwards: give trust first. The seller who refunds without a fight gets customers who rarely ask for one. The market stall owner who lets you taste before you buy is not being naive. Trust is a mirror. Extend it, and it comes back multiplied.

For a new founder this is practical. A plain guarantee, in ordinary words. A small paid pilot instead of a big commitment. A first deliverable sent before the invoice, the way Dana did. Each lowers the risk the buyer has to price in, which is also why [a price is not the same as value](/blog/value-created/is-price-the-same-as-value): part of what a new customer is buying is relief from uncertainty.

The grown-up version is the Ritz-Carlton, which is known for giving every employee the authority to spend up to $2,000 per guest, per incident, to fix a problem, with no manager approval. You do not need $2,000. You need a rule, decided in advance. Advertising buys attention. Bought reviews buy suspicion. Kept promises buy trust.

## Your first mistake is the real test

It will happen. A missed date, a wrong number, a job that goes sideways. This is where the chapter's other rule comes in: when you break trust, overcorrect.

Apologies are cheap. Made whole is expensive, and everyone can tell the difference. Johnson and Johnson did not issue a careful statement and wait. They pulled 31 million bottles when nothing required it, and the overcorrection was the message. One genuine overcorrection can leave you more trusted than if you had never failed at all, because now people have seen what you do when it costs you. A mistake is the first time a new customer gets to watch that part.

Dana's version was small. In month two she miscategorized a payment, caught it herself, told the owner before he noticed, fixed it, and took that month's $250 fee off the bill. She did not have to do the last part. That is why it worked.

So decide the steps now, while nothing is on fire. Write down your standing fix, a redo plus a refund or a waived fee, before you need it.

## The line that is not on the balance sheet

Trust never appears on a balance sheet, and it quietly decides every number on it. It is what skill, attention, and problem-solving run on. No trust, and your skill gets no buyers, and every job has to be re-proven, re-contracted, and re-inspected.

None of this is fast. That is the deal. Trust goes up slowly and comes down instantly, so the first few promises you make matter more than anything you will ever say about yourself. Make them small. Keep them large.
