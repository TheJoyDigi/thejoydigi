No. Price is not the same as value, and confusing the two is expensive. A price is the record of one trade between two specific people at one specific moment. Value is what the thing actually does for the person who gets it. The first number is a report about a handshake. The second is a fact about a life.

Start with a bottle of water. At the grocery store it costs 50 cents. Past airport security it costs $5. At a concert, when you are thirsty and there is a line behind you, you would gladly pay $20. Same bottle, same cold water. What changed was the moment: the thirst, the options, the crowd, and the clock.

I used to read the $5 tag as a fact about the water. It is a fact about the airport.

## A price is a handshake, not a measurement

We treat prices the way we treat weights and temperatures, as if they were properties of the thing. The house costs $600,000. The stock costs $40. It feels solid and objective.

But a price, mechanically, is only this: the number where one buyer and one seller shook hands. Change either person, or change the day, and you get a different number for the same thing.

Picture two identical houses on the same street, same floor plan. One sells in spring for $600,000. The other sells in autumn for $520,000. The houses did not change. The cast of characters did.

Or picture a steady business with the same factories, the same customers, and the same profits all year. Its shares trade at $40 in January, $60 in June, and $35 in October. The business sat still. The crowd around it ran laps, euphoric in June and frightened in October.

This connects to an earlier idea in this series: [value lives in people, not in things](/blog/value-created/what-is-value-creation-for-founders). A thing also has two faces. There is its use value, what it does for you, and its exchange value, what other people will trade for it. A price only ever measures the exchange face, and even then only roughly, only for one moment, and only between two people.

## Who actually sets the number

Not the average person. Not the calm majority. A price is set at the margin, by the most eager buyer and the most desperate seller who happened to show up that day.

That is why the airport can charge $5. To a thirsty traveler, the use value of that bottle is enormous: relief, comfort, a headache fading. The $5 does not measure that relief. It measures the seller's leverage. There is no competition past security, the audience is captive, and the clock is ticking.

Prices are also messages. The same fish sells for three shells in one village and eight in another, twenty miles apart. The fish did not change. The market around it did. A high price is often the market saying: bring us more of this. Read a price as information about scarcity, demand, and who is in the room. Then decide. Do not marry the number.

## The price measured desperation, not drywall

Here is a composite scene, invented but built from a pattern anyone who has watched a street sell will recognize.

Call them the moving family. A job across the country means they have to sell this month, so they take the first decent offer that lets them leave on schedule. Three doors down, a seller with no deadline waits 11 months and gets 30 percent more.

Same street. Same kind of house. The difference in price did not measure better drywall, better light, or a better kitchen. It measured one family's deadline against another family's patience.

Hold that picture next time a single price tempts you to make a big claim about worth. One trade is one data point, often about urgency.

## Why prices miss

If a price is the record of whoever was in the room, then every price can be wrong, sometimes a little and sometimes spectacularly. That gap even has a name: mispricing. Four usual suspects create it.

First, urgency. The seller who must sell today accepts a number far below value. The buyer who must buy today pays far above it. Patience is an edge, mostly because so few people have any.

Second, information. Picture a painting at a garage sale with a $10 tag on it. To the seller it is clutter taking up sun in the driveway. To one passing buyer who happens to know art, it looks like a lost masterpiece worth millions. That is the buyer's judgment, not a proven fact, and it may be wrong. But the gap between $10 and what that buyer believes is not luck. It is knowledge meeting opportunity.

Third, emotion. In a panic, sellers accept almost any number just to escape. In a rush of greed, buyers pay almost any number just to join in. The thing did not change. The feelings did.

Fourth, the crowd itself. When few people show up, the most extreme person in the room sets the number. One desperate seller and one sharp buyer can move a price more in an hour than a thousand calm traders move it in a year.

Warren Buffett put the whole distinction in one line: price is what you pay, value is what you get.

## Three questions before you act on a price

So stop asking only, what is the price, as if the tag were the truth. Ask three questions instead.

One: what is this worth to me, in use value, in my life, at my margin? Two: what would the world pay for it, calmly, as exchange value? Three: what does the tag say right now?

Then act on the gap between those answers. When the price sits well below the value to you, that is an opportunity worth a serious look. When the price sits well above it, walk away, no matter how good the story around it sounds.

A warning, because this cuts both ways. Sometimes you will hold the wrong number yourself. You will pay $60 for something you are sure is worth $80, and watch it trade at $35. That does not mean the method failed. It means your estimate was wrong, or the crowd stayed frightened longer than your patience lasted. The answer is never to worship the price instead. The answer is to get better at valuing.

## What this means when the price is yours

Founders meet this from the other side of the handshake. Your price is also one trade, not a verdict on your worth or your work.

When one buyer says no at your price, you have learned something about that buyer, that moment, and that room. You have not learned that your work is worth less to everyone. Read the no as information. Ask the three questions about your own offer: what is the result worth to this buyer, what would the market calmly pay for it, and what did my tag say?

And when you are ready to set that tag well, the practical sequel in this series is [how do I price my services without undercharging?](/blog/value-created/how-to-price-my-services) It covers the other half: your costs set the floor, and the buyer's outcome sets the ceiling.

Next time a number looks like a fact, ask whose hands shook on it, and what was pressing on each of them. Hear the price. Read it as information. Do not marry it.
