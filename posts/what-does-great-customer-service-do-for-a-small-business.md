Great customer service buys a small business four things: trust you cannot buy with advertising, a story people keep telling, loyalty instead of a price race, and a relationship that works as a moat. Service is a relationship compressed into a moment, built one human decision at a time. Here is the story that shows it.

As the story goes, a man walks into a Nordstrom department store carrying two snow tires. He did not buy them there. Nordstrom does not sell tires. He sets them on the counter and asks to return them. The clerk looks at the tires, looks at the man, and hands him $75. No argument. No manager called. Just a refund, for tires, at a store that sells suits.

## What a $75 refund actually bought

Nordstrom lost $75 on that return, but it bought something worth far more. Every person who hears the story thinks the same thought: that is a store that will take care of me. The story has been told in business schools for decades, and it is still paying.

You cannot buy that kind of trust with advertising. You can only earn it by hand, one human decision at a time. I covered the new-business version of this in [how to build trust with customers when you are new](/blog/value-created/how-to-build-trust-with-customers-when-new).

So what was the value, really? It was not the $75. It was the feeling: this place is on my side. There is a kind of value that cannot be discovered, packaged, or shipped. It can only be delivered in person, by a human being, in the moment. A machine can process your return. Only a person can make you feel taken care of.

## Why automation caps service at adequate

The automation pitch is always the same: faster, cheaper, no humans required. Self-checkout, chatbots, automated everything. I will give it this much: for routine transactions, the pitch is right. Nobody wants a heartfelt conversation with their parking meter.

But notice what happens at the top of every market. The most expensive restaurants still have a human take your coat. The best hotels still have a person remember your name. The priciest products come with a human being on the phone. Automation ate the bottom. The top is still handmade.

Here is why. A system does exactly what it is programmed to do, no more. But service lives in the no more. The moment you automate the interaction, you cap it at adequate. Adequate never gets talked about. Adequate never earns loyalty. Adequate competes on price, and commodities compete on price, which is a race to the bottom. The handmade moment earns loyalty instead, and it is the escape from that race.

A chatbot can follow the return policy. Only a human can break the return policy in exactly the right way, at exactly the right moment, for exactly the right reason. Judgment is the product. The refund was just the packaging.

## The services you remember have one shape

Think about the services you actually remember. It is never the efficient ones. Nobody tells stories about a checkout that took thirty seconds.

You remember the nurse who held your hand before the surgery. The mechanic who called to say the repair was cheaper than expected. The barber who remembered your kid's name.

Every one of those moments has the same shape. A human being noticed something, cared about it, and did something extra that no system required. The extra is the value.

<PullQuote quote="The extra is the value." />

That is also why service never fully automates away. It is not that humans are better at the task. The machine is often faster and more accurate. The customer is not buying the task. They are buying the feeling of being cared for by another person. You cannot automate being cared for. The moment you try, the customer can tell, and the magic evaporates. The chatbot that says "I understand your frustration" understands nothing.

## Three habits that build it

So what does great service by hand actually look like in practice? Three things.

First, be present. The phone in your pocket is the enemy of service. The customer can feel the difference between your full attention and your partial attention within seconds. Presence is the cheapest luxury in business, and the rarest.

Second, solve the real problem, not the stated one. The customer who complains about the wait is really saying they feel disrespected. The customer who asks for a discount is really saying they are not sure it is worth it. Listen past the words. The man with the tires did not need $75. He needed to know the store would do right by him. Solve that, and the transaction takes care of itself.

Third, follow up. Service does not end when the customer leaves. The call the next day, the note that says we fixed it, the remembered detail on the next visit: that is what turns a transaction into a relationship. It is a quiet form of the [give value first](/blog/value-created/how-to-give-value-first-without-getting-taken-advantage-of) habit, giving care after the sale when nothing requires it.

And relationships are the most durable asset a business has. They do not show up on the balance sheet, but they are the reason the balance sheet works.

## Try this week

Think of the last service you still talk about. What was the extra, the thing no system required?

Then pick one customer, client, or colleague this week and practice all three habits. Give them your full attention. Listen for the problem behind the words. Follow up the next day.

The human touch is not an inefficiency to be eliminated. It is the moat. Machines do the routine. Humans do the caring. And caring is the one value that cannot be manufactured, only delivered, by hand, one person to another. The tire refund cost $75. The story it bought is still paying dividends decades later.
