Value creation is changing something for someone so that they are better off. A founder does it by finding a specific person with a specific problem and solving that problem. That is the plain answer, and everything else in this article is a footnote to it.

Picture two identical steel kitchen knives. One sits on a hardware store shelf with a price tag. The other is in a restaurant kitchen, where the line cook has been fighting a dull blade all week. Same steel, same factory, same knife. Different worth. The gap between those two knives is the entire subject of value creation, and it is where your business lives.

I spent years building things that were good and valued by nobody. The fix was not better things. The fix was a better question: not "what can I make," but "what changes for whom."

## Value lives in people, not things

There is an old puzzle every economics student meets in the first week. Water keeps you alive and costs almost nothing. A diamond does nothing useful and costs a fortune. If value lived inside things, that would be backwards.

The resolution is that value was never in the thing. It is in the person, in a moment. A bottle of water at a sink is worth pennies. The same bottle at a trailhead on a hot afternoon, three miles from the car, is worth real money to the hiker holding an empty one. The water did not change. The desire, the scarcity, and the context did, and all three belong to the buyer.

That is not a philosophy point. It is the operating fact of your business. You do not set what your work is worth. The person with the problem does, every time they decide whether to hand you money.

## Creating value means changing something for someone

Meet a mobile knife sharpener, call him Marcus. He drives a route of restaurants and sharpens knives on site for $3 a knife. A kitchen with 14 knives is a $42 visit, and he is in and out in twenty minutes.

Notice what Marcus actually sells. The knives were already there. He did not manufacture anything. He changed what those knives do for the cook: from a daily fight to a clean cut. The hardware store down the street sells a whetstone for less than one visit, and it creates no value at all sitting in a drawer, because nobody in that kitchen has the time or the skill to use it. The stone is the same. The change is missing.

That is the test for anything you are building. Name the change. If nothing changes for a specific person when your product exists, you have inventory, not value. Features do not count. Hours do not count. The change counts.

## Creating and capturing are two different jobs

Now meet a baker, call her Rosa. Her shop has a line out the door by 7 a.m. She is, by any fair measure, excellent at creating value. At her lease renewal, the landlord raises the rent $1,400 a month. Why can he? Because he owns the one thing every bakery on that street needs, the corner. Rosa created the morning line. The landlord captured part of what it is worth.

This is the split most founders learn late. There is the value your customer receives, and there is the part of it you can charge for. The two are related, and they are not the same number. A founder can be brilliant at the first job and careless at the second, and end up working for everyone except herself: the landlord, the platform, the supplier who owns the scarce piece.

Capture is not greed, and it is not an afterthought. It is the half of the work that decides whether creating value pays you or just keeps you busy. It gets its own articles in this series. For now, hold the two jobs apart in your head.

## The value sentence

Here is the tool I now use before building anything. One sentence, four blanks:

**I change ___ for ___, from ___ to ___.**

Marcus's version: "I change the knives for restaurant cooks, from a daily fight to a clean cut." Rosa's: "I change mornings for commuters on this street, from nothing open to the best coffee and bread in the neighborhood, before 7 a.m."

<Flow title="Write your value sentence">
  <FlowStep title="Name one person" text="Not a market. One kind of customer with one problem." />
  <FlowStep title="Name the change" text="From what, to what. The cook goes from a daily fight to a clean cut." />
  <FlowStep title="Name the price" text="If you cannot say what it is worth to them, you are not done." />
</Flow>

Try to write yours. If the first blank will not fill, you do not have a product yet, you have a hobby with expenses. If the second blank is "everyone" or "small businesses," narrow it until it is a person you could picture. The sentence feels restrictive. That is the point. Value is created for someone specific, or it is not created at all.

## Start with the sentence

Do this today, on paper: write your value sentence for your current customer, or for your best guess at one. Then circle the word "from." The "from" is the pain you are actually selling against, and it is worth more of your attention than your feature list.
