If you are trying to choose what to build next, start with the problem, not the product. Run your idea through three questions, then ask one more, honestly: is this worth three years of your mornings? Effort does not pick the winner. The problem does.

Consider two builders. Both were brilliant. Both worked 16-hour days for years. One built the best fax machine accessory money could buy, in 2006, just as fax machines were on their way out. The other built a small tool that helped people send money to family across borders, slow and steady, for a decade. Same effort. One ended up with a beautiful product nobody wanted. The other ended up wealthy. The difference was never effort. It was the problem each one chose.

## Why the problem matters more than the effort

You do not get to choose your talent, not really, and you barely get to choose your luck. But you do get to choose what you build, and that choice, made once, echoes through everything else.

Pick the right problem and even mediocre execution carries you. Pick the wrong one and perfect execution just gets you to the wrong destination faster. Effort aimed at the wrong problem is still the wrong problem, which is also why [being busy is not the same as making money](/blog/value-created/why-am-i-busy-but-not-making-money).

## Taste is just trained judgment

So is this about taste? That word makes people nervous. Taste sounds like something you are born with, like perfect pitch. Either you have it or you do not.

Taste is not magic. It is judgment, trained. A chef's palate is not a gift. It is something like 10,000 meals remembered. Judgment works the same way. Every problem you study, every product you take apart, and every customer you watch goes into the file. Over time the file gets thick enough that your gut starts whispering before your head catches up. People call that taste. It is really just evidence, digested.

You train it by studying problems the way a jeweler studies stones. Most people fall in love with solutions. They have a clever app idea and go looking for someone who needs it. Flip that. Go live inside a problem first. Find people who wake up thinking about it, who would pay real money to make it disappear. Learning to tell real pain from mild annoyance is the work I covered in [finding a customer pain point](/blog/value-created/how-to-find-a-customer-pain-point). If the pain is sharp enough, the solution can be clumsy and it still works. If the pain is fake, no amount of polish saves you.

## The three questions

The pattern I keep noticing is that the problems that look exciting are usually the crowded ones. Everyone wants to build the next big social app. The good problems look boring from the outside: freight invoices, dental scheduling, the plumbing supply chain. The best ones are where the incumbents are asleep and the customers are quietly furious.

So run a candidate through the three questions, in order.

First, do people already spend money trying to solve this badly? If they pay for a clumsy workaround, they will pay for a good solution.

Second, does the problem grow if you ignore it? The best problems are rivers, not puddles. They keep flowing whether you build or not.

Third, can you start small and still be useful? If the only version that works costs $10 million, it is not your problem. If a scrappy version helps 10 people tomorrow, it might be. Starting small and charging real people early is how you learn whether anyone will actually pay, the test in [getting your first paying customers](/blog/value-created/how-to-get-first-paying-customers).

## One idea, run through the questions

Here is an invented example, a composite, not a real client. Call her Priya, a freelance operations contractor. Working with small freight brokers, she kept seeing the same chore: staff reconciling carrier invoices in spreadsheets, line by line.

First question: one broker already pays a part-timer about $1,800 a month to chase mismatched invoices. That is a clumsy workaround, and people already pay for it. Second question: as that broker adds carriers, the invoice volume grows, so the problem gets worse if nobody fixes it. Third question: a simple script that matches invoices to rate confirmations could help about 10 brokers within a week or two, with no big platform required. Three yeses, all modest.

Priya also loved a second idea, a full freight marketplace. It passed the first two questions and failed the third. The only version that worked needed capital she does not have. She put it down. That is not a failure of ambition. That is the filter working.

Then she asked the last question: is the invoice problem worth three years of mornings? Her answer was yes, oddly, because she likes the boring work. No fundraising story, no exit story. Just a dull problem she is happy to live inside.

## Big, real, and still wrong for you

A problem can be big and real and still wrong for you. Some problems need capital you do not have. Some need credentials the gatekeepers will never give you. Some take 20 years, and you needed it to work in two. Choosing what to build means choosing something you can actually finish. Judgment is not just seeing the mountain. It is knowing whether you brought the right boots.

This matters because your deep efforts are finite. You get maybe five or six real, all-in bets in a working life, and each one costs years you never get back. Almost everything else in this series is a multiplier: it makes whatever you choose worth more. But a multiplier times zero is still zero.

The cleverest trap is a brilliant idea for a problem you do not actually care about, because you will quit at the first hard winter.

## Try this

Take the idea you are most excited about right now, and write it down as a problem, not a product, in one sentence. Then run it through the questions above. Find one person or business already paying for a clumsy workaround. Ask whether the problem gets bigger if nobody fixes it. Sketch the scrappy version that could help 10 people next week. Run it on two ideas, not one, so you have something to compare.

Then ask the last one honestly: is it worth three years of your mornings? If the answer is no, put it down, no matter how clever it is.

Everything else in this series makes whatever you choose worth more. This is the part that decides what that is. Choose the problem first, and let the product follow.
