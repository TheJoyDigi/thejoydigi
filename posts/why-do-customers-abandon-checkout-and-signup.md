Customers usually abandon checkout and signup because of friction, not because they changed their mind. They wanted the thing. Then the business asked for one small thing at exactly the wrong moment, or added one step too many, and their patience ran out first.

In the mid 2000s, an online retailer had this mystery. Shoppers filled their carts, clicked checkout, and vanished. Prices were fine. The site worked. Between buy and bought, a river of money was disappearing.

Three habits find leaks like that. Watch where people stall: friction lives in behavior, not in opinions. Delete a step before you add a feature: subtraction is where the money is. Measure the wait in seconds: put a number on each wait and one name next to each number.

## The button that cost $300 million

Researcher Jared Spool sat down next to real shoppers and watched. Within minutes, he found the leak: a button on the checkout page that said one word, Register.

Before you could spend a dollar, the site demanded an account. Username, password, password again. Shoppers froze. Some saw a commitment they did not want. Some tried to log in, failed, and gave up. The button was not broken. It worked as designed, and asked one small thing at exactly the wrong moment.

Spool's team changed almost nothing. They replaced Register with Continue, adding one line: you do not need to create an account to shop. The account became optional, at the very end. No redesign, sale, or new features. Purchases jumped 45 percent: $15 million in new sales in the first month, $300 million in the first year. It became the $300 million button. The money was always there. Friction was hiding it.

## Friction does not destroy value, it locks it up

Friction is anything between a person and the thing they want. The extra click. The line at the counter. The form that asks for your life story to deliver a pizza. Each one feels small. But friction is never one big wall. It is a thousand tiny toll booths, and every booth takes its cut in attention, in time, in willpower. A customer who wanted your product at the first booth can be gone by the fifth. When a good product stalls, the product is not always the problem: I wrote about that in [why do great products fail to sell](/blog/value-created/why-do-great-products-fail-to-sell).

In 1937, an Oklahoma City grocer named Sylvan Goldman noticed something. People stopped buying when their arms were full, not when their wallets were empty. The basket they carried was the ceiling on the sale.

So Goldman invented the shopping cart: a folding chair frame with wheels and two wire baskets. Nobody used it. Men thought it looked unmanly. Women thought it was a baby stroller. So he hired models, well dressed men and women, to push the carts and fill them up. Shoppers copied them. Sales soared. He had changed no products, prices, or advertising. He had removed one physical limit, the carrying capacity of two arms, and the trapped value poured out.

Goldman did not create new groceries. The $300 million button did not create new shoppers. Friction does not destroy value. It locks it up. Every hassle is a lock on a door, and behind it sits money, loyalty, and goodwill that already belongs to you. Removing friction is the closest thing business has to free money: collecting what is already yours.

## The tax your spreadsheet never shows

Friction has a price tag, and it is bigger than you think. In 2006, an Amazon engineer named Greg Linden described tests that added artificial delays, a tenth of a second at a time. Every 100 milliseconds of delay cost Amazon 1 percent of its sales. Around the same time, Google found that a half second delay caused traffic to drop 20 percent. Your customers are busy people, and the moment a page stalls, their attention is already somewhere else.

<Stat value="1 percent" label="Sales lost for every 100 milliseconds of delay at Amazon" note="Measured in 2006 tests described by Greg Linden" />

In 1999, Amazon patented one click ordering: buy with a single click, no cart, no checkout, no forms. Amazon sued a competitor over it, and Apple later licensed it. That patent is not really a technology. It is the legal ownership of a removed hassle: one click instead of six.

The tax never appears on any spreadsheet. The company only sees the customers who made it through. It never meets the ones who gave up at the password field, so it congratulates itself while a fortune walks out the back door.

## Three things to do this month

Rule one: watch where people stall. Do not ask them. Watch them. Customers say everything is fine on a survey, then a recording shows them clicking the same button four times, sighing, and leaving. Spool found the button by sitting next to shoppers, not polling them. Goldman found the limit by watching arms, not comment cards. Once a month, sit next to a real customer and watch. Watching shows you where people stall. Whether they will pay is a separate test, and I wrote about that one in [how to get first paying customers](/blog/value-created/how-to-get-first-paying-customers).

Rule two: delete a step before you add a feature. Every business loves to add a field, a screen, a verification. Almost nobody subtracts. A travel site had one optional field labeled Company, right under the address line. Customers typed their company name into the address box by mistake, the credit card check failed, and the booking died. That field cost $12 million a year. The fix was not a better field. It was no field. Before your next launch, ask what to remove, not what to add.

Rule three: measure the wait in seconds, and make somebody own it. In most companies nobody owns the waiting: page load belongs to engineering, hold music to support, the line to nobody. Pick the three waits your customer feels most, put a number on each, and put one name next to each number. Amazon's 100 millisecond rule was a budget, enforced like money, not a slogan. Every second of hold music is a second of your customer's life spent without permission. Treat their time like your money.

## Why you cannot see it yourself

Friction is invisible to the people who built it. You know your checkout because you designed it, so it feels obvious. It is not obvious to a tired parent on a phone at midnight, one eye on a crying baby. The $300 million button looked reasonable inside that company, until someone watched a stranger use it.

Your business is full of toll booths you cannot see, because you built them and stopped looking. This month, sit next to one customer, watch without helping, and count the pauses, repeated clicks, and sighs. Then remove one toll booth instead of adding anything new. The value on the other side is already yours.
