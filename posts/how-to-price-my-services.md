If you are asking this question, you are probably doing the math the way I used to. Add up your costs, add a margin, divide by your hours, and hope nobody winces at the number. That math is not wrong. It is just answering a different question. It tells you the lowest price you can survive at. It says nothing about what the work is worth to the person buying it.

Here is the tell that your price is wrong: you are booked solid, you are tired, and the money is still not there. When that happens, demand is not your problem. The number is.

Take a solo bookkeeper, call her Dana. She charges $45 an hour, because that is roughly what she earned at her old firm, and it felt honest. She has a waiting list. She also has no evenings. Dana does not need more clients. Dana needs to learn what her price is actually made of.

## Your costs set the floor, not the price

Start with Dana's real math, not the hopeful version.

She works about 40 hours a week, but only 25 of those are billable. The rest goes to email, invoicing, chasing receipts, fixing her own software, and the hundred small tasks nobody pays for. Add self-employment taxes, health insurance, her bookkeeping software, a laptop every few years, two weeks of vacation, and the occasional sick day she currently works through.

Spread her target income over the hours she can actually sell, and her honest floor is closer to $70 an hour. She was charging $45. That gap is where her evenings went.

<Stat value="$70" unit="an hour" label="Dana's honest floor, once unbillable time is counted" note="About 40 hours a week, only 25 billable. She was charging $45." />

This is what cost-based pricing is good for: finding the minimum. It is a survival calculation. The mistake is stopping there and treating the floor as the price. A floor tells you when to walk away. It was never meant to tell you what to ask.

## The buyer's result sets the ceiling

Now look at the same Dana through one client's eyes.

One of her clients owns a small restaurant. His books were a shoebox for years. Last spring he got a surprise tax bill of $11,000, and his accountant told him clean monthly records would have cut it by roughly a third. Nobody enjoys paying taxes they did not have to owe.

So ask the question Dana never asked: what is a clean year worth to him? Not in hours. In his terms. No surprise bill. No April panic. A set of numbers he can actually borrow against. If that peace of mind is worth $4,000 a year to him, then a monthly fee of $250 is not expensive. It is a fraction of the pain it removes, and it is less than the cost of one bad surprise.

That is the ceiling, and it has nothing to do with Dana's costs. The same work, priced from the buyer's side, supports a completely different number. Costs set your floor. The buyer's outcome sets your ceiling. A good price lives between them, much closer to the ceiling than most of us dare to put it.

## A low price tells a story you did not intend

There is a second problem with pricing low, and it is not arithmetic. It is storytelling.

Imagine two plumbers quoting the same drain job under your kitchen floor. One says $90. The other says $240 and puts the guarantee in writing. You cannot see the work once the floor is back down. You have no way to judge the quality directly, so you judge the signal you can see: the price.

Plenty of buyers privately assume the $90 plumber cuts corners. For work the buyer cannot evaluate, price is the first piece of evidence they get. A low price does not read as generous. It reads as a warning. When you undercharge, you are not just leaving money on the table. You are telling a story about your own work that you never meant to tell.

## Say the number, then stop talking

The last skill is delivery, and it is mostly about silence.

Here is the version I used to give: "So, um, that would be around $250 a month, but that includes the cleanup, and if it feels like a lot we could start smaller, and honestly the first month might be less because..." Every qualifier is a small discount offered before the buyer asked for one. I was negotiating against myself, out loud, for free.

Here is the better version: "It is $250 a month. That includes monthly books, payroll, and a cleanup of this year so far." Then stop. Let them respond. Silence feels long to the person who just named a price. To the buyer, it just sounds like a person who knows what their work costs.

And when someone says "that is more than I expected," do not drop the price. Ask two questions instead: what did you expect, and what is getting this handled worth to you? Half the time the gap closes itself once the result is said out loud.

## Raise it in three steps

You do not have to fix years of undercharging in one frightening afternoon.

1. **New clients first.** Quote the new price to the next person who calls. They have no old number to compare against, and you get practice saying it.
2. **Give existing clients notice and a date.** A short note: rates change on the first of next quarter. People respect a date. They resent a surprise.
3. **Count the noes.** Some people will say no, and that is information, not failure. If literally nobody says no, your price is still too low. A healthy price gets refused sometimes.

## The one-page exercise

Do this before your next quote, on paper, in about fifteen minutes:

1. Calculate your true floor, including the unbillable hours and the vacation you keep not taking.
2. Write down the result the customer gets, in their words and their dollars, not yours.
3. Set your price at a fair fraction of that result, comfortably above your floor.
4. Say it once, plainly, and stop talking.

Price is not a confession of what the work costs you. It is a claim about what the result is worth. Make the claim like you believe it, because your buyer will take their cue from you.
