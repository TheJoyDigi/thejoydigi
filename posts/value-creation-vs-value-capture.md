Charles Goodyear spent five years trying to make rubber usable, experimenting in a borrowed laboratory, then in his kitchen, then in debtors' prison. In 1839, legend says, he dropped a mixture of rubber and sulfur onto a hot stove, and it did not melt. It vulcanized. He had found the process behind every tire and every rubber sole since.

Goodyear died penniless, with debts of $200,000, and never saw a cent of the rubber billions. Nearly 40 years later a tire company took his name. If you are asking why founders create value and still do not get paid, Goodyear is the short answer: he won the creating game and lost the capturing game completely.

## Two games, two rulebooks

Creating value and capturing value are two different games, with two different rulebooks. Creating is making something people want: the invention, the recipe, the song, the software. Capturing is arranging the world so the money flows back to you: the contract, the brand, the distribution, the price.

The market does not pay you for creating value. It pays you for capturing it. Most of us were taught the opposite: make something good, and the money will follow. Sometimes it does. Often it follows someone else.

This series made a related split earlier, in [price is not the same as value](/blog/value-created/is-price-the-same-as-value): create use value for a person, and capture exchange value in a trade. The gap between the two is where a business lives. No gap kept by you, no business for you.

## Follow the bottleneck

Once you see the two games, you start seeing who actually gets paid. The farmer who grows your coffee captures pennies per cup. The cafe on the corner captures $5, for the cup, the chair, and the logo. The app developer does the creating, and the app store takes a cut, often 30 percent, for owning the shelf. A research lab at Xerox pioneered the graphical interface and the mouse, and Xerox captured almost none of it, while Apple and Microsoft turned those ideas into fortunes.

None of that is a conspiracy. Value flows to the scarcest piece of the chain. If 10,000 people can make a thing but only one can sell it, the seller eats. Creation is abundant. Capture is scarce.

That scarce piece is the bottleneck: the step in the chain from raw material to customer that is hard to replace. It might be a patent, a brand, a distribution network, a relationship with the buyer, or the one machine nobody else owns. Whoever controls the bottleneck captures the margin. Everyone else splits what is left.

Goodyear had no bottleneck. His process could be copied, his patents were tied up in court, and he had no factory, no brand, and no customers of his own. There was nothing about his position that the world had to pay for. So ask the chapter's question about everything you make: who captures this, and is it me? If the honest answer is someone else, you do not have a business yet. You have a donation.

## A tool Dana built and did not own

Here is a composite scene, invented, but built from the pattern this chapter describes. Call her Dana, a freelance developer hired by a small chain of physical therapy clinics to build a scheduling tool. The front desks love it. It ends double bookings and fills cancelled slots. Dana charges one build fee, does good work, and moves on. There are no written terms about who owns the code, what happens if the chain grows, or what she is paid if more locations use it.

A year later the chain has rolled the tool out across all its locations. Dana gets one awkward email thanking her again. She created the value. She owns nothing scarce: no contract, no per-location fee, no customer list that is hers, and no relationship beyond one contact who has since left. A bigger vendor can offer a near copy, and Dana has no position to negotiate from.

Now rewind her deal. Same tool, same skill, one different afternoon. Before she builds, she and the chain agree in writing who owns what, that each new location pays a license fee, and that she keeps the right to sell a version of the tool to other clinics. Nothing about the product improved. The capture did.

## The better product, the better deal

History has a cleaner version of Dana's problem. In the summer of 1980, IBM was building its first personal computer and needed an operating system. The obvious choice was CP/M, made by Digital Research and run by Gary Kildall, a brilliant programmer whose system ran on nearly every small computer in America. Legend says Kildall went flying that day, his lawyer argued with IBM over a nondisclosure agreement, and IBM walked away.

IBM took its business to a tiny software shop called Microsoft, run by a 24-year-old named Bill Gates. Microsoft had no operating system, so Gates bought one, a rough program called QDOS, from a small Seattle company for $50,000. Then he licensed it to IBM, with one condition buried in the contract: Microsoft kept the right to sell it to anyone else. When the clone makers arrived, every machine ran Microsoft's system.

Kildall made the better product. Gates made the better deal. The best position won. That retained right is the second rule below in action: get it in writing, before it works. The deal gets made when the value is small and everyone is friendly, or it gets fought over when the value is huge and nobody is. Robert Kearns invented the intermittent windshield wiper, watched the car companies take it, and spent decades suing them to get paid. Decide who owns what and who gets paid how much while it is still cheap to decide.

## Three rules for keeping what you make

The chapter gives three rules. First, own the scarce piece. Ask what part of your chain the world cannot route around, then own that part: a patent filed early, a brand people remember, a customer list that is yours, a channel you control. Tesla treated the charging network, not the batteries, as scarce, and built its Supercharger stations. Apple treated the app store, not the phone, that way.

Second, get it in writing before it works, as the Kildall story shows. Third, build the business, not just the product. Most creators spend 90 percent of their energy on the thing and 10 percent on pricing, selling, distribution, and brand. Flip that ratio. The iPod was not the first MP3 player. What set it apart was everything around it: a store, a brand, and an ad campaign. Customers cannot buy what they never see, which is also why [getting the first paying customers](/blog/value-created/how-to-get-first-paying-customers) is a capture test, not just a sales test. And [pricing your services](/blog/value-created/how-to-price-my-services) is one of the capture skills you will use every time.
