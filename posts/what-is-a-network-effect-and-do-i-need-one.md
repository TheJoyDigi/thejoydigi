A network effect is when a product gets more valuable to each person the more people use it, because the users are the ones manufacturing the value. Do you need one? That depends on one test. You need one only if your product is useful mainly when other people are on it, or each customer makes it better for the next one.

If you are in network territory, three rules decide most of it. Build for the second user, not the first: the real product is the invitation, the reason to bring someone along. Seed where people gather, and borrow a crowd before you try to build your own. Protect the signal, because growth that brings noise destroys the thing it was supposed to build.

## The telegraph that connected two places

In 1844, Samuel Morse tapped out the first telegraph message, from Washington to Baltimore. It was a marvel of the age. Copper wire, electromagnetic clicks, words traveling faster than any horse. And for a while, it connected exactly two places on Earth. The machine was extraordinary. The network was worth almost nothing, because there was almost nobody on it.

That gap is the whole lesson. A product's value is not just what it does. It is who else is on it. Every new user does not add one unit of value. They add value to every user who was already there. The hundredth person to join makes the previous 99 richer, just by showing up. That is why networks feel like they appear out of nowhere and then feel inevitable. The value was being built by the crowd, quietly, one arrival at a time.

## Same machine, different value

When Alexander Graham Bell demonstrated the telephone, the most common reaction from investors was polite dismissal. It was a toy for the rich, they said. A curiosity for people with money to burn. They were staring at the device, which meant they were staring at the wrong thing. The device never mattered. What mattered was that, within a few decades, a telephone in your house meant you could reach anyone who also had one. Bell's company did not really sell mouthpieces and earpieces. It sold the size of the crowd. Every new subscriber was a gift to all the existing ones.

Value lives in the connections, not in the nodes. A telephone with one subscriber is a sculpture. A telephone with a million subscribers is a necessity. The same machine. Completely different value. The price can stay fixed while the value keeps rising, because nobody at the phone company had to invent a new feature to make your phone more valuable. Your neighbors joining did it for free.

The fax machine makes the mechanism visible. The first one sat in an office like a paperweight. Then the second fax machine was sold, and the first one turned into a business. The company that built the first commercial fax machines nearly gave up. It sold a handful of units, and the buyers complained, not because the machine was bad, but because there was nobody to fax. Then a few large companies bought the machines in bulk, for use inside their own offices. Suddenly every fax machine that had ever been sold became more useful, including the ones in offices that had already given up on theirs. No software update. No new feature. No price change. The product you already sold got better after the sale, and you did not lift a finger.

## It shows up everywhere, and it can run backward

A marketplace with ten sellers is a ghost town. A marketplace with ten thousand sellers is a destination, and every buyer who arrives makes it more attractive to the next seller, who makes it more attractive to the next buyer. The flywheel spins both ways. A language nobody speaks is trivia. A language a billion people speak is a career. The value was never in the dictionary. It was in the speakers.

Networks also have a dark mirror. The same mechanism that makes them valuable makes them fragile. Every new user adds value, but only if they add signal. When the new arrivals bring noise, spam, scams, or cruelty, the network gets bigger and worse at the same time. More users, less value. A network is a commons. It rewards the builder who protects it and quietly punishes the one who just counts heads. The platforms that last are the ones that learned to defend the conversation, not just grow the crowd.

## Do I actually need one?

Here is a test I draw from that definition, and it is a question, not a slogan. If every other customer vanished tomorrow, would your product get worse for the one who is left? If yes, you are in network territory: your product is useful mainly when others are on it, and each customer makes it better for the next. If no, you have a product, not a network. It works the same for the first customer as it would for the thousandth, and that is not a failure.

Many businesses have no network effect and should not fake one. A good product that is not selling is a different problem, mostly one of distribution, and I wrote about that in [why do great products fail to sell](/blog/value-created/why-do-great-products-fail-to-sell). Forcing a community, a feed, or a marketplace onto a product that works fine alone can add noise instead of value. You can still borrow a crowd and give people a reason to bring a friend. They just are not a network effect, and calling them one does not make them one.

## Three rules if you do

Rule one: build for the second user, not the first. If your product is only useful when other people join, then your real product is the invitation, the reason to bring someone along. Ask what that reason is before you ask about features. Alone, your product may honestly not be worth much yet.

Rule two: seed where people gather. The cold start is the whole game, because every network begins as two paperweights. The fax company solved it by selling to big companies first, so each buyer was really buying a small network, not a single machine. The telephone companies ran their wires down the busiest streets first, where each new subscriber could immediately reach the most people. Borrow someone else's crowd before you try to build your own. That overlaps with [how to get customers without running ads](/blog/value-created/how-to-get-customers-without-running-ads), which is about being useful where customers already gather.

Rule three: protect the signal. The moment the network fills with noise, the value drains out of every single connection, and no feature, no redesign, and no marketing budget will put it back.

Most of us are not building the next telephone, and that is fine. Think of a product or group you use because other people are on it, and ask what made you join: who was already there, and who invited you? Then look at what you are building and ask the same question about it. What is the reason someone would bring a friend? The most valuable thing you can build is not a product people use alone. It is a place people bring each other. The winners look inevitable in hindsight. They were not. They were just the ones who understood that value lives in the crowd.
