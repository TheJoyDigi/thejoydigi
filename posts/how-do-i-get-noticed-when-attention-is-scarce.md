You get noticed by earning attention, respecting it, and never stealing it. Content is infinite. Attention is not. There is no trick, because attention is the one thing that cannot be manufactured.

In October 1994, the first banner advertisement in history appeared on a website called HotWired. It was a small rectangle for the phone company AT&T. It said: "Have you ever clicked your mouse right here? You will." Forty-four percent of everyone who saw it clicked. Nearly half. Today, the average banner ad gets clicked by about 0.05 percent of the people who see it. Same rectangle. Same internet. The ads did not change. Attention got scarcer, because there is so much more competing for it now.

## Why attention got scarce

The attention business is older than the internet. In the 1830s, the penny press figured out it could sell newspapers for a penny and sell its readers to advertisers for far more. Radio and television followed, and the internet industrialized it. In 1971, a political scientist named Herbert Simon gave the whole game its truest sentence: a wealth of information creates a poverty of attention. He was 50 years early.

More video is uploaded every single minute than you could watch in a lifetime. Millions of podcasts. Billions of posts. And on the other side, a fixed number: 24 hours in a day, about 16 of them awake. That is a fixed lifetime budget, and every creator on earth is bidding for a slice of it.

This is the great inversion. For most of human history, the scarce thing was content. Books were rare. News traveled by horse. Then the internet flipped the table. Information became free and infinite, and scarcity moved into the human mind. The scarce thing is no longer the message. It is the moment of a mind actually receiving it. Scarcity is part of what makes things valuable, and attention is the purest scarcity in the economy today.

## What a slice of attention costs

In 2024, a 30-second commercial during the Super Bowl cost $7 million. That is over $200,000 per second. Nobody was buying television time. They were buying the only thing that cannot be manufactured: 100 million human minds, all pointed in the same direction at the same moment.

Reed Hastings of Netflix was once asked to name his biggest competitor. He did not say Disney. He did not say HBO. He said: "We compete with sleep." Netflix fears your bedtime, not other entertainment companies. Because attention is finite, and every hour you give to one thing is an hour you can never give to another. You are in the same auction, without the $7 million.

## Earn it or steal it

Wherever scarcity lives, a market forms. This one is the attention economy. The platform gives you the content for free, harvests your attention by the hour, and sells it to advertisers by the slice. The users are not the customers. The users are the inventory. If you are not paying for the product, you are the product being sold.

None of this is a conspiracy. It is arithmetic. But there are two completely different ways to capture attention, and they lead to opposite destinations. Earning it means giving someone something genuinely worth their minutes. Stealing it means hijacking their wiring: the outrage headline, the infinite scroll, the little red notification badge engineered to itch until you scratch it. Both work this quarter. Only one of them compounds.

Study the thieves. Clickbait promises what the article never delivers, and trains you never to come back. Outrage farming keeps you furious, because furious people do not log off. The feed learns what makes you react, not what makes you better. Stolen attention always arrives with an invoice, and the invoice always comes due. What it burns is trust. I wrote about that in [how to build trust with customers when you are new](/blog/value-created/how-to-build-trust-with-customers-when-new).

The earners play the opposite game. The teacher whose class nobody skips. The writer whose letter you actually open. They treat your attention the way a good bank treats your money: spend it carefully, never waste it, and return more than they took. So you keep depositing more.

## The three rules in practice

Rule one: earn it with value, before you ask for anything. Give first. The free sample. The genuinely useful guide. The help with no pitch attached. Show up useful, over and over, and attention follows on its own. Nobody owes you their focus. You rent it, and the rent is value delivered in advance.

Picture the local plumber with a three-week waiting list who has never run a single ad. His customers did his marketing for him, one kept promise at a time. I wrote about that pattern in [how to get customers without running ads](/blog/value-created/how-to-get-customers-without-running-ads).

Rule two: respect it once you have it. Attention is a loan, not a gift, and the interest rate on disrespect is brutal. The meeting that could have been a message. The video with 90 seconds of throat clearing before the first real sentence. Every wasted minute teaches one lesson: this person does not respect my time. And respect, once spent, is the most expensive thing in the world to buy back.

Rule three: never steal it. No manufactured outrage. No bait and switch. No fake countdown timers, no dark patterns, no drama engineered to hold eyeballs that would rather be somewhere else. Attention gained by theft converts into nothing that lasts. No loyalty. No trust. Nobody who stays. You rented a crowd and woke up owning an empty room.

## Your own attention is an asset too

Where you place your focus is where your life goes. Two hours a day of idle scrolling is 730 hours a year: more than a full month of waking life, handed to strangers who sell it to advertisers. Guard your attention the way you guard your savings, because it purchases the same things: your time, your judgment, your future.

The people richest in attention are usually the stingiest with their own. They do not doomscroll. They decline the meeting. They protect long, unbroken stretches of focus. Scarcity cuts both ways. Spend your attention like capital, not like pocket change.

## Try this week

For one day, keep a tally of where your waking hours go, and mark each block earned, if it gave you something real, or taken, if it held your eyes and gave little back. Then look at one thing you make for other people and ask: does it earn the minutes it asks for?

People give their attention to whatever solves their problem, eases their pain, or answers their question. The tricks decayed, from 44 percent to about 0.05 percent, and the law held. Give people something real, and the scarcest resource in the economy flows toward you.
