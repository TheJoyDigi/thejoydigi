A business moat is whatever makes you hard to replace, hard to copy, or hard to leave. You build one by layering several small advantages a competitor must cross at the same time, and by starting while business is good.

I used to think a great product was the whole job. The chapter opens with a chef who did everything right and still closed. This post covers six kinds of moat, one test you can run today, and a weekly habit for digging yours.

## Daniel had value and no defense

In the late 1990s, a chef named Daniel opened a small restaurant in Chicago. The food was extraordinary. Within a year there was a line out the door every night, and critics called it the best new restaurant in the city.

Then, 18 months later, a restaurant opened across the street. Same style, same menu almost dish for dish, prices 20 percent lower. Within a year, two more copies opened on the same block. Daniel cut prices, then portions, then staff. Three years after the line out the door, he closed.

Two neighborhoods over, a bakery tells the other half. Nothing fancy. But the owner spent ten years learning the names, the birthdays, and the usual orders of half the street. When a cheaper bakery opened next door, her customers did not move. As one of them put it, "I am not buying bread. I am buying my morning."

Daniel created enormous value. It did not matter, because nothing stopped anyone else from making the same food for less. Creating value is only half the game. The other half is keeping it.

## Castle, treasure, water

The investor Warren Buffett made the moat idea famous. He looks for businesses with a wide moat, a durable competitive advantage that keeps rivals out. Think of a castle. The castle is your business. The value you create is the treasure inside. The moat is the water around it. No moat, and anyone with a ladder walks in and takes the treasure.

Most founders obsess over the treasure and ignore the water. They perfect the product, launch to applause, and only then discover that applause is not protection.

## Six kinds of water

A moat is manufactured scarcity: the scarcity you build on purpose. There are six kinds worth naming, because you cannot build what you cannot name.

The first is secrets. Coca-Cola has kept its formula secret for more than a century. Anyone can make brown sugar water. Nobody can make Coca-Cola, because nobody knows exactly what is in it. A secret is the cheapest moat to dig, and one of the hardest to cross, as long as it stays secret.

The second is network effects. The first telephone was useless. The millionth was priceless, because every new user made it more valuable for everyone already on it. I wrote about that in [what is a network effect and do I need one](/blog/value-created/what-is-a-network-effect-and-do-i-need-one). The chapter calls this the widest moat in modern business, and the hardest to dig from nothing.

The third is switching costs. Your bank, your accounting software, the tool your whole team learned. Leaving is such a hassle that you stay even when mildly unhappy. Every hour a customer invests in learning your product is another gallon of water.

The fourth is brand, trust stored up over time. That is why [how to build trust with customers when you are new](/blog/value-created/how-to-build-trust-with-customers-when-new) belongs in a post about defense, not just marketing. The fifth is scale. If you make 10 million units, your cost per unit is lower than anyone making 10,000, and you can set prices nobody smaller can survive. The sixth is permission: a license, a patent, a regulation that says only you may do this.

The best businesses rarely rely on just one kind. They layer them.

## Four shallow moats beat one deep one

Take a neighborhood coffee roaster. Anyone can buy the same beans and the same roasting machine. But this owner has a roast profile he spent five years tuning, and he never wrote it down. That is a secret. Half his regulars are on a subscription, beans at the door every two weeks, and canceling would mean finding a new morning ritual. Those are switching costs. He knows 300 customers by name, and they bring their friends. Those are relationships. And the shop has become a local landmark with a 15-year reputation. That is brand.

Four shallow moats, layered, together are wider than any single deep one. A competitor with twice his money could copy any one layer. Copying all four at once, in the same location, with the same history, is a different job. You do not need the widest moat in the world. You need several a competitor must cross at the same time.

## Every moat drains

For decades, owning a taxi medallion in New York was one of the safest moats in business. The city issued only a fixed number. No medallion, no taxi. Medallions sold for more than a million dollars apiece, and the owners collected rents behind that government-built moat for generations.

Then a company with an app asked what if the car is not a taxi. Within a few years, medallion prices collapsed by about 80 percent. The moat was not attacked. It was made irrelevant, and no amount of lobbying filled it back up.

So no moat lasts forever. Secrets leak. Patents expire. Networks get disrupted. Scale gets undercut. A moat is a maintenance job, not a one-time achievement. The moment you believe yours is permanent is the moment it starts draining.

## The one-question moat test

Run this test on whatever you are building. If a competitor with twice my money opened across the street tomorrow, what would still be mine?

If the answer is nothing, you are Daniel, creating value on borrowed time. If you do have answers, rank them. Your secret recipe, the process only you know. Your relationships, the customers who would follow you if you moved. Your reputation, trust built one kept promise at a time. Your speed. Your depth in one narrow niche too small for giants to bother with.

Most small builders will never have a patent or a network effect. They can have the other moats, and those are real. The bakery owner had no patent. She had mornings. Nobody could copy her mornings.

## Dig before you need it

Moats are built in good times and tested in bad ones. While the line is out the door, lock in the long lease, deepen the relationships, document the secrets, earn the reputation, and learn the niche three levels deeper than anyone else. Daniel dug no moat while he was winning, so he had nothing when the copies arrived.

Once a week, ask what would stop a well-funded copycat from eating what you built that week. Then widen your moat a little: call a customer, write down a process, or learn the thing one level deeper. Small moats, dug consistently, become wide ones.

Value gets you in the game. A moat keeps you in it. Creating something people want is the entry fee. Protecting it is the whole rest of the career. I am still digging mine, and some weeks my honest answer to the test is thinner than I would like. That is the point of asking weekly, while there is still time to dig. Build the treasure. Dig the moat. Keep digging.
