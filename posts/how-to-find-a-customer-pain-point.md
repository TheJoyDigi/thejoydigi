Three moments, one wallet. You buy a loaf of bread for $4 without thinking. An hour later you stand at a window for 10 minutes, admiring a leather bag, doing math you would never do for bread. That night, at two in the morning, water starts pouring through your kitchen ceiling. The emergency plumber names a price that would make you laugh at noon. You say yes before he finishes the sentence.

The bread was a reflex. The bag was a fantasy. The plumber was a rescue. Same person, same money, three different decisions. The difference was not you. It was the well the value was drawn from.

So why will anyone pay? Because something in their life is unmet, missing, or hurting, and they pay fastest when it hurts. If you are searching how to find a customer pain point, the working answer is: find something that hurts right now and has a clock on it. Every purchase draws from one of three wells: needs, wants, and pain. If you cannot tell which well your customer is drinking from, you will price wrong and pitch wrong. It sits under the first idea in this series, that [value lives in people, not in things](/blog/value-created/what-is-value-creation-for-founders).

## Needs: the crowded well

Needs are the things a person must have: food, water, shelter, sleep, medicine. Without them, you suffer and stop functioning. Needs are the oldest well, and the most crowded.

The cruel property of a need is that it is mostly already met. Almost nobody is bidding for their first loaf of bread. They are choosing between 40 loaves, and when a need is met 40 ways, sellers compete until the well becomes a commodity market.

So bread is deeply valuable and nearly worthless at the same time. Valuable because you must eat. Worthless because everyone sells it. Needs mean enormous volume, thin margins, and a customer who is not grateful, just comparing. Build only on needs, and you had better be the cheapest or the closest.

## Wants: where the margins live

Wants are the things a person desires but does not require: the leather bag, the sports car, the corner office, the phone upgrade. Nobody needs these. That is why they can be profitable.

A need has a ceiling, because the body only needs so much bread. A want has no ceiling, because desire has no ceiling. The bag is not priced on leather and thread. It is priced on a feeling: identity, status, beauty, belonging. The product is the feeling of being the person who owns it, which is why a want's price says little about the thing itself: [price is a handshake, not a measurement](/blog/value-created/is-price-the-same-as-value).

But wants fade. The sixth slice of pizza thrills you less than the first, and the second designer bag thrills you less than the first one did. Wants are fashion, and fashion changes. Build on wants and you live on a treadmill, inventing the next desire before the old one expires. A want can also wait.

## Pain: the deepest well

Pain is what hurts right now: the burst pipe, the toothache at two in the morning, the laptop frozen before the big presentation. Pain is the deepest well, and the reason is time. A need can wait until morning. A want can wait until payday. Pain has a clock, and the clock says now.

Watch the flooded homeowner. She is not comparing plumbers, reading reviews, or negotiating. She is buying the fastest rescue available. The plumber's price is not compared against other plumbers. It is compared against the cost of doing nothing: a flooded kitchen, a ruined floor, a night of no sleep. Against that bill, almost any number looks small.

We seem to be wired for this. Psychologists have found that a loss tends to feel roughly twice as heavy as a gain of the same size, about 2x the weight. The fear of loss beats the hope of gain, and nothing feels more intense than pain.

That is why the person selling relief names the price, while the person selling bread takes whatever the market gives. The locksmith at midnight is not selling a $20 lock. He is selling the end of standing outside your own door in the cold. Needs keep you alive. Wants make life sweet. Pain makes you act now.

And the deepest veins of pain come back. A burst pipe happens once. Payroll comes every two weeks. Taxes come every year. Backups, security, accounting, insurance: recurring pain is why the subscription exists. Solve one that returns on schedule, and the customer pays you monthly, never to feel it again.

## One bookkeeper, two wells

Here is a composite scene, invented, but built from a pattern I keep seeing. Call her Dana, a bookkeeper who sells a pack of neat invoicing templates for $15. A few people buy, politely. Mostly she gets shrugs, because a nicer invoice is a want, and wants can wait.

Then Dana asks a client, a freelance electrician, about his worst Tuesday. It is not invoicing. It is chasing $6,000 in unpaid invoices while a truck payment comes due. The late money costs him $200 a day in late fees and jobs he cannot take while he is on the phone begging to be paid.

So Dana starts selling the rescue instead: she chases the invoices, makes the awkward calls, and gets him paid, for a monthly fee priced against the $6,000 and the $200 a day, not against her hours. He did not sleep on it. He asked when she could start.

## Three questions that find the pain

You find a pain point by listening, not by brainstorming products. Three questions do the listening.

First: whose pain is this? Name a real person, not a market. Markets do not feel pain. People do. If you cannot name the person and describe their worst Tuesday, you have not found a well. You have found a guess.

Second: what does the pain cost them for every day it continues? Not in feelings, in numbers. The flooded kitchen costs a floor. The frozen laptop costs the contract. Price your solution against the cost of the pain, not the cost of your effort.

Third: would they pay to make it stop today, not someday? Someday is where wants live. Today is where pain lives. If the answer is someday, you are selling a want. Price it like a want, and do not be surprised when the customer sleeps on it.

## Real pain, not manufactured pain

One warning before you go digging: there are two ways to work the third well. Relieve real pain, and you are creating value. Manufacture fake pain, and you are doing something else: the countdown timer that resets when you refresh the page, the virus warning sold by the people who planted it, the scarcity invented to force your hand.

The test is simple. If you stopped selling tomorrow, would the pain still be there? The burst pipe does not care about your marketing. Build on pain that exists without you.

Try this. Pick one problem and run the three questions on it in writing. If the third answer comes back someday, you have found a want, so price it as one or keep looking. A quick payment, without haggling, is the proof of which well you drew from, the same proof this series pointed to in [how to find out if customers will actually pay](/blog/value-created/how-to-get-first-paying-customers). And once you know the cost of the pain, [pricing your services](/blog/value-created/how-to-price-my-services) gets easier: the buyer's outcome, not your effort, sets the ceiling.
