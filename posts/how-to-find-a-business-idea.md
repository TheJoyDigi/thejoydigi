You do not find a business idea. You find a problem someone would pay to end today, and the idea is what you build after you have stared at that problem long enough. If you are searching how to find a business idea, that is the working answer: collect problems, not products.

Nobody woke up wanting a shipping container. They woke up with cargo rotting on the dock. The container came later. That order, problem first and product second, is the whole method. It is also the foundation this series started with: [value lives in people, not in things](/blog/value-created/what-is-value-creation-for-founders), in what changes for a real person when a problem ends.

## Nobody bought the tool

Look at three ordinary rescues. A driver locked out of her car in the rain, keys on the seat, phone almost dead, pays a locksmith and is dry and moving again in about 20 minutes. A restaurant sits empty on a Friday night, its problem invisibility, and one honest review from the right critic fills it. A nurse copies patient charts by hand for 12 hours a shift, and a simple tool fills them instead, so the tired errors stop.

Nobody in those scenes bought a slim jim, a review, or a software tool. They bought the end of a problem. The shipping container did not invent trade, it solved loading a ship taking days and breaking things. The barcode did not invent selling, it solved slow checkout and guesswork inventory. From the outside each one looks like an invention. From the inside, each one is a rescue.

Say it as a formula: value equals the problem solved, minus the cost of solving it. Find a real problem, solve it well, and charge less than the pain it caused. There is no other source.

## Products first is backwards

Most idea hunting runs the other way. Someone starts with a solution, builds the app, polishes the product, and only then asks the terrible question: who is this for? That is solution-first thinking, and it is why so many startups die with good technology and no customers. The problem was never found. Only the solution was built.

Problem-first thinking watches first and builds second. The tool that freed the nurse was not designed in a meeting room. Someone watched her copy charts for days, felt how heavy the twelfth hour was, and only then asked what could lift it. The creator's job is not to invent. It is to notice.

## Real pain vs mild annoyance

So how do you tell a real problem from a mild annoyance? Use one brutal test: would someone pay you to make it stop, today? Not download it. Not try a free trial. Pay, today, with real money, to end this specific misery.

If the answer is yes, you have a problem worth pursuing. If the answer is maybe later, or that is interesting, or let me think about it, you have a hobby with a logo. A mild annoyance gets a polite nod. Real pain gets a wallet, because pain has a clock on it, the distinction this series drew in [needs, wants, and pain](/blog/value-created/how-to-find-a-customer-pain-point).

## A spreadsheet at the dental office

Here is a composite scene, invented, but built from a pattern I keep seeing. Call her June, who runs the front desk at a small dental office with six people on staff. June keeps a spreadsheet of insurance pre-authorizations that three people update by hand, because the office system cannot do it. Next to the keyboard is a sticky note listing which payers are slow.

The invented cost is modest, and that is the point: about 5 hours a week spent retyping the same information, plus a few missed claims a month. Nobody calls this a business problem. They apologize for it. Sorry, our system is a mess, they tell patients on hold. That apology is the problem leaking out.

Run the test on it. Would the office manager pay this month to make the retyping stop? In this scene, yes, without a long meeting. That yes does not prove a company. It proves a real problem, close enough to watch, with a cost you can count.

## Three moves for collecting problems

You collect problems by watching real work, not by brainstorming products in a quiet room. Three moves do most of the work.

First, live where the problems are. Not in the brainstorming session, but in the nurse's twelfth hour, the restaurant's empty Friday, and the rain outside the locked car. Problems do not announce themselves in meetings. They leak out in complaints, workarounds, and the things people apologize for.

Second, listen for the workaround. Every workaround is a problem wearing a disguise: the spreadsheet three people maintain by hand, the drawer full of phone chargers because the battery never lasts, the notebook where the nurse writes what the software cannot capture. Wherever people have built a clumsy bridge, there is a river that needs a real one.

Third, price the problem before you price the solution. Ask what it costs per day, per month, and per year while it stays unsolved. The empty restaurant loses a Friday. The hand-copied charts lose hours and risk worse. The locked car loses the meeting, the flight, the day. That cost is the ceiling on what your solution can charge, which is why [pricing your services](/blog/value-created/how-to-price-my-services) starts with the buyer's cost, not your effort.

## Two filters before you commit

Two filters keep a real problem from becoming the wrong business. First, is this the problem they actually feel? Some problems are load-bearing. The couple arguing about the dishes does not have a dish problem. The company with a toxic manager does not have a process problem. Solve the wrong problem brilliantly and you will be resented, not paid.

Second, should you be the one to fix it? The test is not whether you can see the problem. It is whether you are close to it, understand it, and are already equipped to end it. Proximity is part of the solution. The nurse's charts needed someone who had watched the twelfth hour. The locked car needed someone nearby with the tools.

Size helps, but it is not a filter by itself. A headache pill solves a small problem for billions of people. A cancer drug solves an enormous problem for far fewer. Both can work, as long as the multiplication is real. And remember the shelf life: solved problems stop paying. The tenth headache pill is worth less than the first. Value is a temporary property of an unsolved problem.

Try this for one day. Listen for workarounds and write down three. For each one, write what it costs per month while it stays unsolved, whether someone would pay to make it stop today, and whether you are close enough to be the one who fixes it. The one that survives all three questions is worth pursuing, and the next step is the plain proof this series described in [how to find out if customers will actually pay](/blog/value-created/how-to-get-first-paying-customers): charge for the smallest version and see who pays. One problem is a job. A pipeline of problems is a business, and a pipeline starts with a list, not a logo.
