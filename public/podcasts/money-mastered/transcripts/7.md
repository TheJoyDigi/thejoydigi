**Alex:** This morning, one company did something with its own stock that was bigger than anything ever done in the history of American business. Nvidia announced it is spending one hundred fifty billion dollars to buy back its own shares. Not building factories. Not hiring engineers. Buying its own stock. And its shares went up on the news. So here is the question I want to open chapter seven with. What exactly did Nvidia just buy? What is a share, really?

**Jordan:** I will be honest. I have always treated a share like a little casino chip with a ticker symbol on it. It flashes red, it flashes green, you try to buy low and sell high. I never really stopped to ask what the chip represents.

**Alex:** And that is exactly where part two of this book begins, because for six chapters we have talked about money itself. The dollar, inflation, the Fed, interest rates, the money supply, the global system. Now we are crossing into markets, and the first thing in markets is this. A share of stock is not a chip. It is a slice of a real, living business. When you buy one share of Nvidia, you become a part owner of Nvidia. Of the chip designs, the data centers, the cash in the bank, and yes, the future profits. A small part, but a real part.

**Jordan:** Okay, so it is like slicing a pizza. The company is the whole pizza, and a share is one slice.

**Alex:** That is the cleanest image there is, and it unlocks almost everything. The pizza is divided into a fixed number of slices. Nvidia has tens of billions of slices outstanding, and each slice entitles you to one slice's worth of everything the company owns and earns. The price of a slice is simply what the last buyer and seller agreed on. Each slice trades for over two hundred dollars, which is why the whole pizza is valued at around five point four trillion dollars.

**Jordan:** Let me slow down on one thing. You said the slice entitles me to a share of the profits. But companies do not just hand me cash every quarter because I own a share. Most of the time, nothing shows up.

**Alex:** Right, and this is where we need to be precise, because there are two kinds of claims on a company, and they are opposites. Remember chapter four, when we talked about bonds. A bond is a loan. The company promises you fixed payments, and if the business fails, you stand near the front of the line. Equity, which is what a share is, stands at the very back of the line. If the company fails, the lenders get paid first, and shareholders get whatever is left, which is usually nothing. That is the risk layer. But here is the trade. The bondholder's return is capped. Equity has no ceiling. If the pizza grows ten times bigger, every slice grows ten times bigger. Nobody caps your slice.

**Jordan:** So equity is the leftover claim. The riskiest seat in the house, but also the seat with no limit on the upside.

**Alex:** Exactly. And that explains something enormous about markets that we will come back to in chapter fourteen. Over long stretches of time, stocks have returned more than bonds. Not because of luck. Because investors demand to be paid for sitting in the riskiest seat. Economists call it the equity risk premium. No risk premium, no reason to own the back of the line.

**Jordan:** The pizza image is working. But here is what I have always wondered. Why does a company sell slices of itself at all? If you built a great business, why give away ownership? Why not just borrow money, or keep the profits?

**Alex:** Sometimes borrowing is not enough, and sometimes the founders want out. Think about the birth of a company. Two founders in a garage own one hundred percent. To grow, they need far more money than any bank will lend a garage startup, especially at the interest rates we talked about in chapter four. So they sell slices to early investors. Then the company grows, those investors want their money back, the founders want to turn some of their paper wealth into real wealth, and the company wants a war chest for the future. So it holds an initial public offering, an I P O, the first sale of slices to the general public. That is the moment the pizza moves from a private kitchen to a public market.

**Jordan:** And after the I P O, does the company just keep selling new slices whenever it wants?

**Alex:** It can, but most of the time it does the opposite. And this is where today's Nvidia news becomes a lesson. A buyback is the reverse of an I P O. Nvidia is spending one hundred fifty billion dollars to remove slices from the pizza. Fewer slices means every remaining slice is a bigger percentage of the company. If earnings stay the same and the slice count shrinks, the earnings per slice go up automatically. Investors love that math, which is why the stock rose on the announcement. And it is the single largest buyback in American history, which tells you something about how much cash this company is generating.

**Jordan:** Hold on. I want to make sure I am not missing a trick here. When Nvidia buys back shares, does the money go to the company, or to the shareholders who sold?

**Alex:** To the shareholders who sold. The company hands cash to whoever is willing to sell their slices back. Think of it as the company shrinking the pizza so each remaining slice is worth more, while paying cash to the people who gave up their slices. It is one of the two ways a company returns value to its owners. The other is the dividend, a direct cash payment per slice. Dividends are the old-fashioned way. Buybacks are the modern way. Same idea. Owners get paid.

**Jordan:** Let me list what I actually get as a shareholder, to keep it straight. One, a claim on the profits, paid out as dividends or buybacks. Two, voting rights?

**Alex:** Yes, voting rights. Each slice usually carries one vote, and shareholders elect the board of directors, who hire and fire the chief executive. In practice, small shareholders rarely swing a vote, and many founders keep special slices with extra votes so they stay in control. But the right exists, and occasionally it matters, like when a large investor builds a big position and demands change.

**Jordan:** And three, the right to sell my slice to someone else. Which is the entire stock market.

**Alex:** Which is the entire next chapter. But there is a fourth thing you get, and it is the most underrated. Limited liability. Your downside is capped at zero. If the company borrows billions and collapses, the creditors cannot come after your house. The worst thing that can happen to a shareholder is the slice goes to zero. That asymmetry, unlimited upside and capped downside, is a huge part of why equity built the modern economy. It lets millions of strangers fund risky ventures they would never touch if their personal savings were on the line.

**Jordan:** That is a great point. Without limited liability, who would ever fund a chip startup? You would be personally on the hook for the factory.

**Alex:** Precisely. Now let me add one more layer, because this connects everything from part one. A stock price is not really about today. It is a bet on all the future profits of the business, pulled back into today's dollars. And that pulling back is done with interest rates, chapter four again. When rates are high, future profits are worth less today, so stock prices get pressed down. When rates fall, the same future profits are worth more, and prices rise. This is why the market fell this morning. The ten year treasury yield hit five point two three percent, the highest since two thousand seven, and every slice of every pizza in America got a little cheaper in present value terms.

**Jordan:** So this morning's selloff was not about companies suddenly being worse. It was the discount rate moving. The pizzas are the same. The math on the slices changed.

**Alex:** Beautifully put. The businesses did not change overnight. The price of money changed, and the price of money reprices everything. That is the sentence chapter four was building toward. Stocks, bonds, houses, all of it. And notice the connection to chapter five as well. When the Fed was running Q E and flooding the system with liquidity, risk assets soared. When liquidity tightened, they sank. Stocks live downstream of money. That is the whole reason we spent six chapters on money before touching a single share.

**Jordan:** Let me try the recap, and you tell me what I am missing. A share is a slice of a real business. It is one claim among many, but it is the leftover claim, last in line, with unlimited upside. Companies go public to raise big money, let early investors cash out, and create a currency they can use. As an owner, I get a cut of the profits through dividends and buybacks, a vote, the right to sell, and limited liability, so my loss stops at zero. And the price of my slice is really a bet on future profits, discounted by interest rates, which is why rate moves shake the whole market.

**Alex:** That is a complete recap. The only thing I would sharpen is the mindset shift. Stop seeing a stock as a ticker that flashes red and green. See it as a business you partly own. Tickers are noisy. Businesses are real. Over years, the price of the slice follows the size of the pizza. Over days, it follows fear, greed, and the ten year yield. Chapter seventeen will be all about telling those two apart.

**Jordan:** Then here is the one key takeaway for this chapter. When you buy a stock, you are not buying a lottery ticket or a casino chip. You are buying ownership of a living business, and the value of that ownership comes from the profits the business will earn, which is why the price moves with earnings and with interest rates.

**Alex:** That is the takeaway. Own the business in your mind before you own the slice in your portfolio.
