**Alex:** Welcome back to Money, Mastered. Imagine a stock market where every trade, every wallet, and every movement of every share was written in a public notebook that anyone on earth could read, forever. You would not need to guess what investors were doing. You could watch them do it.

**Jordan:** That sounds impossible for normal markets, and also a little unsettling.

**Alex:** For stocks it is impossible. For Bitcoin and other public blockchains, it is close to everyday reality. That is our subject today, chapter thirty one, on-chain basics, what to watch. Supply, flows, and a handful of simple metrics that let you see behavior directly, instead of only watching price argue with itself.

**Jordan:** Before we dive in, a quick honesty check. In chapter thirty we learned crypto cycles are real but not a clock. Is on-chain data the crystal ball that fixes that?

**Alex:** No, and that warning belongs at the very start. On-chain data is evidence, not prophecy. It tells you what holders actually did, in the past and up to the present moment. It does not tell you what they will do tomorrow, and it does not tell you why they did it. Used well, it improves your questions. Used badly, it becomes a fancy way to fool yourself.

**Jordan:** Good. Start simple, then. What does on-chain actually mean?

**Alex:** On-chain means recorded on the blockchain itself. In chapter twenty eight we learned that Bitcoin is a shared ledger maintained by thousands of computers. Every time bitcoin moves from one address to another, that movement is written into a block, and that block becomes part of a permanent public record.

**Jordan:** An address is like an account number, but not tied to a name.

**Alex:** Exactly. An address is a long string of letters and numbers that can receive and send coins. One person can control many addresses, and one address on an exchange can hold coins for thousands of people. That single fact explains both the power and the limits of everything in this chapter.

**Jordan:** So we can see the coins move, but we often cannot be certain who moved them.

**Alex:** Right. On-chain analysis is watching movements and balances, and then making careful, humble guesses about what those movements probably mean. Anyone who speaks about it with total certainty is selling something.

**Jordan:** All right, layer one. What is the first thing worth watching?

**Alex:** Supply, because supply was the heart of chapter twenty seven. Bitcoin has a hard limit of twenty one million coins, and new coins arrive on a schedule nobody can vote to change. On-chain data lets you verify that story yourself instead of trusting it. You can see how many coins exist today, roughly nineteen million and counting toward that limit, and how many new coins miners create each day.

**Jordan:** That is different from a company, where the board can issue new shares and dilute you quietly.

**Alex:** Very different, and it goes deeper than the total. On-chain data splits supply by how long coins have sat still. Coins that have not moved in more than a year are often called long-term holder supply. Coins that moved recently belong, loosely, to short-term holders.

**Jordan:** Why does stillness matter?

**Alex:** Because stillness is behavior. When the share of supply held for a long time keeps rising while price is flat or falling, it usually means experienced holders are quietly accumulating and refusing to sell. When long-held coins suddenly wake up and move in large numbers during a big rise, it often means older holders are taking profits into strength.

**Jordan:** Often, usually, loosely. I notice you are choosing careful words.

**Alex:** Because an old coin moving is not always a sale. Someone may be moving coins to a safer wallet, combining addresses, or shifting custody. The movement is a fact. The reason is an interpretation. Keep those two separate and on-chain data will serve you well. Mix them up and it will mislead you with great confidence.

**Jordan:** Layer two, then. You mentioned flows.

**Alex:** Flows are where most beginners should spend their time, especially exchange flows. An exchange is where most people buy and sell, as we covered in chapter eight. So analysts watch how many coins move into exchanges, and how many move out.

**Jordan:** Let me guess the logic. Coins moving onto an exchange are one step away from being sold, and coins leaving an exchange are often heading to private wallets for holding.

**Alex:** That is the standard reading, and as a tendency it makes sense. Large, sustained inflows during a rally can warn that holders are preparing to sell. Steady outflows after a fall can suggest buyers are taking their coins home, which reduces the supply sitting ready for sale.

**Jordan:** And the catch?

**Alex:** There are several. An inflow might be a trader posting collateral, not preparing to sell. An outflow might be an exchange moving its own coins between its own wallets. And because one exchange address can serve thousands of customers, the data is grouped in ways that hide individual intent. Flows are a weather report, not a court verdict.

**Jordan:** So watch the trend over weeks, not a single scary headline about one big transfer.

**Alex:** Exactly. One whale moving coins makes a headline. A month of steady net outflows makes information. That distinction, signal over a series versus excitement over a single event, is the same discipline we built in chapter twenty five on backtesting and journaling.

**Jordan:** Layer three. What simple metrics should a normal listener actually know by name?

**Alex:** I will give you five, and only five, because a dashboard of fifty charts is a dashboard you will never use. First, realized price and the idea behind a famous ratio built on it. Every coin can be valued at the price where it last moved, instead of todays price. Average that across all coins and you get a kind of cost basis for the whole network.

**Jordan:** So if the market price is well above that average, the typical holder is sitting on a profit, and if it is below, the typical holder is underwater.

**Alex:** That is the intuition behind the market value to realized value ratio, often shortened to a set of initials. When it runs very high, holders have large unrealized profits and history suggests more temptation to sell. When it falls near or below one, the average holder is near break even or at a loss, a zone that has often appeared near deep lows. Often, again, not always.

**Jordan:** Second metric?

**Alex:** Active addresses, a rough count of how many distinct addresses sent or received coins on a given day. It is a crude measure of network use. Rising activity over months suggests growing real use. Falling activity suggests interest is fading. It is crude because one person can use many addresses, and one exchange address can serve a crowd.

**Jordan:** Third?

**Alex:** Hash rate, from chapter twenty eight. That is the total computing power securing the Bitcoin network. A hash rate that trends upward over time generally signals miners investing in the network and strong security. A sharp drop can follow a price fall, a policy ban, or miners switching off unprofitable machines, and it usually recovers as the system adjusts its difficulty.

**Jordan:** So hash rate is more about health and security than about tomorrows price.

**Alex:** Precisely. Treat it as a vital sign, not a trading signal. Fourth, stablecoin supply and exchange balances, which connects crypto back to Part One and liquidity. Stablecoins are tokens designed to hold a steady value, usually one dollar. When stablecoin supply grows and balances on exchanges rise, there is more ready buying power waiting inside the system.

**Jordan:** Like dry powder.

**Alex:** Like dry powder, yes, though powder can also be waiting to leave. And fifth, for Ethereum, which we met in chapter twenty nine, watch total value locked in smart contracts and the fees users actually pay. Fees are real demand in its simplest form. People do not pay meaningful fees, day after day, for a network nobody uses.

**Jordan:** Five is manageable. Realized value for holder profit and loss, active addresses for use, hash rate for security, stablecoin balances for ready liquidity, and fees where smart contracts are involved.

**Alex:** A clean recap already. Now the part most guides skip. How can on-chain data mislead you, even when the numbers themselves are accurate?

**Jordan:** I was waiting for that layer.

**Alex:** Four traps. First, identity is fuzzy. Labels like whale wallet or exchange wallet come from outside firms making educated guesses. Sometimes those guesses are wrong, and a story built on a wrong label collapses.

**Jordan:** Second?

**Alex:** Internal moves look like market moves. Exchanges and custodians regularly shuffle coins for security. On a chart that can look like a giant holder buying or selling, when economically nothing changed hands at all.

**Jordan:** Third?

**Alex:** Much trading now happens off the chain. When you buy bitcoin on an exchange, that trade usually settles inside the exchanges own books, not on the blockchain. Big price moves can therefore happen with surprisingly little on-chain movement, and quiet on-chain days can hide frantic trading.

**Jordan:** And fourth?

**Alex:** Survivorship in storytelling. After a big rise, someone can always find an on-chain chart that seemed to predict it. After a big fall, someone finds a different chart. If a metric only gets shared when it was right, you are seeing marketing, not evidence. That is why chapter twenty five matters here. Write your reading down before price moves, journal what the data said and what you concluded, and score yourself later.

**Jordan:** That turns on-chain watching from entertainment into a process.

**Alex:** That is the whole point. Let me give you a simple weekly routine a beginner can actually keep. Once a week, same day, spend ten minutes. Check whether long-term holder supply is rising or falling. Check whether exchanges gained or lost coins on balance that week. Glance at the realized value picture to ask whether the typical holder is in deep profit, deep loss, or somewhere in between. And glance at hash rate only to confirm the network looks healthy.

**Jordan:** Four checks, ten minutes, once a week. And what do I do with the answers?

**Alex:** Mostly, you adjust your humility, not your portfolio. If long-term holders are accumulating, outflows are steady, and the typical holder is near break even after a long fall, you can say the evidence leans toward a base being built. That is a description, not a buy signal. If coins are flooding onto exchanges, long-held supply is falling fast, and unrealized profits are extreme during a euphoric rise, you can say risk is elevated. That is a warning to respect the risk rules from chapter twenty two, not a command to sell everything.

**Jordan:** So on-chain data changes the quality of my judgment at the margins. It does not replace valuation, risk management, or a plan.

**Alex:** Beautifully said. It is one lens among several. Price tells you what the market did. Volume, from chapter twenty one, tells you how much conviction was behind it. On-chain data adds something neither can give you in stock markets, a direct look at what holders of the asset itself are doing with their coins.

**Jordan:** Let me try the full recap. On-chain means the public record written on the blockchain itself. From it we can watch supply, including how much is held by long-term holders who have not moved their coins in over a year. We can watch flows, especially coins moving into exchanges, which sit one step from a possible sale, and coins moving out, which tend toward holding. And we can follow a few simple metrics, realized value for the networks cost basis, active addresses for use, hash rate for security, stablecoin balances for ready buying power, and fees on smart contract networks for real demand.

**Alex:** Keep going, you are on a roll.

**Jordan:** And the limits are just as important.
