**Alex:** Welcome back to Money, Mastered. Imagine two traders who find exactly the same good idea on exactly the same day. A year later one is still trading and quietly ahead, and the other has emptied his account and quit. The difference was rarely the idea. It was how much each bet when the idea was wrong. Today we learn the math of survival, risk management and position sizing.

**Jordan:** That is a sobering place to start, because my instinct has always been that trading is mostly about finding the right entry. Pick the right stock at the right moment and the rest takes care of itself.

**Alex:** That instinct is exactly what Chapter Nineteen warned about. Finding ideas is the visible part of trading, so it gets all the attention. But every careful trader shares a quieter obsession. Before asking how much they might make, they ask how much they will lose if they are wrong, and they decide that number before placing the trade.

**Jordan:** So risk management comes before the trade, not after it starts going badly.

**Alex:** Before, always. Start with the cruelest arithmetic in markets, the asymmetry of losses. Lose ten percent, and you need about eleven percent on what is left to get back to even. Lose twenty five percent, and you need thirty three percent. Lose fifty percent, and you need to double your money, a gain of one hundred percent, just to stand where you started.

**Jordan:** Wait, let me make sure I heard that right. Lose half, and you do not need to gain half back. You need to gain all of it back, on a smaller pile of money.

**Alex:** Exactly, and it gets worse fast. Lose seventy five percent and you need three hundred percent to recover. Lose ninety percent and you need nine hundred percent. The hole grows much faster than the ladder out of it. That explains the first law of trading. Job one is not making money. Job one is not losing so much that the math turns against you forever.

**Jordan:** That connects back to Chapter Fourteen on drawdowns. But there we were investors holding through a fall. Here the trader chooses the size of each loss in advance.

**Alex:** That is the key difference, and it is the trader's one great advantage. An investor who owns a stock owns whatever fall comes. A trader decides, in advance, what a single wrong idea is allowed to cost. That decision has a name, risk per trade, and careful traders keep it remarkably small. A common starting rule is one percent. On any single trade, you risk no more than one percent of your whole account.

**Jordan:** One percent sounds almost too small. If I have ten thousand dollars, that is only one hundred dollars at risk on a trade. How does anyone make progress risking so little at a time?

**Alex:** By understanding what that small number buys you, time and survival. Risk one percent per trade and hit a streak of ten losses in a row, which will happen to every trader sooner or later, and you are down roughly ten percent. Painful, but you are still in the game. Risk ten percent per trade, hit that same streak, and the losses compound down to about sixty five percent. Same ideas, same bad luck, and one trader is bruised while the other is nearly finished. Even a good system loses several times in a row just by chance. Flip a fair coin enough times and you will eventually see eight or nine heads in a row, and trading is no kinder. Risk management makes sure a normal run of bad luck is an inconvenience instead of an ending. Now be precise about one word beginners use loosely, risk. Risk is not how much money you put into a trade. It is how much you will actually lose if the trade goes wrong and you exit where you planned to exit.

**Jordan:** That is a distinction I would have missed. If I buy one thousand dollars worth of a stock, is my risk one thousand dollars?

**Alex:** Only if the stock goes to zero and you ride it there. A careful trader plans an exit in advance, a price where the idea is proven wrong, which we will build properly in the next chapter. Say you buy at fifty dollars and decide that at forty five your idea was wrong and you are out. Your risk is five dollars per share, the distance between entry and that exit. That distance, not the price of the stock, is what you size against.

**Jordan:** Okay, so now give me the actual formula, slowly, because I think this is the heart of the chapter.

**Alex:** It is, and happily it is simple arithmetic with three pieces. Your account size. Your risk percentage, which gives you your risk in dollars. And the distance from entry to exit. Divide your risk in dollars by that distance, and the answer is how many shares you may buy. A full example. Your account is ten thousand dollars. You risk one percent, so your budget is one hundred dollars. You buy at fifty dollars, your planned exit is forty five, so you risk five dollars per share. One hundred divided by five gives you twenty shares.

**Jordan:** Twenty shares at fifty dollars each is a position worth one thousand dollars. So I put in one thousand dollars, but I only risk one hundred dollars, because if the price falls to forty five, I sell twenty shares, lose five dollars on each, and lose one hundred dollars total, exactly my budget.

**Alex:** Perfect, and notice what just happened. The size came out of the math at the end. It was never a feeling, and never how much cash happened to be sitting there. Now change one piece. Same account, same budget, same fifty dollar entry, but this stock is wilder and your honest exit has to be forty dollars, because a tighter exit would be shaken out by normal wiggling. Now you risk ten dollars per share. One hundred divided by ten gives you ten shares.

**Jordan:** So a wider exit automatically means a smaller position. The risk stays at one hundred dollars even though the stock is more dangerous, because I own fewer shares.

**Alex:** That sentence deserves to be underlined. Position sizing makes a calm stock and a wild stock cost you the same amount when you are wrong. Beginners do the opposite. They buy the same number of shares of everything, so their wildest trades quietly carry two or three times the risk of their calm ones, and they never chose that. It chose them.

**Jordan:** What about the other side of the trade, the reward? Risking one hundred dollars to make twenty dollars sounds like a bad deal even if I win often.

**Alex:** It is, and that brings us to the second number to know before entering, the reward to risk ratio. It compares how much you stand to gain if the idea works to how much you stand to lose if it does not. Risk one hundred dollars where a realistic target would make two hundred, and that is two to one. If the realistic gain is only fifty dollars for that same risk, you are risking two dollars to chase one.

**Jordan:** And that ratio changes how often I even need to be right, does it not?

**Alex:** Profoundly. At two to one, you can be wrong more often than right and still come out ahead. Picture ten trades. You lose six at one hundred dollars each, six hundred lost. You win four at two hundred each, eight hundred won. You were wrong sixty percent of the time and still finished two hundred dollars ahead. Reverse it, risk two to make one, and you can win most trades and still slowly bleed, because each loss erases two wins. Professionals call the average outcome of all this expectancy, and expectancy, not the win rate alone, decides whether a system actually works.

**Jordan:** I want to raise a temptation I can already feel in myself. Suppose I have lost three trades in a row. The next idea looks really good. Why not risk three percent this time, just once, to win it back faster?

**Alex:** Because that impulse is how small holes become deep ones. Raising your size after losses means your biggest bets happen exactly when your results are worst and your judgment is most emotional. Careful traders do the opposite in spirit. Their risk is a fixed rule, or it shrinks as the account falls. It never grows in anger. There is also a related guardrail, total open risk. Take five trades at one percent each and you have not risked one percent, you have risked five percent at once, and if they are all similar, say five technology stocks that fall together on the same headline, that five percent can arrive on the same morning.

**Jordan:** Correlation again. We met it in diversification in Chapter Fifteen. Five different tickers can still be one bet wearing five costumes.

**Alex:** Exactly the same idea, wearing trading clothes. So traders set a ceiling on total risk across all open trades, often around four to six percent, and count similar trades as partly the same trade. Add one more guardrail many firms enforce, a daily loss limit. Lose, say, two or three percent in a day, and you stop for the day. Not as punishment, but because a trader who is down and frustrated is about to make the worst decisions of the month.

**Jordan:** That is honest. Now, I have heard a fancy term in this area and I want your plain version of it. People talk about the Kelly criterion, a formula that supposedly tells you the perfect bet size. Should a beginner use it?

**Alex:** Understand its lesson, then use far less than it suggests. In plain words, Kelly says there is a growth maximizing size for a bet with a known edge, and betting beyond it lowers long run growth while making the ride far rougher. The catch is that it demands you know your true win rate and true reward to risk, and in real markets you only estimate those, usually optimistically. Bet the full amount on an overestimated edge and the drawdowns can be deeper than most humans can sit through. So experienced traders use a small fraction of it, or stay with the fixed one percent rule.

**Jordan:** So the humble rule beats the clever formula for almost everyone, because it still works when your estimates are wrong.

**Alex:** That is the spirit of this chapter in one sentence. Risk management is not about being clever. It is about building a plan that survives your own mistakes, bad streaks, and worst moods. Here is the checklist. Before any trade, write down four numbers. Your account size. Your risk percentage, and therefore your risk in dollars. Your entry price. And your exit price if you are wrong. Then let the formula tell you the size. Check the realistic reward against that risk, and check your total open risk. Then, and only then, place the trade. And once it is placed, that size decision is finished. You do not double it mid trade because you suddenly feel more confident, and you never gamble bigger after a loss to win it back.

**Jordan:** All right. First, losses are asymmetric. Lose fifty percent and you need one hundred percent just to recover, so avoiding the deep hole matters more than chasing the big win. Second, I decide my risk before each trade, starting with the one percent rule, so a normal losing streak costs me roughly ten percent instead of ending me. Third, risk means the distance from entry to my planned exit, times my size, not the size of the position itself. Fourth, position size is a formula, risk in dollars divided by that distance, so wilder trades get smaller. Fifth, reward has to justify risk, because at two to one I can be wrong more than half the time and still finish ahead. And sixth, I watch total open risk and stop for the day at a set loss limit, because my worst enemy on a bad day is me.

**Alex:** That is a complete recap, and if you truly internalize it you are already ahead of most people who trade. So here is the one key takeaway, stated plainly. You cannot control whether a trade wins, but you can always control how much it costs you when it loses. The traders who survive decide that cost in advance, keep it near one percent, and let a simple formula, not a feeling, set the size of every position.
