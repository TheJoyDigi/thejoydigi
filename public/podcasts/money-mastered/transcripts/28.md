**Alex:** Welcome back to Money, Mastered. Last time we graded Bitcoin against the money scorecard and it scored highest on one quality above all, scarcity. Today we open the hood. No bank runs Bitcoin, no company runs it, and yet it has run for over fifteen years without anyone successfully cheating it. How?

**Jordan:** That is exactly my question. I can accept that the supply is capped at twenty one million. But who keeps the books? With my bank, the bank writes down what I sent and what I received. With Bitcoin, who is holding the pen?

**Alex:** Everyone is, and that is the whole trick. Bitcoin is, at its heart, one shared record book, called the blockchain, and thousands of computers around the world each keep a complete, identical copy of it. When money moves, the movement is written into every copy at once.

**Jordan:** So instead of one private ledger inside one bank, there is one public ledger that anyone can read and no one owns.

**Alex:** Exactly. And because anyone can download the software and check the record themselves, you never have to trust a single company to tell you the truth about who owns what. You can verify it. That idea, do not trust, verify, is the design principle underneath everything we cover today.

**Jordan:** Okay, start at the very beginning. If I want to send some bitcoin to a friend, what actually happens?

**Alex:** You start with a wallet, which is a slightly misleading name. A wallet does not hold coins the way a leather wallet holds cash. It holds keys. Think of a key pair like an address and a signature. Your public address is like an account number you can safely share, so people can send bitcoin to you. Your private key is like a signature only you can make, and it proves a payment really came from you.

**Jordan:** So the coins themselves live in the shared record book, and my key is what lets me move the ones assigned to my address.

**Alex:** Perfectly put. When you send, your wallet creates a message that says, in effect, I move this amount from my address to my friend's address, and it stamps that message with your private signature. Then it broadcasts the message to the network, where computers check two things. Does the signature match, and does the record show you actually have that bitcoin to spend?

**Jordan:** And if both checks pass, the payment waits to be written into the book. Who does the writing, and how often?

**Alex:** Payments are gathered into batches called blocks, and a new block is added to the chain roughly every ten minutes. So the blockchain is exactly what it sounds like, a chain of blocks, each one a page of recent transactions, locked to the page before it. That locking is where mining comes in, and mining is the most misunderstood word in this whole subject.

**Jordan:** When I picture mining I picture people digging. I assume the computers are doing something more useful than digging, so tell me what mining really is.

**Alex:** Miners are the bookkeepers who compete to write the next page, and the competition is a guessing game. Each miner's computer takes the waiting transactions, bundles them into a block, and then guesses numbers over and over, billions and billions of times, looking for one special result that the network's rules demand. The first miner to find a valid result wins the right to add that block, and everyone else checks the work in a split second.

**Jordan:** Guessing sounds wasteful at first. Why build money on a guessing game?

**Alex:** Because guessing is expensive, and that expense is the security. Finding the answer takes enormous computing power and real electricity, but checking the answer is nearly free. So writing an honest page costs a lot, faking a page costs even more, and verifying the truth costs almost nothing. Economists would call that asymmetry, hard to produce, easy to verify, and it is the same quality that made gold money for thousands of years. Gold was hard to dig up and easy to test.

**Jordan:** So mining is really a contest where the entry fee is electricity, and the prize is writing the next page honestly. What is the prize, exactly?

**Alex:** Two things. The miner who wins a block collects the transaction fees from everyone whose payment was included, and the protocol itself pays a reward of brand new bitcoin, created right there in that block. That reward is the only way new bitcoin enters the world. There is no printing press and no committee. New supply arrives on a schedule written into the rules, paid to the people doing the work of securing the network.

**Jordan:** Which brings us to the halving, a word I have heard for years without really understanding it. Walk me through it slowly.

**Alex:** Gladly. When Bitcoin launched in two thousand and nine, the reward was fifty bitcoin per block. The rules say that after every two hundred and ten thousand blocks, which works out to roughly once every four years, the reward is cut in half. Fifty became twenty five, then twelve and a half, then six and a quarter, then three and an eighth. The most recent halving was in April of two thousand and twenty four, and the next one is expected around two thousand and twenty eight.

**Jordan:** And this staircase of halvings is how we eventually land exactly on twenty one million and not a coin more.

**Alex:** Right, and notice the shape of it. Most of Bitcoin's supply arrived early, when few people cared, and each four year step makes the new supply smaller and harder to earn. Miners must spend roughly the same effort for half the reward, so the cost of producing each new bitcoin tends to rise over time. It is the opposite of the dollar system from Part One, where expanding supply is a decision officials can make in an afternoon. Here, tightening supply is automatic, announced decades in advance, and no emergency meeting can change it.

**Jordan:** Let me test the security claim now, because a skeptic in my head is asking a fair question. If the record is just software, what stops a bad actor from rewriting a page, say erasing a payment they made, so they can spend the same bitcoin twice?

**Alex:** That double spend problem is the exact problem Bitcoin was built to solve, and the chain structure is the answer. Every block contains a fingerprint of the block before it. Change even one old transaction and that block's fingerprint changes, which breaks the fingerprint in the next block, and the next, all the way to today. To make the network accept your rewritten history, you would have to redo all that guessing work for every block since the change, faster than the entire honest network adds new ones.

**Jordan:** So an attacker does not just need a big computer. They would need more computing power than everyone else combined.

**Alex:** That is the famous fifty one percent idea. Control more than half of the network's mining power and, in theory, you could rewrite recent history. In practice, for a network as large as Bitcoin, gathering that much hardware and electricity would cost a fortune, the attack would be visible to everyone, and success would crash the value of the very asset you were trying to steal. The incentives point the other way. The same power earns more, honestly and quietly, by mining by the rules and collecting the reward.

**Jordan:** And this is also why people wait for confirmations before treating a big payment as final.

**Alex:** Yes. One confirmation means your payment is in the latest block. Each additional block buried on top of it makes rewriting it exponentially harder, because the attacker must redo more and more work. Small payments settle fine with a confirmation or two. Very large payments often wait for several, sometimes six, which takes about an hour. Compare that with a bank transfer that can look settled for days and still be reversed. Bitcoin settlement is slower by the minute and far more final by the hour.

**Jordan:** One more piece keeps nagging me. If miners are this powerful, what stops them from voting to raise the reward, or lifting the cap we talked about last chapter?

**Alex:** The other half of the network, the nodes. Miners propose blocks, but thousands of independent node operators, including ordinary people running the software at home, check every block against the full rulebook. A block that pays the miner too much, or creates a twenty one millionth and first coin, is simply rejected, no matter how much guessing power produced it. Mining power orders the pages. It does not get to rewrite the rules the pages must follow. That separation is the real security model, competition to write, universal power to refuse.

**Jordan:** There is also a dial I have heard about, difficulty. If more miners join, blocks would come faster and the schedule would break, unless something adjusts.

**Alex:** Something does, automatically. About every two weeks, the network looks at how fast the last two thousand and sixteen blocks actually arrived and retunes the guessing puzzle. More competition makes the puzzle harder, less competition makes it easier, and blocks keep landing near ten minutes apart through booms, bans, and crashes. No official sets that dial. The system measures itself and corrects itself, which is why the halving dates can be estimated years ahead but never promised to the day.

**Jordan:** Let me try the recap, because this chapter is really one machine with four moving parts. One, a shared public record book that thousands of computers copy and anyone can verify. Two, keys that work like an address and a signature, so only the owner can move coins. Three, mining, a costly guessing contest that decides who writes the next page, pays the winner in fees and new bitcoin, and makes cheating more expensive than honesty. And four, the halving, which cuts that new bitcoin reward in half about every four years, marching supply toward the twenty one million cap on a schedule nobody can speed up.

**Alex:** That is a clean recap, and I would add only the sentence that ties it together. Bitcoin replaces a trusted bookkeeper with a contest to write the books, open books everyone can audit, and rules that even the winners cannot change. Trust does not disappear. It moves, from institutions you must believe, to math and incentives you can check.

**Jordan:** If I had to carry one idea out of this chapter, it would be that.

**Alex:** Then let us state the key takeaway plainly. Bitcoin works because honesty is the most profitable strategy. Mining makes rewriting history ruinously expensive, nodes make rule breaking impossible to sneak through, and the halving makes new supply shrink on schedule, so the system stays secure and scarce without anyone in charge.

**Jordan:** Secure and scarce without anyone in charge. That also raises the obvious next question. Bitcoin was designed to be money and mostly money. What happens when someone asks, can this blockchain idea run programs too, not just payments?

**Alex:** That question built the second biggest network in crypto.
