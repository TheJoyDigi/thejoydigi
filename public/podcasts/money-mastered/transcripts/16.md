**Alex:** Welcome back to Money, Mastered. I want to start with a puzzle. Two lemonade stands sit on the same street. One is for sale for ten thousand dollars, and the other is for sale for one hundred thousand dollars. Which one is the better deal?

**Jordan:** I honestly have no idea, because you have not told me anything about what either stand actually earns. The expensive one could be a bargain, and the cheap one could be a trap.

**Alex:** That instinct is the entire chapter. In chapter fifteen we spread our money across different kinds of assets so no single bet could sink us. Today we answer the question that comes before you buy any single business. What is a company actually worth, and how is that different from the price on the screen?

**Jordan:** That distinction trips me up. When I open an investing app, the price is right there, big and green or red. It feels like the answer. Why is it not?

**Alex:** Because price is simply what the last buyer and seller agreed on in one moment. Value is what the business itself can deliver to its owners over its whole life, in cash. Think back to chapter seven. A share is a slice of a real business, with real customers, real costs, and real profits. The screen price can swing ten percent in a week while the business underneath barely changes at all. Our job today is to learn how to judge the business, not just stare at the quote.

**Jordan:** So where do we start judging? What is the first number you look at?

**Alex:** Earnings. Earnings are what is left after a company pays for everything, its materials, its workers, its rent, its taxes, and its interest. If a company sells one hundred million dollars of products and keeps ten million dollars after all costs, it earned ten million dollars. Investors often shrink that down to earnings per share, which is just the total profit divided by the number of shares. If that same company has ten million shares, it earned one dollar per share. That one number is the seed from which almost every valuation grows.

**Jordan:** Why per share? Why not just use the whole company profit?

**Alex:** Because you do not buy the whole company. You buy slices. Earnings per share tells you how much profit stands behind each slice you own. Two companies can both earn one hundred million dollars, but if one has ten million shares and the other has one hundred million shares, your slice of the second company claims ten times less profit. Always ask what you get per share, because that is what you are actually paying for.

**Jordan:** All right, so I know the earnings per share. How does that turn into a price people are willing to pay?

**Alex:** Through the most famous shortcut in investing, the price to earnings ratio. Take the share price and divide it by the earnings per share. If a share costs two hundred dollars and the company earns ten dollars per share each year, the price to earnings ratio is twenty. A simple way to hear that number is this. You are paying twenty dollars for every one dollar of yearly profit your slice currently produces. Or flip it around. At today’s earnings, it would take twenty years of profits to add up to the price you paid.

**Jordan:** So is a ratio of twenty good or bad? Is there a magic number where a stock becomes cheap?

**Alex:** There is no magic number, and anyone who promises one is selling you a shortcut that will hurt you. A ratio only makes sense in comparison. Compare it with the company’s own history, with similar companies, and most importantly with how fast the earnings are growing. A company growing its profits twenty five percent a year can deserve a much higher ratio than a company whose profits are shrinking, because next year’s earnings, and the year after, will be far larger. A high ratio on a fast grower can be cheaper in the long run than a low ratio on a business that is slowly melting away.

**Jordan:** That melting idea is important. A low price to earnings ratio can look like a bargain and actually be a warning.

**Alex:** Exactly, and investors even have a name for it, a value trap. Imagine a newspaper printing company, or a mall retailer losing customers every year. Its ratio might be five, which looks dirt cheap. But if earnings fall by half over the next few years, you paid five times earnings that no longer exist. Cheap has to mean cheap relative to the future, not cheap relative to a past that is disappearing. That is why valuation always faces forward. You are buying tomorrow’s cash, not yesterday’s headlines.

**Jordan:** You keep saying cash, but we have been talking about earnings. Are they the same thing?

**Alex:** They are close cousins, not twins, and the difference matters. Earnings are an accounting measure, and accounting allows estimates and timing choices. Cash is harder to fake. Cash flow is the actual money moving into and out of the bank account. A company can report rising earnings while its customers pay late and its cash drains away. Healthy businesses, over time, turn most of their earnings into real cash. When you value a company, ask whether the profit shows up as cash you could, in principle, put in your pocket, because owners can only spend cash, never accounting entries.

**Jordan:** That leads to the deeper method you mentioned before we started recording, the discounted cash flow idea. That one sounds intimidating.

**Alex:** The name is intimidating, but the intuition is something you already live by. A dollar today is worth more than a dollar promised ten years from now. You would rather have one hundred dollars now than one hundred dollars in a decade, because today’s money can be invested, and because the future promise carries risk. A discounted cash flow valuation simply adds up all the cash a business is expected to produce in the future, then shrinks each future amount back to what it is worth today. Add those shrunken amounts together, and you get an estimate of what the whole business is worth right now.

**Jordan:** What decides how much we shrink those future dollars?

**Alex:** The discount rate, and here the whole course clicks together. Remember chapter four, where interest rates are the price of money, and chapter ten, where safer bonds offer lower yields and riskier borrowers must pay more. The discount rate blends that safe rate with extra compensation for risk. When interest rates rise, future cash gets shrunk harder, so growth companies, whose cash sits far in the future, often fall in price even when nothing about their business changed. That is exactly what markets lived through when rates climbed quickly in twenty twenty-two. Rates are gravity for valuations. Higher gravity pulls every price down.

**Jordan:** So valuation is not a calculator that spits out one true number. It sounds more like a range built on honest guesses about growth, cash, and risk.

**Alex:** That is the mature view, and it protects you. Good investors build a range, then demand a margin of safety, a gap between the price they pay and the value they estimate, so that being a little wrong does not become a disaster. They also check the story against simple ratios, they compare similar businesses, and they ask one grounding question. If I owned this whole business, quietly, with no stock market to sell to, would I be happy with the cash it hands me each year for the price I paid? If the answer needs a greater fool to buy it from you later, it is speculation, not valuation.

**Jordan:** Before we wrap up, can we run one complete example from start to finish, with real arithmetic, so I can feel the steps in order?

**Alex:** Let us do it with a simple, imaginary coffee chain. Suppose it earns four dollars per share this year, and its shares trade at eighty dollars. The price to earnings ratio is eighty divided by four, which is twenty. Now suppose two futures are possible. In the first, the chain opens careful new stores and grows earnings eight percent a year for a decade, turning four dollars into more than eight dollars per share. In the second, competition squeezes it and earnings stay flat forever. Same starting ratio, two completely different values. The first business can be a fair buy at twenty times earnings, and the second can be expensive at the very same price.

**Jordan:** And the discounting step would separate them even more clearly, because the growing chain delivers far more cash in those later years, even after we shrink it back to today.

**Alex:** Precisely. And notice what we never needed. We never needed a prediction about next week’s share price, a headline, or a stranger’s opinion online. We needed earnings, a growth judgment, a cash check, and patience. That toolkit fits in your pocket, and it works on a coffee chain, a software company, or the biggest business in the index from chapter nine.

**Jordan:** Let me try to recap the whole ladder, and you tell me where I wobble. First, price is not value. Price is a moment, and value is the cash a business can deliver over its life. Second, earnings per share tell me what stands behind my slice, and the price to earnings ratio tells me how many dollars I pay for each dollar of that profit. Third, a ratio means nothing alone. It needs growth, comparison, and a warning about value traps. Fourth, cash matters more than accounting profit. And fifth, the deepest method adds up future cash and discounts it back to today, with interest rates acting like gravity.

**Alex:** That is a clean recap, and I would add only one layer on top. Quality changes what a fair price is. A business with loyal customers, low costs, and room to grow into new markets can safely be worth more, because its future cash is more dependable. Two companies with identical earnings today can deserve very different prices if one defends its profits easily and the other fights for every sale. Valuation is never just arithmetic. It is arithmetic wrapped around a judgment about how durable the business really is.

Pay for future cash with a margin of safety, and never confuse a falling price with a rising value or a low ratio with a genuine bargain.
