**Alex:** Welcome back to Money, Mastered. I am Alex, and today we turn a good idea into a complete trade, because an idea by itself is not a trade at all.

**Jordan:** And I am Jordan. Last chapter you taught us how much to risk and how to size a position, and I thought the hard part was over. Then I realized I still did not know when to get in, when to get out, or where to put the line that says I was wrong.

**Alex:** That is where most trades fall apart. A trader finds a promising chart, jumps in because price is moving, and only thinks about the exit after the trade goes against them. By then emotion is driving, and emotion is a terrible planner.

**Jordan:** So today is about planning the whole trade before it starts.

**Alex:** Yes. Every sound trade has three prices decided in advance. The entry, where you get in. The stop loss, where you get out if the idea is wrong. And the target, where you take profit if the idea works. Together they are the structure of the trade. If one is missing, you do not have a trade yet. You have a hope.

**Jordan:** Let me start with the entry, because that is the part beginners obsess over. Is there a perfect entry?

**Alex:** There is no perfect entry, and chasing one will cost you. What there is, is a planned entry that fits your reason for the trade. Why should this price go up? Maybe a strong company pulled back to a level where buyers stepped in before. Maybe a stock is breaking out on heavy volume, which we learned to check in chapter twenty-one. The entry is the moment the market gives you evidence that your reason is starting to work.

**Jordan:** So the entry is a trigger, not a guess.

**Alex:** Exactly. Think of two common triggers. The first is the breakout entry. A stock has been held down by resistance, that ceiling from chapter twenty. When price pushes through it and holds, ideally on volume well above average, a breakout trader buys, betting the old ceiling becomes a new floor. The second is the pullback entry. A stock in an uptrend, making higher highs and higher lows, dips back toward support. A pullback trader waits for that dip to slow and turn, then buys closer to support. Neither is better in the abstract. They are different trades with different risks.

**Jordan:** What is the trade-off between them?

**Alex:** The breakout entry gives you confirmation. Price has already proven it can move. But you pay a higher price, and your distance to a sensible stop is often wider, which, from chapter twenty-two, means a smaller position for the same dollar risk. The pullback entry gives you a better price and a closer stop, so you can size a little larger for the same risk. But you have less confirmation, and sometimes that dip is the start of a real decline. That is why the stop matters more than the entry. A decent entry with a disciplined exit beats a brilliant entry with no exit every time.

**Jordan:** That flips how most people think. They spend ninety percent of their energy on getting in and almost none on getting out.

**Alex:** Professionals reverse it. Now, the stop loss. A stop loss is an order, or a firm decision, that closes your trade when price reaches a level that proves your idea wrong. Notice the words proves wrong. A stop is not a random number of dollars you are willing to lose. It is placed at the point where the story you bought is no longer true.

**Jordan:** Give me a concrete example.

**Alex:** Say you buy at fifty dollars because a stock broke above resistance at forty-eight dollars and held. Your idea is that forty-eight dollars now acts as support. If price falls back below it and stays there, the breakout failed. So your stop might sit at forty-seven dollars, just under that level, giving it room for noise. Your risk is fifty minus forty-seven, three dollars a share. From chapter twenty-two, if you risk five hundred dollars, five hundred divided by three tells you to buy about one hundred sixty-six shares. The stop came first, and size followed.

**Jordan:** And a bad stop would be what, picking a round number because it feels like enough?

**Alex:** Yes, or placing it by a fixed percentage with no connection to the chart. A five percent stop on a calm utility stock might never be touched by normal noise. The same five percent stop on a volatile Bitcoin trade might get hit on an ordinary afternoon, even when the idea is still fine. The stop belongs where the idea breaks, and then size adjusts so dollar risk stays the same. Distance sets size. Never the other way around.

**Jordan:** You said a stop can be an order or a firm decision. Are those different tools?

**Alex:** They are, and each has a cost. A stop market order, when price touches your stop, becomes a market order to sell at the next available price. It almost always gets you out, but the price can be worse than your stop in a fast fall. That difference is called slippage. A stop limit order adds a floor: sell at my stop, but not below my limit. That protects you from a terrible fill, but if price gaps through your limit, the order never fills, and you are still holding a falling trade.

**Jordan:** Gaps sound like the hidden trap here.

**Alex:** They are the limit of every stop. A stock can close at sixty dollars and open at fifty-two dollars after bad earnings news. No stop sells you out at fifty-eight dollars when there was no trading in between. A stop controls your exit in a moving market. It does not guarantee a price in a jumping market. That is one more reason size matters. If a gap can hurt more than planned, the position was too big. Earnings reports and thinly traded assets gap the most, and many traders refuse to hold a full position through earnings.

**Jordan:** What about a mental stop, where I just watch the price and promise myself I will sell?

**Alex:** For a beginner, a mental stop is a promise you break at the worst moment. When price reaches the line, hope whispers that it might bounce, and the line quietly moves. If you watch the market all day and have a proven record of obeying your own lines, a mental stop can work. Until then, put the order in the market. Let your calm morning self protect your panicked afternoon self.

**Jordan:** Okay, that covers getting out when I am wrong. What about getting out when I am right? That sounds easy, but I suspect it is not.

**Alex:** It is surprisingly hard, because greed replaces fear. You need an exit plan for winners too, and there are three honest tools. The first is a target. Before you enter, look at where price could reasonably go, maybe the next resistance level, and measure it against your risk. In our example, you risked three dollars a share, from fifty down to forty-seven. If the next resistance sits at fifty-nine dollars, your possible reward is nine dollars a share. Nine divided by three is a reward to risk of three to one. That is a trade worth taking. If the target were only fifty-three dollars, reward would equal risk, and the same entry would be a poor trade. The target is not a prediction. It is a test you apply before you commit a dollar.

**Jordan:** So I can reject a trade just because the target is too close, even if I like the idea.

**Alex:** You should. Good traders pass on good ideas with bad structure all the time. The second tool is the trailing stop. As price rises in your favor, you move your stop up behind it, locking in gain while giving the trend room to breathe. If you bought at fifty dollars and price reaches fifty-eight dollars, you might raise the stop to fifty-three dollars. Now the worst case is a profit, and if the trend keeps running, you stay in. The third tool is the time stop. Sometimes a trade does nothing wrong, but does nothing at all. Your money sits frozen for weeks. A time stop says, if this has not started working within ten trading days, I will close it and free the capital. Dead money is a real cost, even when the loss is zero.

**Jordan:** And can I combine them, like selling part at the target and trailing the rest?

**Alex:** Many traders do exactly that. Sell a portion at the first target to bank a gain, then trail a stop on what remains and let a big winner run. There is no single correct recipe. What matters is that the recipe is written before the trade begins, not invented while you watch price flicker.

**Jordan:** Let me put the whole structure together. Before I trade, I write down four things. One, my reason, in one sentence. Two, my entry trigger and price. Three, my stop, where the idea is proven wrong. Four, my target, and I check that reward is at least twice the risk. Then I use the distance from entry to stop to set size, risking about one percent of my account. And I place the stop order when I enter, not later.

**Alex:** That is a complete trade, and notice what you never said. You never said you needed to be sure. Out of ten trades built like that, you might lose six and still come out ahead, because your average winner is two or three times your average loser and every loss is small and planned. That is the math from chapter twenty-two, now with a structure that makes it real.

**Jordan:** One more trap I want to check. What about moving a stop further away because I still believe in the idea?

**Alex:** Never widen a stop on an open trade. Moving it away increases risk beyond the plan, usually for an emotional reason, not a new fact. You may move a stop closer as a trade works, to protect profit. You may close a trade and later build a new trade if the idea returns. But the stop you set at entry is a contract with yourself. Breaking it once teaches you that your contracts are optional, and that is the most expensive lesson in trading.

**Jordan:** And averaging down, buying more as it falls toward my stop?

**Alex:** That is widening risk in disguise. Adding to a loser breaks the size you calculated and turns one small planned loss into a large unplanned one. Adding to a winner, after price has proven you right, is a different conversation, and even that belongs in a written plan. For now: plan the trade, size it from the stop, and take the loss when the stop says the idea is wrong.

**Alex:** So here is the one key takeaway from this chapter. Decide your entry, your stop, and your target before you risk a single dollar, place the stop where the idea is proven wrong, and let that distance set your size. A trade without all three prices is not a trade. It is a hope with money attached.
