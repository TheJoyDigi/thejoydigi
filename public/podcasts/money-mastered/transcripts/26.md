**Jordan:** Why does it have to be written down? If I know my rules, is that not enough?

**Alex:** Knowing your rules at your desk on a calm morning and remembering them in the middle of a losing trade are two completely different things. Chapter Twenty Four taught us that pressure changes the person making the decision. Fear and greed do not argue with your plan. They simply make you forget you ever had one. A written plan is a decision you made while you were calm, saved for the moment when you are not. Pilots use checklists for the same reason. It is not that pilots cannot fly. It is that nobody thinks clearly in an emergency, so the clear thinking gets done in advance.

**Jordan:** So the plan is me, on my best day, leaving instructions for me on my worst day.

Its job is to make your behavior repeatable, so that the evidence from Chapter Twenty Five, your backtest and your journal, actually means something. If you trade differently every day, there is nothing to test and nothing to improve. You just have weather. The plan turns weather into climate, something you can measure.

**Jordan:** Where do we start? What is the first section?

**Alex:** The first section is the smallest and the most skipped. It answers the question, who am I as a trader, and what am I not? You write down the markets you trade, and only those markets. You write down the hours you can actually watch them, honestly, around your real life and your real job. And you write down your time frame, meaning whether you trade moves that last minutes, days, or weeks. A person with thirty minutes a day cannot run a strategy that needs six hours of screen time, and a plan built for the life of another person is a plan that gets abandoned by Friday.

**Jordan:** So section one is fit. The plan has to fit my life before it can fit the market.

**Alex:** Beautifully said. Section two is your setup, and here the rule is focus. Pick one setup to start, two at the very most. A setup is the specific pattern you trade, described with the precision Chapter Twenty Five demanded, so clearly that a stranger could spot it without you in the room. Use the language you already have. From Chapter Twenty, what does the trend look like, and where are support and resistance? From Chapter Twenty One, what should volume be doing to confirm the move has conviction behind it? Write the conditions as a short checklist, all of which must be true before a trade even becomes a candidate.

**Jordan:** One setup, written as a checklist. What comes next, getting in and getting out?

**Alex:** Section three is trade structure, and you already learned it in Chapter Twenty Three. For your setup, you write down exactly what triggers your entry, where your stop loss goes, the place where the idea is proven wrong, and how you take profit, whether that is a target, a trailing rule, or a time limit. And you write the sentence that does the heavy lifting on your worst days. If the stop is hit, I exit, no discussion, no moving it, no hoping. The exit is decided before the entry, every single time, because the moment you are in a trade is the worst moment to design its exit.

**Jordan:** That covers one trade. But the chapters kept saying survival is bigger than any one trade.

**Alex:** Which brings us to section four, and if you only write one section perfectly, make it this one. These are your risk rules, the math of survival from Chapter Twenty Two, turned into hard numbers. First, your risk per trade, for example one percent of your account on any single idea, so a string of losses shrinks you slowly instead of ending you. Second, your daily loss limit. If you lose, say, three percent in one day, you stop for the day, full stop, because that is the circuit breaker from Chapter Twenty Four that keeps one bad morning from becoming a ruined month. Third, your maximum number of open positions, so five separate trades cannot quietly become one giant bet on the same idea. And fourth, your drawdown rule. If your account falls by a set amount from its high point, you cut your size in half or stop entirely and review, while you are still in the game.

**Jordan:** Let me test my understanding with real numbers. Say my account is ten thousand dollars. One percent risk means I can lose one hundred dollars on a trade. **Jordan:** What about the parts of trading that are not the trade itself? My routine feels messy.

**Alex:** Section five is your routine, and it is more powerful than it sounds. You write down what you do before the market, during it, and after it. Before, you review your plan, check your journal notes from the last session, and mark the levels that matter today, so you are never improvising your preparation. During, you take only checklist trades, and you journal each one as it happens, using the format from Chapter Twenty Five. After, you record outcomes, grade your execution rather than your results, and close the book for the day. Same steps, every session. Boring is not a flaw in a routine. Boring is the entire point, because excitement is what the market sells you at the worst possible price.

**Jordan:** And the psychology rules? Chapter Twenty Four feels like it needs its own section.

**Alex:** It gets one. Section six is your personal guardrails, and this is where your journal earns its keep, because these rules should come from your own evidence, not from a book. Maybe your journal shows you trade terribly after a loss, so your plan says, after any losing trade, stand up, walk away, and take no new trade for thirty minutes. Maybe you chase moves you missed, so your plan says, a missed trade is gone forever, and the next setup is the only one that exists. Write two or three of these, aimed at the mistakes you actually make. A plan that guards against the weaknesses of another trader while ignoring yours is a costume, not a rulebook.

**Jordan:** Now the question I know is coming. What happens when the plan is not working? When am I allowed to change it?

**Alex:** Section seven, and this section is what separates a plan from a mood with formatting. You are allowed to change your plan, but only under rules. Changes happen at a scheduled review, say once a month, never in the middle of a losing streak, and never during market hours. Every change must cite evidence, meaning backtest results or a pattern across at least twenty or thirty journaled trades, not one painful afternoon. And you change one thing at a time, so you know what caused what. Chapter Twenty Five gave you the laboratory, and this section is the rule that you do not rebuild the laboratory while the experiment is running.

**Jordan:** So the plan is a living document, but it changes slowly, on evidence, at appointed times. It does not change at ten in the morning because I am frightened.
