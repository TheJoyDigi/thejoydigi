**Alex:** Welcome back to Money, Mastered. Last time we opened the hood on Bitcoin and saw a shared record book that no one owns and everyone can verify. Today we ask the question that record book raised. What if the book could do more than track payments? What if it could carry agreements that run themselves?

**Jordan:** That is the phrase I keep hearing, programmable money. It sounds exciting and a little frightening at the same time. If Bitcoin is digital cash, what exactly is Ethereum trying to be?

**Alex:** The cleanest answer is this. Bitcoin is a calculator built for one job, moving value from one address to another, and doing that one job with enormous security. Ethereum is a shared computer built for many jobs. Anyone can write a small program, put it on that computer, and have it run exactly as written, in public, without asking a company for permission.

**Jordan:** A shared computer that belongs to no one. Who proposed that, and when?

**Alex:** A young programmer named Vitalik Buterin described it in two thousand and thirteen, when he was nineteen years old, and the network launched in two thousand and fifteen. His frustration was simple. Bitcoin's language was deliberately limited, for safety, so building new agreements on it was painful. He asked why the blockchain itself should not include a general purpose language.

**Jordan:** So the invention is not really a new coin. The invention is putting programs inside the record book.

**Alex:** Exactly, and those programs are called smart contracts. The name misleads people in two ways. They are not especially smart, and they are not legal contracts in the courtroom sense. A smart contract is simply code stored on the blockchain that runs automatically when its conditions are met, and whose result everyone on the network accepts.

**Jordan:** Give me the simplest possible example, in ordinary life, before we add any finance.

**Alex:** Think of a vending machine. You put in the right amount of money, you press a button, and the machine must give you the snack. No cashier decides whether you deserve it, and no manager can change the price after you paid. A smart contract is a vending machine made of code, except it can hold money, release money, split money, and follow far more detailed rules.

**Jordan:** I like that. The machine does not trust me and I do not trust the machine. We both trust the mechanism we can see.

**Alex:** That sentence could be the motto of this whole chapter. Now scale the vending machine up. Imagine an agreement that pays a traveler automatically if a flight is delayed by more than three hours. Or one that holds a deposit and releases it only when delivery is confirmed. Or a loan that sells some collateral if its value falls too far. Each is a rule that can be written, published, and left to run.

**Jordan:** And where does ether fit in? People talk about Ethereum and ether as if they are the same word.

**Alex:** They are not, and separating them helps. Ethereum is the network, the shared computer. Ether is its currency, the fuel and the money of that computer. Every program you run and every payment you make on Ethereum costs a small fee paid in ether. That fee is called gas, which is a surprisingly honest name. Computation burns fuel, and you pay for the fuel you burn.

**Jordan:** Why charge for computation at all? The internet mostly feels free to use.

**Alex:** Because a free shared computer would be spammed to death in an afternoon. If running a program cost nothing, someone could run a useless program forever and clog the machine for everyone. Gas makes every operation carry a price, so waste hurts the waster first. It also pays the people who run the network and keep it secure.

**Jordan:** Gas prices move though, right? I have heard people complain that a simple transaction once cost fifty dollars in fees.

**Alex:** They do move, and for a familiar reason from earlier chapters, supply and demand in a crowded room. Block space is limited, so when many people want the shared computer at the same moment, they bid against each other for room, and the price of gas rises. In quiet times a simple transfer can cost well under a dollar. In a frenzy it can cost many dollars. That fee problem is real, and it is one of the honest limits we will return to before the end of the chapter.

**Jordan:** Before the limits, let me understand what people actually built with this. What lives on this shared computer?

**Alex:** The programs are often called decentralized applications, and they cluster into a few families. The biggest is decentralized finance, which tries to rebuild familiar financial jobs with code instead of companies. One program lets people swap one token for another directly, using a pool of funds and a pricing formula. Another lets people lend and borrow, where the smart contract holds the collateral and enforces the rules.

**Jordan:** Wait, slow down on the lending one, because that sounds like a bank rebuilt in code. If I borrow from a program, who decides if I am good for it?

**Alex:** Nobody judges your character or your credit history, and that forces a different design. Because the program cannot chase you if you disappear, almost all of this lending is over collateralized. To borrow one thousand dollars of value, you might first lock up fifteen hundred dollars of value inside the contract. If the collateral falls too far, the contract sells it automatically. It is lending with trust replaced by a margin call that never sleeps.

**Jordan:** That is both elegant and harsh. No banker to plead with, but also no banker who can play favorites or lend to friends on a handshake.

**Alex:** Both halves of that sentence are true. A second family is tokens themselves. A smart contract can create a new asset on Ethereum following a shared standard, so every wallet can read it the same way. That is how thousands of tokens live on one network, from tokens meant to track the dollar to unique tokens that certify one specific digital item is the original.

**Jordan:** The dollar tracking ones matter, I think. If a token aims to stay at one dollar and people trust it, then the shared computer suddenly has dollars moving on it at all hours.

**Alex:** Those are stablecoins, and in a later chapter on risk we will examine how that promise of one dollar is kept, and how it can break. For today, notice the pattern. Once money becomes programmable, people program everything money touches, payments, loans, trades, insurance, even organizations.

**Jordan:** Organizations? That sounds like a stretch. How do you program a company?

**Alex:** People tried that with a decentralized autonomous organization. Members hold tokens that carry votes, proposals are published in the open, votes are counted by code, and the treasury can only move when a vote passes. In two thousand and sixteen an early and famous one raised roughly one hundred and fifty million dollars of value, which made it the perfect test of whether code alone could govern money.

**Jordan:** I sense a turning point in the story. What happened to it?

**Alex:** An attacker found a flaw in its code and used that flaw, exactly as the code allowed, to drain about a third of its funds. The code had done what it was written to do. The humans had written the wrong thing. The community eventually rewrote history to recover the funds, a decision so controversial that the network split in two. That episode taught the field its hardest lesson, worth stating slowly.

**Jordan:** Which is that code is law only until the code is wrong, and then humans discover they were in charge all along.

**Alex:** A smart contract removes the middleman, but it does not remove mistakes, and it adds a new kind of risk. A bank error can often be reversed by a phone call. A smart contract error executes instantly, publicly, and usually irreversibly, and attackers read the code looking for exactly those errors, because the code and the money are both visible to everyone.

**Jordan:** That is the transparency paradox. Everyone can audit the contract, which is good, and everyone can study how to rob it, which is dangerous.

**Alex:** Precisely, and it is why serious projects pay independent firms to audit their code before launch, and why even audited projects still sometimes fail. There is also the oracle problem. A contract can see everything on the chain, but it cannot see the outside world. It does not know a price or whether a flight landed, unless an outside service, called an oracle, feeds that fact in. The moment you trust an oracle, you have reintroduced a trusted middleman through the back door.

**Jordan:** So the system is trustless inside its walls and still dependent on trust at the gates where real world facts enter.

**Alex:** Exactly right. One more big change completes the picture. For its first years Ethereum used mining, the same costly guessing contest as Bitcoin. In September of two thousand and twenty two, in an event called the merge, it switched to proof of stake. Instead of competing with electricity, the people who secure the network lock up ether of their own. Honest work earns rewards, and dishonest work can cause that stake to be destroyed, so security comes from money at risk rather than energy spent.

**Jordan:** And that cut its energy use dramatically, I remember the headlines.

**Alex:** By well over ninety nine percent by most estimates, yes. It also changed the economics of ether. Validators must hold it, and a portion of the ether paid in gas is burned, destroyed forever, so new supply grows slowly and sometimes shrinks. But Ethereum has no fixed cap, so its scarcity story is different from Bitcoin's fixed ceiling of twenty one million.

**Jordan:** That is an important contrast and I do not want to blur it. Bitcoin sells certainty about supply. Ethereum sells usefulness of a shared computer, and its currency is the fuel that computer runs on.

**Alex:** That contrast is the heart of this chapter. Briefly on fees, much activity now happens on companion networks built on top of Ethereum, which bundle many transactions together and post a summary back to the main chain for security. They lower fees a great deal, though they add their own complexity and trust questions.

**Jordan:** Let me try the recap. One, Ethereum is a shared computer where anyone can publish a program. Two, a smart contract is that program, code that holds value and executes its rules automatically, like a vending machine no one can argue with. Three, ether is the fuel, and gas is the price of computation. Four, people have used this to rebuild lending, trading, tokens, and even organizations in code. And five, the risks are new, not absent, bugs execute irreversibly, outside facts need trusted oracles, and crowded demand makes fees spike.

**Alex:** That is a complete recap, and I would add only the sentence that ties it to the rest of our course. In Part One we said money is a technology for moving value through time and space. Smart contracts extend that idea. They are a technology for moving value conditionally, money that waits, watches, and acts when the agreed facts arrive.

**Jordan:** If I had to carry one idea out of this chapter, it would be that conditional part. Money that follows instructions.

**Alex:** Then let us state the key takeaway plainly. Ethereum turns a blockchain from a payment ledger into programmable money. Smart contracts can hold value and execute agreements without a middleman, which removes favoritism and delay, but replaces them with code risk, so the question is never just what a contract promises, it is whether the code actually does only that.

**Jordan:** Promises are cheap. Code that holds money has to be right, not just well intentioned. That makes me wonder about the market built on top of all this, because Bitcoin and Ethereum do not move like calm savings accounts. They boom and they crash in a pattern people swear they have seen before.

**Alex:** They do, and that pattern is our next subject. Next time, in chapter thirty, we study crypto market cycles, how halvings, hype, leverage, and fear have driven this young market through repeated booms and deep drawdowns, and how to recognize where in a cycle you might be standing.

**Jordan:** Crypto cycles, booms and drawdowns on a schedule people try to trade. I will bring the skepticism, especially about anyone who claims the pattern guarantees the future.
