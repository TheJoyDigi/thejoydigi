**Alex:** Welcome back to Money, Mastered. Picture a trader who is completely sure his strategy works, because he remembers three big winners from last spring, and he has quietly forgotten the nine small losers that ate those winners alive.

**Jordan:** That sounds painfully familiar. My memory keeps the highlights and loses the receipts.

**Alex:** That is the problem this chapter solves. Today we are talking about backtesting and journaling, which is one idea in two directions. Backtesting tests your strategy against the past, and journaling tests you against the present, and together they replace opinions with evidence.

**Jordan:** So after entries, exits, stop losses, and psychology, this is where we find out whether any of it actually works?

**Alex:** Exactly. In Chapter Twenty Three you learned to decide your entry, your stop, and your target before you risk a dollar. In Chapter Twenty Four you learned that your mind will try to talk you out of that plan under pressure. This chapter adds the missing piece, which is proof. A plan you have never tested is just a story you tell yourself, and a trade you never recorded is a lesson you paid for and then threw away.

**Jordan:** Start with backtesting. What does it actually mean, in plain language?

**Alex:** A backtest means you take a set of trading rules and apply those exact rules to old price data, trade by trade, as if you had been there at the time, with no knowledge of what happened next. You record every trade the rules would have taken, wins and losses alike, and then you ask a simple question. After all those trades, after costs, would this have made money, and could a real person have survived the rough stretches?

**Jordan:** So it is like a flight simulator for a strategy. A pilot does not test a landing procedure for the first time with passengers on board.

**Alex:** Perfect. A simulator cannot prove a procedure works in every storm, but it can prove that a procedure fails in ordinary weather, and finding that out for free is the whole point. A backtest is much better at killing a bad idea than at guaranteeing a good one, and killing bad ideas cheaply is one of the most valuable skills in trading.

**Jordan:** What do you need before you can run one? My guess is most people start with something vague, like buying when a stock looks strong.

**Alex:** Your guess is right, and vague is the first enemy. A backtest needs rules so precise that a stranger could follow them and get the exact same trades. What counts as the setup? What price triggers the entry? Where is the stop, meaning the place where the idea is proven wrong? Where is the target? How much do you risk per trade, using the sizing math from Chapter Twenty Two? If any answer is, it depends how I feel, you do not have a strategy yet. You have a mood, and you cannot test a mood.

**Jordan:** Step one is rules a stranger could follow. What is step two?

**Alex:** Choosing your test honestly, and this is where most backtests quietly become fiction. You want a large sample, ideally at least one hundred trades, because ten trades prove almost nothing. Ten coin flips can easily land on seven heads, and that does not mean the coin is broken. You also want different kinds of markets, because a rule that only worked in a rising market, in the language of Chapter Seventeen, may simply be a bull market wearing a costume. Test through calm periods and stormy ones, or you have only learned how your strategy behaves on sunny days.

**Jordan:** And I am guessing you have to include the costs from Chapter Nineteen?

**Alex:** You have named the detail that turns many winning backtests into losing real accounts. Every test must charge real costs, meaning commissions where they apply, the spread between the buying and selling price, and slippage, which is the difference between the price you expected and the price you actually got. A strategy that trades very often can look brilliant before costs and bleed to death after them, one small cut at a time. If your edge is smaller than your costs, you do not have an edge. You have a subscription service for your broker.

**Jordan:** What are the big ways people fool themselves in a backtest?

**Alex:** There are three classic traps. The first is look ahead bias. Your test accidentally uses information you could not have known at the moment of the trade, like buying at the lowest price of the day after seeing how the whole day turned out. In real time you never see the future first, so that test is testing a time machine, not a strategy.

**Jordan:** No time machine on offer, sadly. What is the second trap?

**Alex:** Survivorship bias. If you test only on companies that exist today, you leave out all the companies that failed and disappeared, and of course the survivors look better than the real world did. It is like judging the safety of old airplanes by only inspecting the ones that made it back. The missing ones are the data.

**Jordan:** That one is sneaky, because the data looks clean. And the third?

**Alex:** The most seductive of all, called overfitting. You test a rule and it loses, so you adjust it a little. Maybe the entry needs more confirmation, maybe the stop should be wider, and you keep adjusting until the test suddenly looks beautiful. What you have done is memorize the past instead of learning from it. You built a key that opens one specific old lock and nothing else. Here is a simple check. A real pattern usually survives small changes to the rules. If changing one number a little destroys the whole result, you probably never had an edge. You had a coincidence, carefully polished.

**Jordan:** So how do you protect against polishing a coincidence?

**Alex:** You split your history into two parts. You build and adjust your rules using the first part, called the in sample period. Then, once the rules are completely frozen, you test them once on the second part, the out of sample period, which the rules have never seen. If the strategy still works on data it was never tuned on, you might have something real. If it falls apart, the first result was probably memorized, and you just saved yourself from paying real money to learn that. And the hard rule is this. After the out of sample test, you do not keep tweaking and retesting, because every peek turns fresh data into tuning data. The test only stays honest if you respect it.

**Jordan:** That takes patience. When a backtest is done honestly, how do you read the result? Is it about the winning percentage?

**Alex:** Winning percentage is what beginners stare at, and it matters least on its own. What matters is expectancy, the average result of one trade after many trades. Here is a worked example. Imagine a test produced one hundred trades. Fifty five were winners and forty five were losers. The average winner made three hundred dollars and the average loser lost two hundred dollars. The winners brought in sixteen thousand five hundred dollars, the losers took away nine thousand dollars, and the strategy kept seven thousand five hundred dollars before costs, or seventy five dollars per trade on average. That seventy five dollars is the expectancy, and positive expectancy, repeated many times with controlled risk, is the whole game.

**Jordan:** Let me check that. Winning a little more than half the time is not what saved it. The average win was bigger than the average loss, which is the reward to risk idea from Chapter Twenty Three showing up in the results.

**Alex:** Perfectly said. A strategy can lose more often than it wins and still make money, if the winners are much larger than the losers. And alongside expectancy, look at the worst stretch, the maximum drawdown from Chapter Fourteen, the deepest fall from a high point to a low point during the test. Ask honestly, could I have kept following this plan while it fell that far? If the answer is no, the strategy does not fit you, no matter how good the final number looks, because the trader who quits in the middle gets the drawdown without the recovery.

**Jordan:** So a backtest tells me about the strategy. Where does journaling come in?

**Alex:** Journaling is the forward version of the same honesty, and it matters even more, because a backtest tests the strategy, but a journal tests the trader. From your very next trade, in a practice account or a real one, you write down the setup, why you took it, where your stop and target are, how much you risked, and honestly, what you were feeling. After the trade closes, you record what happened, and most importantly, whether you followed your own plan.

**Jordan:** Why write down how I was feeling? That sounds like a diary, not a trading tool.

**Alex:** Because of everything Chapter Twenty Four taught us. Your feelings are data about your future mistakes. If your journal shows that your worst trades all happened after a loss, when you were trying to win money back quickly, that is revenge trading, caught in your own handwriting. If your worst trades share a setup you keep taking out of boredom, the journal shows you exactly where your money leaks. Memory will never show you this, because memory edits. The journal does not edit. It keeps the receipts.

**Jordan:** How should I grade a trade in the journal? By whether it made money?

**Alex:** No, and this might be the most important sentence in the chapter. Grade the execution, not the outcome. A trade that followed every rule and lost a small, planned amount is a good trade. A trade that broke every rule and got lucky is a bad trade, because luck you reward becomes a habit you pay for later. Give each trade a process grade. Did I enter where the plan said? Did I size it the way Chapter Twenty Two said? Did I leave the stop alone? Over twenty or thirty trades, those grades tell you whether the problem is your strategy or your discipline. A broken strategy needs new rules. Broken discipline needs better systems, like the checklists and daily loss limits from Chapter Twenty Four.

**Jordan:** So the journal separates, is my map wrong, from, am I not following the map? I would never get that from my account balance alone.

**Alex:** That brings us to the weekly review. When the market is closed and you are calm, read your journal like a coach watching game film. Which setup earned the most? Which mistake cost the most? Find one leak at a time, fix that one thing, and test the fix in the following weeks. Small, honest corrections, repeated for months, are how a beginner becomes competent.

**Jordan:** Is there a middle step between the backtest of the past and real trades?

**Alex:** Yes, and it is called forward testing. After a strategy survives an honest backtest, you trade it in real time, in a practice account or at very small size, and you journal every trade. This catches things the past cannot show you, like whether trades arrive at hours you can actually watch, and whether you personally can execute the plan when real prices are moving. The backtest asks, did this ever work? Forward testing asks, does it work now, in my hands? Only then does real size ask the final question.

**Jordan:** Alright, let me recap. Backtesting means applying precise rules a stranger could follow to old data, with at least about one hundred trades, across different conditions, and always charging real costs. The three traps are look ahead bias, survivorship bias, and overfitting, and the protection is freezing the rules and testing them once on out of sample data. I read results by expectancy and worst drawdown, not winning percentage alone. Then journaling takes over. I record the setup, the plan, the risk, and my state of mind, grade execution rather than outcome, review weekly for one leak at a time, and forward test before real size.
