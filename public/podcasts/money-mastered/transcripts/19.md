**Alex:** Welcome back to Money, Mastered. Today we open Part Four, the trading part of this journey, and we start with the most honest chapter in the whole book, because today we ask why most traders lose money.

**Jordan:** That is a cheerful way to start a new part. We just spent six chapters learning how to build a portfolio you can hold, and now you are telling me trading usually goes wrong?

**Alex:** I am, and I would rather tell you now than let the market tell you later at a higher price. Study after study finds the same pattern. Most people who trade actively end up losing money, and many lose a lot of it.

**Jordan:** When you say most, how many are we actually talking about?

**Alex:** The estimates vary, but they all point the same way. Regulators in Europe require brokers to publish the share of their customers who lose money on certain leveraged products, and those warnings often say that somewhere between seventy and ninety percent of accounts lose money. A famous study of day traders in Taiwan found that fewer than one percent could predictably earn good returns after costs, year after year.

**Jordan:** Fewer than one percent. That sounds less like a skill gap and more like the game itself is stacked.

**Alex:** That is our first big idea today, called base rates. A base rate is how often something happens in a whole group, before you know anything special about one person. If ninety percent of active traders lose, a new trader should expect to lose, not because they are foolish, but because that is what happens to almost everyone who plays.

**Jordan:** So the honest starting question is not, how do I win, but, why would I be different from the ninety percent?

**Alex:** Exactly. And notice how different that is from how trading is usually sold. Nobody markets a trading course by saying, here is a game almost everyone loses. They show you the one winner, the screenshot of the one great trade, and your brain quietly assumes that story is normal. That is called survivorship bias. You see the survivors, because the losers do not post their account statements.

**Jordan:** It reminds me of Chapter Seventeen, on market cycles, where we said emotion makes people buy high and sell low. Is that the main reason traders lose, just feelings?

**Alex:** Feelings are a big part, and we will spend a whole chapter on trading psychology later. But today I want you to see three structural reasons that would make even a calm, disciplined person struggle. They are costs, competition, and the simple math of needing an edge. Let us take them one at a time, starting with costs, because costs are the quietest and the most certain.

**Jordan:** Costs, like commissions? I thought commissions were basically free now. Every app says zero commission.

**Alex:** Zero commission is real, but it is not the same as zero cost. Every trade still crosses the spread, which you met in Chapter Eight. The bid is what buyers will pay, the ask is what sellers want, and when you trade instantly, you buy at the ask and sell at the bid, so you start every single trade a little bit behind. It is small, often just a few cents on a stock, but a trader who makes hundreds of trades pays it hundreds of times.

**Jordan:** A few cents does not sound like much though.

**Alex:** On one trade it is not. Imagine a trader with ten thousand dollars who turns over the whole account just once a week. If the round trip cost is only one tenth of one percent each time, that is ten dollars a week, five hundred and twenty dollars a year, more than five percent of the account, gone before any profit or loss on the trades themselves. Trade more often, and that drag can reach ten or twenty percent a year.

**Jordan:** So the trader has to earn ten percent just to break even, while my boring index portfolio from Chapter Eighteen pays almost nothing in costs and keeps the first dollar it earns.

**Alex:** You have it. A trader starts every year in a hole, and a low cost index fund starts at ground level. Two more costs hide underneath. Taxes, because gains on things held less than a year are usually taxed at a higher rate. And your time, which nobody counts.

**Jordan:** Okay, costs are the first wall. What is the second one, competition?

**Alex:** Competition is the part beginners never picture clearly. When you buy a stock, someone is selling it to you at that price. Sometimes that is another beginner. But very often the other side is a professional firm with faster computers, better data, and decades of experience.

**Jordan:** So it is not me against the market in the abstract. It is me against specific people and firms who do this all day.

**Alex:** Right, and here is a useful way to think about it. Trading, after costs, is close to a zero sum game, and with costs it is actually negative sum. For every dollar one trader wins beyond what the market itself gives, another trader has to lose that dollar, plus both of them paid costs along the way. Poker players understand this instantly. If you sit down at a poker table and cannot tell who the weakest player is, it is probably you.

**Jordan:** That is blunt. But markets do go up over time, we covered that with compounding in Chapter Thirteen. Does that not lift traders too?

**Alex:** It lifts owners, and that distinction matters. The long rise of a stock index comes from businesses growing and earning profits, and an investor who simply holds the index collects that rise almost for free. A trader who jumps in and out keeps stepping off that moving walkway and paying to step back on. They can easily miss the best days, which matter enormously, because a large share of a decade of returns often comes from a small number of very strong days, often right after the scariest falls.

**Jordan:** So the trader is fighting costs, fighting professionals, and fighting the walkway itself. That brings us to your third idea, the edge. What exactly is an edge?

**Alex:** An edge is a repeatable reason why your trades should make money over time, after costs, that is not just luck. Repeatable is the key word. Winning three trades in a row proves almost nothing, because pure chance produces winning streaks all the time. If a thousand people flip coins, some will flip five heads in a row and feel like geniuses, until the flips keep going.

**Jordan:** How would a trader ever know if they have a real edge, and not just a lucky streak?

**Alex:** With evidence, and a painful amount of it. Chance is so noisy that you need many trades before the signal stands out. A trader who wins fifty five percent of the time does have a small real edge. But over just twenty trades, that trader could win only eight times, or fourteen times, by luck alone. Over two hundred trades, the truth starts to show.

**Jordan:** Which means most beginners quit, or blow up their account, long before they could even know whether their idea works.

**Alex:** That is the cruel timing of it. And most beginners test ideas in the worst way. They try something for a week, it loses, they switch to the next idea from a video, it wins twice, they bet bigger, and then it fails. They never run anything long enough to learn if it works, but they pay full costs on every attempt. Later we will cover backtesting and journaling, which is how you gather evidence without paying full price for every lesson.

**Jordan:** Let me check I am following the three walls. Costs mean I start behind. Competition means the other side is often stronger than me. And the edge problem means I probably cannot even tell if I am good until I have survived hundreds of trades, which most people do not.

**Alex:** That is a perfect summary. Now add position size, which we will treat properly in Chapter Twenty Two. Many new traders lose in one or two big trades where they bet far too much. Losing fifty percent needs a one hundred percent gain just to get back to even. That is why survival, not brilliance, is the first job of a trader.

**Jordan:** That recovery math came up in Chapter Fourteen with drawdowns. Lose fifty percent, need one hundred percent to recover. Lose seventy five percent, need three hundred percent. The hole gets deeper much faster than it gets shallower.

**Alex:** Exactly, and that asymmetry is why the small group who do survive in trading all sound strangely boring when you listen to them. They talk about risk, records, rules, and costs. They rarely talk about excitement. Meanwhile the loudest voices online sell excitement, because excitement sells courses and sign ups. Always ask who gets paid when you trade more. Very often, it is everyone except you.

**Jordan:** Okay, so here is my honest question. If the base rates are this bad, should nobody trade at all? Is the lesson of this chapter just, do not do it?

**Alex:** No, and I want to be careful here, because that would be the wrong lesson too. Trading is a real skill, some people do build a genuine edge, and markets need traders, because traders provide the liquidity from Chapter Eight. The lesson is narrower. Do not trade casually, do not trade with money you need, and do not confuse a bull market lifting everyone with personal skill. Treat trading like starting a small business. The ones that survive plan, keep costs low, and keep records from day one.

**Jordan:** A small business, not a lottery ticket.

**Alex:** Exactly. That framing gives us the path through Part Four. We will learn to read price, understand volume, size positions, plan entries and exits, manage psychology, and test and journal everything. None of that guarantees success. It moves you from gambling on hope to running a process you can measure, and in a game with these base rates, that is the only respectable way to play.

And an edge is hard to detect, because luck dominates small samples, so I need hundreds of recorded trades to know anything real. The takeaway is not never trade, it is never trade casually.
