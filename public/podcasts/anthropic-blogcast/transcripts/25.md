**Jordan:** Let us walk through the case studies, starting with Generate Biomedicines. Generate is working on a cancer treatment called C A R T cell therapy. It has historically helped treat blood cancers, and Generate wants to reengineer it so it can treat solid tumor cancers too.

**Alex:** Their approach is to shorten one part of the therapy's genetic instructions, which creates room for additional instructions suited to other kinds of cancer. Six Generate scientists used Claude Code to propose new designs for that part. One design came out about seventy percent shorter than the standard version, and in lab tests it was more effective. It allowed immune cells to make roughly ten times as many receptors, the proteins they use to spot cancer cells. It killed tumor cells more efficiently, and the immune cells stayed active for longer.

**Jordan:** That is a striking result for a design exercise. The second case is Divergence Labs, which is working on a new way to treat food allergies. Food allergies are set off by a molecule called I G E. Some of it sits attached to our immune cells, and some floats freely in our blood. Standard medications mostly neutralize the I G E circulating in the blood. Divergence wants a drug that detaches the I G E from the immune cells entirely.

**Alex:** That design needs two arms. One arm binds tightly to I G E, and the other helps prise it away from the immune cell. Claude turned Divergence's goals into a detailed design plan, and then, largely on its own, generated roughly thirty-five thousand antibody designs in about a month, to be narrowed down and tested.

**Jordan:** And the early tests found hits for both arms. Three designs could bind to I G E, and twenty could dislodge it. Three of those twenty appeared to dislodge I G E more effectively than the standard medication. The next step is to build a single drug that combines the two arms.

**Alex:** The third case, BigHat Biosciences, is about lead optimization. BigHat pairs A I with an automated lab to improve potential drugs, called leads. A good lead has to bind its target tightly, stay stable through manufacturing and storage, and avoid clumping together. Claude, connected to BigHat's lab data system, proposed changes to two antibody fragments, one designed to target cancer cells and one designed to keep a drug in the body longer. BigHat's lab then made and tested about one thousand ten variants in about ten weeks.

**Jordan:** My favorite detail in the whole post lives here. Partway through, the team suspected that the standard heat stability and clumping metrics were misleading. Claude and an Anthropic researcher reanalyzed the raw data, proposed different metrics, and suggested a protocol change to test them. BigHat's scientists refined and ran that protocol, and the main predictions held.

**Alex:** And the results were large. Compared with the originals, the best versions bound more than ten times more tightly. The cancer targeting lead stayed in solution at a temperature eighteen degrees Celsius higher. And the lead meant to make drugs last longer clumped more than one hundred times more slowly.

**Jordan:** The fourth case is Enveda, and it is about the paperwork side of drug development, which turns out to be enormous. To run clinical trials, companies need records showing every batch was made to strict quality standards, plus applications to regulators. At Enveda, manufacturing chemists spent up to seventy-five percent of their time on paperwork.

**Alex:** Enveda built a system that connects all of its manufacturing documents to Claude and lets Claude flag possible issues. Across tests on one thousand sixty-one pages of documentation, Claude caught all eleven issues that human reviewers had spotted, and flagged seven more that they had missed. Human review time fell by eighty-nine percent, from over twenty-five hours to less than three.

**Jordan:** Claude also wrote the first drafts of two important regulatory documents. One took four hours, compared with about three months by hand. For the other, Enveda's standard human review process found just one minor error.

**Alex:** The fifth story is The Column Group, an investor in biotechnology companies. Because drug discovery is slow, expensive, and prone to failure, investors have favored safer, previously validated ideas over riskier new ones, even when the riskier ideas could help more patients. The Column Group is establishing a new company called Pleiades that will use Claude to run many more, and more complex, drug programs than is usual for a small team.
