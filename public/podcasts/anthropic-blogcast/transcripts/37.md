**Alex:** Welcome to Anthropic Blogcast. Today we are talking about what happens when you put one thousand agents on the same hard problem, and how you keep that safe.

**Jordan:** This is the new developer post from Claude dot dev, called Safely scaling multi-agent teams with Claude Fable five point two. It is a follow up to a story from September.

**Alex:** In September, Anthropic published the first end to end formalization of Fermat's Last Theorem. That is the claim that no positive whole numbers a, b, and c can satisfy a to the power n plus b to the power n equals c to the power n, when n is greater than two.

**Jordan:** It went unproved for more than three hundred and fifty years, until Andrew Wiles published his proof in nineteen ninety five. Formalizing it means translating every step into a form a computer can check, in this case in a language called Lean.

**Alex:** The September version took a human researcher and dozens of agents eleven days. It used roughly six billion output tokens, wrote thirteen million lines of Lean code, and leaned on a special platform called Prove Two Me to keep everyone coordinated.

**Jordan:** So the new experiment asks, can a much bigger team do it again without that special platform, and without a researcher steering the work?

**Alex:** The answer is yes, and faster. One thousand agents running an early version of Claude Fable five point two reproduced the proof in twenty eight point seven hours. They used one third as many output tokens, and their proof was one seventh the length of the original.

**Jordan:** That matters for developers, because it tests whether general purpose tools can coordinate a huge team on a long horizon job.

**Alex:** Each agent got sixty statements to prove, including the final theorem and fifty nine intermediate milestones, plus a one sentence sketch of the classical route, the definitions involved, and forty seven already proved lemmas from the earlier work. That was basically it for math guidance.

**Jordan:** They were not told how to organize, apart from one structure, one lead agent and nine hundred and ninety nine helpers. The lead was told to split the problem and assign work. Helpers were told to expect work from the lead, and to check if something was unclear.

**Alex:** The tools were deliberately ordinary. Every agent worked in its own sandbox with no internet access, with a shell and a file editor, the Lean toolchain and the Mathlib library, its own Git branch with the ability to open merge requests into main, and access to a shared Postgres database.

**Jordan:** Why a database? S Q L offers general primitives for organizing information, its transactions handle concurrent writes from one thousand agents, and Claude is already familiar with it. Instead of a custom platform, coordination ran through Git and S Q L.

**Alex:** Scale forced that choice. A single shared machine hit its limits at about one hundred agents, with more than half of those runs stopped or restarted because memory, disk, or C P U ran out. Separate sandboxes solved that part.

**Jordan:** Now the safety side, which is the point of the title. Anthropic says no single layer is enough, so they combined sandboxing, real time monitoring, and offline monitoring.

**Alex:** In the sandbox, agents had no dedicated messaging tools, only the database and Git. An agent could push only to its own branch. Database calls ran under an unprivileged role, each statement had a five second limit, and writes were refused above two hundred and fifty six megabytes.

**Jordan:** There was no network access. The only link out was a pipe to a gateway that tagged each request with the agent's identity, so one agent could not pose as another. Commands ran as an unprivileged user, tools like sudo were removed, and agents could not read or change the agent loop that ran them.

**Alex:** Then real time monitoring. A prompted large language model classifier checked every shell command and file edit before it ran, watching for escape attempts or tampering with monitoring. In red team tests, other agents tried decomposition attacks, splitting a bad plan into pieces that each looked harmless. Monitors that could see chain of thought and communication between agents detected all sabotage attempts in those evaluations.

**Jordan:** In this run they used only the action based monitor, which sees actions but not chain of thought. Across more than one million tool calls, none met the bar to be blocked. Replayed later against the chain of thought monitor, only one call came close, a check for root access. An offline monitor also reviewed actions afterwards, with the ability to look across agents.

**Alex:** With those boundaries in place, what did the team do? In the first ten minutes, the lead created five tables in the shared database, entirely on its own.

**Jordan:** Announce, for the lead's orders to everyone. Assign, for who works on what. Notes, an open board for claiming work and reporting progress. Subtasks, a shared to do list where the first helper to claim a task owns it. And Landed, a log of finished work.

**Alex:** The lead assigned each statement to a crew of sixteen or seventeen helpers, and made one helper in each crew a captain to post a plan and split the work. Helpers started by judging how hard their statement was and posting a verdict.

**Jordan:** About twenty seven minutes in, three statements were already proved. The lead set aside the forty seven statements most helpers judged infeasible, and reassigned nearly everyone to the remaining ten. Crews grew to between forty six and one hundred and thirty nine helpers, and all ten were proved within roughly seventy minutes.

**Alex:** The lead noted that throughput was far higher than those early verdicts assumed, wrote that there is no reason to idle, and moved helpers onto the harder statements it had discarded. Helpers took more initiative too. By hour five point six, seventy three helpers had written seven hundred and fifty six tasks, and only sixteen were captains. By the third hour the lead was telling free helpers, do not wait for me.

**Jordan:** That is a useful pattern for builders. Tight assignment helped at the start, but progress accelerated when helpers could claim and create work without waiting for the lead.

**Alex:** The hardest piece left was Langlands Tunnell, a classical result Wiles's proof relies on. Its captain estimated at least seven hundred thousand to one million lines of Lean code, with less than a one percent chance of finishing in time, so it had been set aside.

**Jordan:** After a restart around hour eighteen, with nearly everything else done, the lead refocused about seven hundred and fifty helpers onto it, honestly noting the odds were low. The team organized it as a tree, dividing it into ten areas, and splitting any piece that was too big for one helper. The tree grew to two thousand four hundred and eighteen tasks, up to ten levels deep.

**Alex:** Langlands Tunnell was proved at hour twenty eight point four, and the final theorem at hour twenty eight point seven. The lead closed with, thank you all, Fermat's Last Theorem is on main, kernel checked by the official checker. In total there were more than thirty six thousand writes and more than three hundred and eleven thousand reads to the shared tables.

**Jordan:** The post is careful not to oversell it. Domain specific harnesses may still help, for example by compiling Lean faster or reusing human results. And agents misjudge difficulty. They judged forty seven of sixty statements infeasible, yet proved most of them. The final count was fifty four of sixty proved, with five alternatives no longer needed and one dropped, which was enough to complete the theorem.

**Alex:** So three takeaways. First, coordination can start with ordinary tools, Git for code and a database for claims, notes, and status, plus a clear lead and helper split.

**Jordan:** Second, safety has to be layered before scale, isolated sandboxes, least privilege, a blocking classifier on every tool call, and offline review.

**Alex:** And third, expect surprises in both directions. A team may underestimate what it can do, and if you let it reassign people and split big tasks into a tree, it may finish work its own captains thought was almost impossible.
