**Jordan:** Today we are going back to a developer post from claude dot dev that a lot of Claude Code users will feel in their daily work. It is called Using Claude Code, Spending Your Effort, and it asks a deceptively simple question. What is effort, really, and when should you use which effort level?

**Alex:** Effort is one of those controls people move without fully knowing what it changes. This post does a deep dive into the evaluations, and the author also ran his own tests of effort across normal work, so we get both the benchmark picture and a practical loop you can copy.

**Jordan:** Let us start with the definition. At a high level, effort gives the model an approximation of how much compute you want it to spend on the task. It is somewhat related to your own sense of how difficult the task is.

**Alex:** He makes the point with a human comparison. If someone gave you twelve hours for a task, you would assume they want you to try very hard. If they gave you one hour, you would deliver the best version that fits, and expect to iterate. Claude will always try to do your task reasonably, but higher effort means it takes more independent action, uses more of its own judgment, and does more verification.

**Jordan:** That last word, verification, is the center of the whole post. The author's headline finding is that effort was a great way of modulating how much verification and edge case testing Claude did. Extra effort gave better results in domains where that testing matters more, like hardware, code review, and security.

**Alex:** And for normal software engineering, he is not running everything at max. He describes a loop he actually uses. He gets the model to interview him first, then implements on low effort, reviews what it built, and then runs verification on high effort.

**Jordan:** That loop treats effort as something you spend in stages, not a single setting you pick and forget. Low effort for the first build keeps you in the loop and moving fast. High effort for the verification pass is where the extra compute earns its keep.

**Alex:** The post then tests what effort does to a build, trying the same tasks at several levels on Claude Opus five point five. First, an underspecified task, building a personal fitness tracker app. At low effort, the app is just a log and a simple graph. At higher levels it grows more detailed, and at max effort there is a heat chart as well. With a vague ask, higher effort means a more fleshed out result, but also more choices made on your behalf.

**Jordan:** Second, a lightly specified task, redesigning the config menu in Claude Code. Every pass had roughly the same idea, submenus and better search, but at low effort, in one minute, he got a rough interactive sketch, while at max effort, in twenty-eight minutes, he got a polished mockup with walkthroughs for different flows. For iterating and giving feedback, he prefers low effort first, to see Claude's vision fast.

**Alex:** Third, when he gave Claude a detailed specification from an in depth interview, the models behaved much more similarly at every level, and max effort mainly simplified a few details. So for regular feature work, low effort gives a quick starting point, while higher effort gets more done but makes more assumptions for you.

**Alex:** Then the post turns to the harder question. Those were tasks Claude could complete either way. What about cases where effort is the difference between completing a task and not completing it at all? For that, the author went to Terminal-Bench three point zero, a community sourced benchmark with far more ambitious problems than the average daily task.

**Jordan:** His main takeaway from those results is that higher effort is best for tasks with lots of hidden edge cases. He proves it with a clean example, a task that asks for a filter that strips every way of smuggling JavaScript into a web page.

**Alex:** On that task, Claude Fable five point one went from succeeding one time out of five at low effort, to five times out of five at extra high effort. And the difference was not magic, it was work. A typical low effort attempt takes about two minutes, writes the filter in roughly one pass, and tests it against a single hand written page.

**Jordan:** A high effort run finishes in about thirty-three minutes. In the run he traced, Claude reviewed its first draft adversarially, read the installed parser's source code to check for bugs, ran many clean test cases, ran a standard security test suite for cross site scripting, and finally wrote a random document fuzzer.

**Alex:** That is the pattern in one story. Low effort writes and spot checks. High effort writes, attacks its own work, reads the underlying code, and builds a tool to hunt for what it missed.

**Jordan:** The post also breaks down every result by how it failed, and the pattern matters. Increasing effort tends to reduce failures that come from missing edge cases. It does not fix the cases where the model simply has the wrong approach. Effort is not a cure for a wrong plan. It makes a workable plan much more thoroughly checked.

**Alex:** The gains also depend on the problem area. Comparing pass rates at low effort with pass rates at top effort for Fable five point one, security rose from sixty-four percent to eighty-seven percent, hardware from thirty-four percent to seventy-five percent, and software from forty-three percent to fifty-six percent. Effort helps everywhere, but it helps most where hidden edge cases are densest.

**Jordan:** He then picks three problems where Opus five point five failed at low effort and succeeded at high effort, mostly because it tested and accounted for edge cases. The first is a storage engine bug fix, where the fix must not break a process called compaction. Opus five point five went from zero successes out of five at low effort, to four out of five at extra high effort.

**Alex:** At low effort, taking about a minute per attempt, Claude would edit the code before building it or running the reproducer, and it did not check that its new test would have caught the original bug. At extra high effort, taking about eleven minutes, Claude reproduced the crash first, wrote a randomized test against a reference version that never compacts, and checked that its tests actually failed on half finished fixes. The order of operations changed, and that changed the outcome.

**Jordan:** The second example is a command line solver for linear programming problems. Opus five point five went from zero out of five at low, to five out of five at high. The low attempts wrote a solver in one pass, checked a few small problems, and stopped at around ten thousand tokens, even warning it might be slow on big problems without checking. At high effort, Claude tested random problems against a separate brute force solver, timed bigger ones, hit cases that ran too long or crashed, and reworked its search.

**Alex:** The third example is a data analysis task, finding which of eight treatments resemble a target tissue. At low effort, Claude picked one reasonable way to prepare the data and reported the result. At high effort, it tried two ways, noticed the list of significant treatments changed, and dug into why before choosing the correct one. With no user in the loop to ask, high effort does better because Claude does that comparison itself.

**Alex:** The author is also careful about the numbers. He notes these results come from Anthropic's own internal runs, with five attempts per task, and that some worked examples come from individual runs at intermediate settings. So treat the figures as a guide to the pattern, not a promise for your own repository.

**Jordan:** Which brings us to the part most listeners can use tomorrow, his rule of thumb for effort levels in Claude Code. Low effort is for quick responses while you stay in the loop, for example brainstorming, sketching, and easy changes. Medium effort is for most regular software engineering work, for example implementing a new feature.

**Alex:** High effort is for work where verification is important, or where there are edge cases, for example fixing a bug in an older, established codebase. Max effort is for when you want Claude to operate fully autonomously on difficult problems, for example building and verifying an app end to end, or finding security vulnerabilities in critical software.

**Jordan:** He closes by inviting readers to try varying effort for Opus five point five and Fable five point one based on the task, or even mid conversation, by using the effort command in Claude Code.

**Alex:** So here is the takeaway. Effort is a budget for judgment, verification, and edge case hunting. Spend it low when you want a fast starting point and your own review will do the checking. Spend it high when the cost of a missed edge case is real, when the task is hard to verify by eye, or when Claude has to work without you in the loop.

**Jordan:** And remember the limit as well as the promise. More effort will not rescue a wrong approach, but on a sound approach it can be the difference between a first draft that looks right, and work that has actually survived being tested.
