**Alex:** Welcome back to Anthropic Blogcast. Today we are looking at a security post from Anthropic, published on October twelfth, twenty twenty six, called Strengthening Our Defenses Against Distillation Attacks. The short version is that Anthropic says new defenses have cut the amount of reasoning data that attackers can harvest from Claude by roughly ninety to ninety nine percent.

**Jordan:** Let us start with the word distillation itself, because in this context it does not mean making a smaller model in the normal, legitimate way. Anthropic is talking about illicit distillation, which means a covert campaign to pull capabilities out of a frontier model and use them to train another model, without permission.

**Alex:** Exactly. The attackers send huge numbers of requests to Claude, collect the responses, and then train their own model on those responses so it learns to behave in a similar way. Anthropic says that lets another company approach frontier capability for a fraction of the time and cost of developing it themselves.

**Jordan:** And the most valuable material is not just the final answers, is it. The post says the real prize is the reasoning transcripts, meaning the thinking Claude writes out before it answers.

**Alex:** Right. A final answer tells a student model what Claude said. A reasoning transcript shows how Claude got there, step by step, and that is much more useful for teaching a smaller model to copy the behavior. So almost all of the defenses in this post are about making that reasoning harder to extract.

**Jordan:** Anthropic gives two reasons for caring so much. The first is safeguards. A distilled model can reach close to the frontier, but it may be released without safeguards that are anywhere near as strong.

**Alex:** And they name a concrete example in the post, involving a model whose cyber capabilities were built partly by distilling leading United States models, and which Anthropic says can be bypassed easily and will help develop security exploits from start to finish. That is the worry in plain terms, capability travels, but caution does not always travel with it.

**Jordan:** The second reason is national security. Anthropic says some of the companies doing this are putting the gained capabilities into foreign military, intelligence, and surveillance systems, and it describes a case where requests to another lab's models were secretly forwarded to Claude as part of a distillation effort.

**Alex:** One of those requests, they say, came from a user they believe was affiliated with a foreign military, and it asked for help analyzing surveillance camera footage of a specific individual. So this is not an abstract argument about copying. It is about where the copied capability ends up.

**Jordan:** Now, why were simple defenses not enough. Anthropic says its early approach was to summarize Claude's reasoning before showing it, which blunts passive distillation, where an attacker just uses the model normally and trains on what comes back.

**Alex:** And they measured that. In their test, a model trained on full reasoning transcripts scored about eighty point four percent on a well known coding benchmark, while the same setup trained on summarized transcripts scored about seventy one point eight percent. Summaries helped, but they did not end the problem.

**Jordan:** Because attackers moved from passive to active extraction. Instead of accepting the summary, they tried to trick Claude into revealing the full reasoning anyway.

**Alex:** The post describes tactics ranging from a direct instruction to output the prior reasoning word for word, all the way to one lab testing twelve thousand different requests, each designed in a different way to get past the guardrails. Ordinary users almost never ask for hidden reasoning like that, which is part of how Anthropic learned to spot these attempts.

**Jordan:** But spotting some attempts was not enough either, because the scale changed. Anthropic describes proxy networks running hundreds of thousands of fraudulent accounts, and one network managing more than twenty thousand fraudulent accounts at once, using false identities and stolen payment details.

**Alex:** Which brings us to the three new layers of defense. The first layer is controlling where reasoning can go. Attackers had been moving protected reasoning blocks between conversations, between accounts, and even between models, trying to trick a less protected model into revealing a stronger model's reasoning.

**Jordan:** And the fix has names developers will recognize. With the newest models, Anthropic introduced something called preserved thinking, which prevents newer accounts, meaning accounts created on or after August thirty first, twenty twenty six, from editing Claude's prior context. They also stopped earlier models with weaker safeguards from reading the newer models' reasoning, and for one recent model they limited a reasoning transcript to the organization whose request created it.

**Alex:** The second layer is blocking and patching extraction attempts directly. Anthropic says it built a two stage classifier system, similar in structure to the safeguards it uses for dangerous biology and cybersecurity requests, and then red teamed it internally and externally and fixed what the testers found.

**Jordan:** The numbers there are striking. Anthropic says those safeguards are now about as robust as the ones that stop Claude from helping build bioweapons, and that they wrongly block fewer than zero point one percent of legitimate requests. That classifier runs on its most capable generally available models, and they plan to keep it on future models that would be attractive targets.

**Alex:** It is also a living system, not a one time patch. Anthropic says that when it sees a new trick, like asking Claude to translate its earlier reasoning into another language, or to hide that reasoning in tags alongside an answer, it updates the safeguards quickly.

**Jordan:** The third layer is making repeated attempts painful. Accounts that keep trying to extract reasoning face stricter usage limits, persistent offenders get banned, and Anthropic says it has banned tens of thousands of accounts linked to distillation, often in less than two hours after a campaign begins.

**Alex:** There is also a pricing change with real teeth. Since September twenty fourth, Anthropic has resumed charging for requests that its safeguards block before Claude responds, in the specific areas where the false positive rate is below zero point one percent. So failed extraction attempts are no longer free to keep trying.

**Jordan:** So how well does all of this actually work. Anthropic estimates the amount of reasoning tokens extracted on its own platform, and after the classifier arrived, that amount was consistently ten to one hundred times lower than before, which lines up with the ninety to ninety nine percent reduction they lead with.

**Alex:** With an honest caveat, which I appreciated. They say they do not yet know how long these gains will last, they expect a continuing cat and mouse game, and they have not yet seen a highly sophisticated and persistent attempt to get around the new system. In other words, this is progress, not a permanent victory.

**Jordan:** And there are trade offs for legitimate users, which the post does not hide. Preserved thinking has required some developers to update their integrations. The classifiers can still block a benign request in fewer than zero point one percent of cases, and now users are charged for those blocked requests.

**Alex:** Anthropic also says it has turned away meaningful revenue and trillions of tokens worth of paying traffic from distillers, and that its research and product teams have spent substantial time on this. That matters, because the post ends with an argument aimed at the whole industry, not just at Anthropic customers.

**Jordan:** Right, the closing argument is that distillation is a problem for every developer and every platform. If one frontier lab protects its models but another equally capable lab does not, attackers will simply go to the weaker door, which is exactly what Anthropic says happened in the example it opened with.

**Alex:** And platforms matter too, because distillers spread traffic across the main application programming interface, third party cloud platforms, and other inference providers. Anthropic says those platforms can slow distillation a lot by detecting abuse quickly and removing fraudulent accounts.

**Jordan:** The uncomfortable incentive is stated plainly. Defenses are expensive, distillers are still paying customers, and doing nothing would be easy. Anthropic says it published its methods to show that a workable alternative exists, even with the costs and the trade offs.

**Alex:** So the takeaway for listeners is threefold. First, the reasoning behind an answer is now one of the most valuable and most protected assets in a frontier model. Second, protecting it took a layered system, controlling where reasoning can move, classifying extraction attempts, and making repetition slow, costly, and likely to end in a ban. And third, no single company can solve this alone, because attackers will always test the least defended model they can reach.

**Jordan:** That is the episode for today. A frontier model can be copied through its own answers if nobody guards the door, and Anthropic argues guarding that door has to become normal practice. Thanks for listening to Anthropic Blogcast.
