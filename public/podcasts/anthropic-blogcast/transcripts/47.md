It is a picture of knowledge work in general.

**Jordan:** And the headline finding is a hopeful one, with a catch. The companies described very few layoffs, but they also described reduced hiring. And the most valuable uses of AI often involved reorganizing roles and workplaces around it, rather than simply dropping a chatbot into the old workflow.

**Alex:** Let us start with where the value actually shows up. Across the interviews, the team catalogued nearly three hundred AI applications that people described in detail. Most of them added value by increasing a company's output, rather than by cutting costs.

**Jordan:** That split matters. About half of the output gains came from AI accelerating or expanding work people were already doing. The other half came from improving the quality of products and services, or creating entirely new ones. A medical group, for example, used AI to give patients an up front estimate of what care would cost after insurance.

**Alex:** And in about one tenth of the cases, the value was captured directly by the employees themselves, who were either doing more enjoyable work or simply doing less work altogether. One manager said bringing AI into his construction company meant that work could be a joy again instead of a difficult, painful thing.

**Jordan:** Agents are doing remarkably varied things out there. Some are sending faxes back and forth as doctors' offices refer and intake patients. Eighty percent of that software automated previously manual work, like spreadsheet based financial updates or taking meeting notes. The other twenty percent replaced existing software, often software the company had been buying from outside vendors.

**Jordan:** Which brings us to the first big strategy the researchers found, top down automation. That is where a company picks a workflow, automates it from above, and rolls the solution out to everyone.

**Alex:** And that is where the employment effects were sharpest. People at two thirds of the companies reported reduced hiring because of AI. As one person put it, we may not be firing, but we are not hiring. A third of those hiring declines came from companies actively shrinking functions through attrition, without backfilling the roles.

**Jordan:** Most of the rest came from companies forgoing hiring they would otherwise have needed in order to grow. One engineering leader at a health management company said, we have added a lot of capacity by buying tokens instead of hiring people. That sentence might be the whole labor story of this moment in miniature.

**Alex:** Actual layoffs driven by AI were rare in these interviews. Only two companies had seen AI automation fully eliminate a position and lead to someone losing their job. And ten companies reported hiring more because of AI, mainly into AI implementation roles and IT support.

**Jordan:** The jobs that were cut were often contractor roles. Nine companies described cutting contracted software developers, customer service representatives, legal counsel, and back office functions, many of which had already been offshored. One finance company downsized well over one hundred offshore data and developer contractors.

**Alex:** The startups were the extreme case. One chief operating officer said ninety percent of his operations budget was spent on AI tokens and ten percent on headcount. And the six startups that gave an estimate said they would need, on average, four times as many employees without AI.

**Jordan:** Top down automation had a darker side too, and that was monitoring. Some companies are using AI to watch employees more closely, with one supervisor starting each day by reviewing an AI report on the throughput and error rate of each member of his twelve person team.

**Alex:** One engineering executive summed up the shift by saying, you want to make your work legible to your manager, but nowadays you want to make your work legible to the agents your manager uses. Another executive called the risk a panopticon effect. The post notes that the monitoring they heard about appeared to comply with Anthropic's usage policy, which requires disclosure to employees and a human in the loop for employment decisions.

**Jordan:** And top down systems turned out to be brittle. They are often built by outside vendors, they go stale as models improve, and one employee said of one such system, it will be obsolete in three months. A law firm built document automations that ended up as low quality zombie apps that hardly anyone used.

**Alex:** There is also a human problem at the center of top down automation. Building an effective workflow usually requires experts to design the prompts and evaluate the outputs. But that means experts are being asked to build and maintain the very systems that could replace them. One employee put it bluntly, if I am open to this, I am essentially building my own coffin.

**Jordan:** So that is strategy one. Strategy two is the opposite, bottom up experimentation, where a company hands out AI tools and waits for individuals to find valuable uses on their own.

**Alex:** The stars of the bottom up world were often nontechnical experts. Quality assurance supervisors at a chemical plant built an app to monitor filtration rates despite having no coding experience at all. **Alex:** But bottom up success has a ceiling. An individual can replace a ten thousand dollar a year software product, but someone still has to review and maintain what they build.

**Jordan:** And when one part of the work speeds up, the bottleneck just moves. One engineer translated the text on their products into ten different languages, then realized they had nobody who could answer the phone in those languages, so the feature was turned off and never shipped.

**Alex:** Power users can also make life harder for everyone else. **Jordan:** Which sets up the third path, and it is the one Anthropic clearly finds most promising. Some companies are reorganizing around AI, making complementary changes to the organization itself instead of just automating old workflows or handing out tools.

**Alex:** The first move is broadening roles, so one person or a small team owns an entire workflow instead of one narrow slice of it. Engineers start working across product, design, and even customer engagement. One healthcare startup asked all of its engineers to speak regularly to the medical professionals who used their product.

**Jordan:** One engineering leader said, two people plus Claude is the most amazing thing, because if two people agree on something, they can just ship it. Another company described collapsing designer, product manager, engineer, and quality assurance, and even infrastructure, into a single role.

**Alex:** Broadening roles also softens the fear of job loss, because even when part of a job is automated, the person still owns the whole workflow and the ongoing maintenance. One engineer automated the system that responded to production alert tickets, and then spent months reading transcripts, tweaking prompts, and repairing integrations, until the company basically ran on it.

**Jordan:** The second move is redeploying people toward bottleneck tasks, the work AI still cannot do well. Ellen, a software engineer who had feared coding agents would make her obsolete, eventually concluded that the entire job had switched from writing code to making decisions.

**Alex:** Project managers at a construction company used to spend their mornings calling crew members at different sites and writing up daily reports. **Jordan:** And for engineers, code review became the critical bottleneck, because code is no longer the scarce thing. One CTO put the human role beautifully. Claude is a terrible product manager, he said, so a lot of the job is now taste, deciding what features are important.

**Alex:** The third move is building common infrastructure, especially shared data. A trucking company built an agent accessible data warehouse, and managers across the company began running their own analyses instead of waiting in a queue that could stretch a year or two. **Jordan:** That democratization shifts power downward, and not everyone loved it at first. At one company, the reporting team initially protested that people were reporting on things that the reporting team normally reported on. The AI advocates won, and the reporting team's work changed from building dashboards to offering advice and insight.

**Alex:** So let us pull the threads together. The conclusion Anthropic draws is that bottlenecks are the real story. If prototyping is fast but deployment is not, or production is fast but marketing is slow, then AI delivers far less than model evaluations would imply.

**Jordan:** As one executive put it, the closer you are to the money, the harder it is for models to excel in that job. The companies that got the most from AI were the ones willing to move people toward the interpersonal and strategic tasks where humans are still the bottleneck.

**Alex:** And there is an honest caveat at the end.
