**Alex:** Welcome to Anthropic Blogcast. Today we are talking about a question that sounds simple, but turns out to be surprisingly hard to answer. When an A I model sends you a response, how do you actually know that the model you asked for is the model that answered?

**Jordan:** Right, because right now, you mostly just have to trust the provider. You pick a frontier model, you pay for that model, and you get some text back. But that text could have come from a cheaper model swapped in by a routing service, or from a degraded copy, or, in a worst case, from an attacker who tampered with the stream, and you would have no reliable way to tell just by reading the output.

**Alex:** That is the problem Anthropic is tackling in a new research post from November second, called Progress on provable inference for large language models. The team, led by Roy Rinberg and Alex Allain at Anthropic with collaborators from E P F L and M I T, is sharing a system called FlashProve, and they are releasing the code, the proofs, and the documentation openly.

**Jordan:** So let us unpack that phrase, provable inference. What does it actually mean in plain language?

**Alex:** It means the provider does not just send you an answer. They also send you a cryptographic proof that goes with that answer. First, long before any conversation, the provider publishes a commitment to a specific model. Think of that commitment as a short fingerprint of the exact model weights. It pins down one precise set of weights, but it does not reveal those weights.

**Jordan:** And then, when the model answers a prompt, the provider creates a proof that says, the committed model, run on this prompt, produces exactly these tokens. Anyone with the commitment, the prompt, and the reply can check that proof quickly, without ever seeing the private weights.

**Alex:** Exactly, and the tool they use is called a zk SNARK, which is short for zero knowledge succinct argument of knowledge. Zero knowledge means the proof reveals nothing about the weights beyond the fact that the statement is correct. Succinct means the proof is small and fast to check, even though the computation it certifies is enormous.

**Jordan:** That matters, because the naive way to check would be to run the model yourself. But a frontier model is far too large for a typical user, or even for most evaluators, to run on their own. FlashProve tries to give you the confidence of rerunning it, without the cost of rerunning it.

**Alex:** And the headline result is how much faster this has gotten. The team tested FlashProve on Llama Four Maverick, which is a four hundred billion parameter mixture of experts model, with about seventeen billion parameters actually used for each token, using four bit quantized weights.

**Jordan:** And on a single H one hundred G P U, FlashProve generates proofs at around one hundred and ten tokens per second. Each proof covers a window of one thousand and twenty four tokens, and the proof itself is about nineteen megabytes per request. Checking a proof takes about seventeen seconds on a twenty four core C P U, and the commitment you download first is only about six point five megabytes.

**Alex:** Proving is still slower than just running the model, about seventy to ninety five times the G P U time of running it in v L L M, a common inference library, so you would not do this for every chat today. But compared to prior work, it is a dramatic jump. The only earlier system that proved the same model, called VerInf, managed about zero point zero two tokens per second, took about fourteen point three hours for one thousand tokens, and produced a ninety three point six gigabyte proof that took almost a day to verify.

**Jordan:** How does it pull that off?

**Alex:** A few choices help. The prover only does work proportional to the experts that actually ran, while still hiding which experts were chosen. Requests are proved in batches after the fact, and the commitment is opened once per batch. The system fits on a single G P U, streaming weights in segment by segment. An offline phase runs once per model, takes about thirty nine minutes, and is good for about sixteen million requests.

**Jordan:** Okay, so why go to all this trouble? What changes if you can actually prove inference?

**Alex:** The post walks through four challenges. The first is pacing. Some proposals for pacing A I development rely on knowing how hardware is being used, for example, that a provider is devoting hardware to serving an approved model rather than secretly training a new one. If a regulator can ask for proof certificates covering the inference that was served, a provider cannot fake them without actually running the claimed model.

**Jordan:** The second is tamper detection. As models get more valuable, inference servers become prime targets. An attacker, or even a more advanced model trying to pass itself off as a weaker authorized one, could serve malicious responses while hiding among the genuine ones. If every response has to carry a valid proof bound to the committed weights, that substitution gets detected, because the attacker cannot forge a proof for weights they do not have.

**Alex:** The third is text provenance, or proof of effort. Imagine a developer says their library was scanned for vulnerabilities by a frontier cybersecurity model. Today, publishing the chat logs proves almost nothing, because those logs could have been generated by a cheaper model. With provable inference, you could attach a proof to that work, and you could even prove a negative, that a specific model spent at least one million tokens looking for bugs in a patch and did not find any.

**Jordan:** And the fourth is model approval. If regulation requires that only approved, safe models be served, a provider might be tempted to serve a riskier or cheaper model instead. Requiring a proof with each response gives a regulator a lightweight way to check, without rerunning the request themselves.

**Alex:** Anthropic is also explicit about trust in the proof system itself. Cryptographic code is notoriously brittle, so they use formal verification in a language called Lean. They state three properties, completeness, soundness, and zero knowledge, as mathematical statements in Lean, and they prove them in a way that is machine checkable. They ship two verifiers, a fast one written in C plus plus that you would use day to day, and a slower executable verifier in Lean that is provably correct. For soundness, the verifier is really the whole game. If you trust the verifier, you do not have to trust the prover at all.

**Jordan:** And there is a nice twist on who did the optimizing. The post says Claude itself produced improvements to the system, finding novel ways to apply tools from the cryptography literature. That is part of why they lean so heavily on those auditable Lean statements, because Claude is good at optimization, but it often makes decisions that are hard for humans to evaluate in a large, complex system.

**Alex:** So what are the limits? Anthropic is clear that this is a research prototype, not a finished product.

**Jordan:** They list them directly. There are no formal guarantees on the fast code itself. The proof is about an integer version of the model under greedy decoding, so a real floating point or sampled reply could diverge. It only proves contexts up to one thousand and twenty four tokens, far short of modern windows. It hides the weights, not the architecture, and side channel attacks are out of scope.

**Alex:** So this will not be attached to every Claude response tomorrow. It is intermediate progress, released under the Apache two point zero license so others can improve it. It pursues the same objective Anthropic set in its Frontier Safety Roadmap, a prototype for verifying inference traffic by September thirtieth, twenty twenty six. They met that goal through a separate project, and FlashProve is a different, purely cryptographic path to the same goal.

**Jordan:** If I step back, the takeaway is that trust in A I is moving from a social promise to a checkable artifact. Today we trust that a provider served the model they claimed, because we have no practical alternative. FlashProve suggests a future where, at least for the requests that matter most, for evaluators, for enterprise customers going through a router, for regulators, and for security teams, that claim could come with evidence anyone can verify in seconds, without learning the secrets inside the model.

**Alex:** And the speed numbers are what make that future feel closer. Going from fourteen hours and a ninety gigabyte proof to one hundred and ten tokens per second and a nineteen megabyte proof on the same huge model does not finish the job, but it changes the conversation from, is this even possible, to, where would we use it first.
