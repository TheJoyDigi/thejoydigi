**Alex:** Welcome to Anthropic Blogcast. Today we are talking about a guest post on Anthropic's research site called Teaching AI to Build Scientific Models, and it asks a question that goes much deeper than whether an AI can fit a curve.

**Jordan:** Right, because fitting the data turns out to be the easy part. The authors are Jakob Macke, Daniel Gedon, and Stefan Wahl, researchers in Tübingen supported by Anthropic's AI for Science program, and their argument is that a model that merely reproduces the numbers does not yet give you understanding.

**Alex:** They start in nineteen fifty-one, when Cambridge University's computer went out of action for six months, right when Alan Hodgkin and Andrew Huxley had just built equations to describe how a squid nerve cell fires.

**Jordan:** Those electrical pulses underlie everything brains do, from perception to intelligence. Huxley had hoped to use the computer to solve the equations and compare them to real data, and instead he solved them on a hand cranked mechanical calculator, which took him over three weeks.

**Alex:** That slow process matters, because Huxley learned things a fast answer would never have taught him. Hodgkin and Huxley modeled the cell membrane as an electrical circuit, and most of that structure came from known facts about the cell, but one part was an educated guess.

**Jordan:** To make the curve fit their measurements, they had to raise one variable to the fourth power, meaning multiply it by itself three times, and at the time they did not know why. They cautiously suggested the gate in the membrane might need that power because several charged particles had to move into place before the gate would open.

**Alex:** Decades later, long after both men won the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in nineteen sixty-three, another team found a related potassium channel protein with four identical pieces arranged around a central pore. So a model fit to data in nineteen fifty-one had helped predict a physical structure it was never fit to at all.

**Jordan:** That is the difference between an answer and an insight. The authors quote mathematician Richard Hamming, who wrote in nineteen sixty-two that the purpose of computing is insight, not numbers. An AI could plausibly find an equation like theirs in an afternoon, but that alone would not tell you which parts you should actually believe, because many different models can reproduce the same data.

**Alex:** To organize that, the post breaks scientific modeling into three steps that scientists repeat in a loop. The first is building the model, deciding what it is made of and what shape its equations take. The second is constraining it, tuning its adjustable parameters to fit real measurements. And the third is interpreting it, asking what it actually says about the real system.

**Jordan:** They start with constraining, where machines have made the most progress, and they use a dinner bill analogy. Imagine you know a group dinner added up to one hundred twenty dollars, but not who ordered what. Many combinations could produce that total, from everyone ordering a burger, to one person ordering steak and lobster while everyone else had soup.

**Alex:** Nerve models work the same way. Different combinations of parameters can produce nearly identical activity, so one combination that fits hides all the other plausible settings, and those others might lead to different predictions.

**Jordan:** The proper solution is Bayesian inference, which gives you every set of numbers that works reasonably well, as a probability distribution. The best fits sit at the peak, and the weaker ones sit at the fringes, so you can see what the data pin down and what they leave open. The trouble is that for complex models, calculating that directly is usually impractical.

**Alex:** That is where simulation based inference comes in. You pick random parameters, run the model, and record the output, again and again, perhaps one hundred thousand times for a model like Hodgkin and Huxley's, and millions of times for larger ones. Then you train a neural network on those results, so given a real output, it can return the range of parameters that could have produced it.

**Jordan:** Running all those simulations can take weeks, but once it is done, inference on new data takes seconds. The post notes that when two neutron stars collide, this approach can locate the collision fast enough to point telescopes at it while the signal is still detectable, and it has also been used in cosmology, structural biology, and geophysics.

**Alex:** Building the model in the first place is the next frontier. Historically that belonged entirely to human expertise, and the space of possible models is essentially infinite, so a scientist proposing a handful of candidates will very likely miss models that could explain the data equally well.

**Jordan:** Now a language model agent can help with that search. Given data and a plain language description, the agent writes code for a candidate model, runs it, constrains its parameters, compares the output to real data, and revises the equations, repeating that loop until the model replicates the data accurately enough.

**Alex:** But the dinner bill problem returns, one level up. Many different models can explain the same data equally well, and running the agent twice might return two different, equally good models. An agent that returns only one is hiding the rest, just as a single best fit set of numbers does.

**Jordan:** So their recommendation is to ask for the whole range of plausible models. Each candidate is weighted by how much the data support it, models that need a lot of flexibility to fit get less credit, weak candidates drop out, and the system proposes variations on the stronger ones. If every surviving model shares a feature, the data likely favor it, and if they disagree, that shows where the evidence is thin and where the next experiment should aim.

**Alex:** The authors are careful not to oversell this. So far these systems have mostly been tested on rediscovering models scientists already know, and evidence on problems with no known answer is still sparse, although newer language models are getting better at research tasks.

**Jordan:** Then comes interpreting, which the post calls the hardest step to hand over to AI. Hodgkin and Huxley's four particle hypothesis only carried weight because of what they had ruled out. If a simpler model had worked just as well, there would have been no reason to think the gate had several moving parts.

**Alex:** And success is hard to even define here, because we can measure how well a model fits data, but not easily whether it produces understanding. One proposal the post mentions is to judge understanding by how well a system predicts what happens when you disturb the real system, and another is to ask whether its outputs help human scientists understand better.

**Jordan:** A range of models helps with both. If every model that fits the data gives the same answer when you block the sodium gates on a membrane, you can be fairly confident, and if they disagree, that disagreement can suggest the next experiment.

**Alex:** The final section zooms out to scale. Most famous models are compact, with a handful of variables, small enough for a person to reason about, and that is no coincidence, because people had to build, constrain, and interpret them. As AI takes over more of that work, the size limit lifts.

**Jordan:** Brains are their example. The Hodgkin and Huxley equations describe a single nerve cell, but a fruit fly's brain contains more than one hundred thousand neurons, and a human brain contains tens of billions, and it is the interplay between them that gives rise to behavior and thought. For the fruit fly, researchers now have a complete wiring diagram of its brain, and they have started building large models on top of it.

**Alex:** At those scales there is no single correct model to find. The post quotes statistician George Box, who wrote that all models are wrong, but some are useful. So the question becomes which properties all data compatible models share, and which experiments would rule out the greatest number of remaining models. Climate scientists already work this way, reporting projections as the spread across many simulations, and particle physicists do something similar.

**Jordan:** So the closing point is this. Nobody wants to return to fitting curves by eye and hand cranking a calculator for three weeks, but that slow work was not wasted, because much of a model's insight comes from the process of creating it. Hodgkin and Huxley understood their model because they built it themselves and knew what had not worked.

**Alex:** And that is the standard the post sets for AI. A system that does this work in an afternoon still needs to deliver what the slow work produced, which is not just the model that fits, but an account of which parts are pinned down by the data, which are still open, which alternatives failed, and what new puzzles appeared along the way. A model may provide an answer, but the process of constructing it provides the insight. Thanks for listening, and we will be back with the next post from Anthropic.
