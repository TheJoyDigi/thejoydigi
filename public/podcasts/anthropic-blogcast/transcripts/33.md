**Jordan:** And it is not small, either. The post says it is roughly three times the mass of a ribosome, which makes it one of the largest known molecular complexes in complex cells like ours.

Vaults are found across most of the tree of complex life, from slime molds to humans, so scientists believe they must do something useful.

**Jordan:** But nobody actually knows what that something is. That is the part that surprised me most.

**Alex:** That is right. Researchers have proposed that vaults might transport molecules between the nucleus and the rest of the cell, or protect cells from toxic compounds such as cancer drugs, or support innate immunity. None of those ideas has been conclusively confirmed, and mice that lack vaults still survive, which suggests vaults might only be necessary under specific conditions.

**Jordan:** So the obvious question is, why would evolution keep something so large, in so many species, if it did not have an important job?

**Alex:** That is exactly the question Anthropic's biology team set out to chip away at. And here is the headline result. For the first time, they have produced a three dimensional structure of a vault particle found in prokaryotic cells. Prokaryotes are the simpler, usually single celled organisms, like bacteria, as opposed to eukaryotes like us, which have complex cells.

**Jordan:** Why does finding one in a prokaryote matter so much?

**Alex:** Because a vault in a bacterium gives researchers a much simpler system to study. Bacteria are quick to grow, so it is easier to investigate where vaults came from, what their basic structure looks like, and what, if anything, they contain.

**Jordan:** And the specific vault they mapped in detail did not even come from a bacterium itself. It came from a bacteriophage, a virus that infects bacteria.

**Alex:** Right. Since a phage hijacks a bacterial cell to make its proteins, a vault built by a phage is still a vault built inside a prokaryotic cell. This one is built from ninety-six copies of a single protein, which the team calls L zero five, compared with seventy-eight copies in human vaults. Its combined mass is about nine point five megadaltons.

**Jordan:** The shape is different too. It is shorter and wider than the mammalian version, roughly fifty-four nanometers long and forty-five wide, compared with sixty-seven by forty in mammals. **Alex:** Its wall is built from seven repeating segments, rather than the nine in mammalian vaults, and it is among the largest protein shells ever identified in prokaryotes.

Related proteins had been found in prokaryote and phage genomes before, but nobody had shown that they actually assemble into vaults.

**Jordan:** So they chose forty-four candidate proteins and tested each in a cell free system. Claude turned each sequence into D N A tailored to the host cell, added the control sequences that switch a gene on, and prepared the designs for ordering.

**Alex:** Then they moved the best performers into living Escherichia coli bacteria, where fifteen of twenty-four produced soluble protein. Next came a practical problem, how to isolate the particles.

**Jordan:** The conventional method spins the sample through a tube of increasingly dense sugar, and it takes approximately three days. The team wanted to try size exclusion chromatography instead, where the sample flows through a column of porous beads, small molecules get stuck in the pores, and the huge vaults come out first.

**Alex:** And Claude initially argued against that approach, because clumps of misfolded protein come out in that same early fraction. The humans went ahead anyway, and then Claude helped make it work, by recommending a specific column and methods to track the vault protein and check whether particles had formed.

**Jordan:** Claude then proposed something even bolder, loading the contents of ruptured cells straight onto the column with no purification beforehand. The team was reluctant, because those columns are expensive and raw cell contents can clog them, but they tested the unconventional idea and it worked. The optimized method takes about ninety minutes from cells to microscope grid, instead of days.

**Alex:** Under the electron microscope, seven of the fifteen proteins had assembled into vault like particles, and cryo electron microscopy confirmed the vault shape for four of them, two from phages and two from bacteria. They chose the phage protein for the detailed structure because it was among the best at forming complete, closed particles.

**Jordan:** The speed on the structure itself is striking. Determining a biomolecular structure often takes weeks or months of manual work. If that identification is right, it could be an early hint at a possible function.

**Alex:** And they made the vault even bigger. When they copied two of the wall segments, giving the protein nine segments, the same number as the human vault, it assembled into even larger particles, increasing the enclosed volume by approximately twenty percent. That shows how vaults could have changed size over evolutionary history simply by duplicating pieces of their own wall.
