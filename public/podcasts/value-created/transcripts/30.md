**Alex:** Let me tell you about two builders. Both were brilliant. Both worked sixteen-hour days for years. One built the best fax machine accessory money could buy, in 2006, right when fax machines were dying. The other built a small tool that helped people send money to family across borders, slow and steady, for a decade. Both worked just as hard. One ended up with a beautiful product nobody wanted. The other ended up wealthy. The difference was never effort. It was the problem each one chose.

**Jordan:** That is the whole chapter in two sentences. You do not get to choose your talent, not really, and you barely get to choose your luck. But you do get to choose what you build. And that choice, made once, echoes through everything else. Pick the right problem and even mediocre execution carries you. Pick the wrong one and perfect execution just gets you to the wrong destination faster.

**Alex:** So this is about taste? That word makes me nervous. Taste sounds like something you are born with, like perfect pitch. Either you have it or you do not.

**Jordan:** Taste is not magic. It is judgment, trained. A chef's palate is not a gift from the gods, it is ten thousand meals remembered. Judgment works the same way. Every problem you study, every product you take apart, every customer you watch — it all goes into the file. Over time the file gets thick enough that your gut starts whispering before your head catches up. People call that taste. It is really just evidence, digested.

**Alex:** Okay, so how do you train it on purpose? Because most people do not have ten thousand meals. They have a job, a commute, and an idea they are scared to say out loud.

**Jordan:** You study problems the way a jeweler studies stones. Most people fall in love with solutions. They have a clever app idea and go looking for someone who needs it. Flip that. Go live inside a problem first. Find people who wake up thinking about it, who would pay real money for it to disappear. If the problem is big enough and the pain is sharp enough, the solution can be clumsy and it still works. If the pain is fake, no amount of polish saves you.

**Alex:** That reminds me of the distribution chapter. Value nobody sees might as well not exist. Is this the cousin of that? Value nobody needs?

**Jordan:** Exactly. Distribution is how value gets seen. This is how value gets chosen. And here is the trap: the problems that look exciting are usually the crowded ones. Everyone wants to build the next big social app. The good problems look boring from the outside. Freight invoices. Dental scheduling. The plumbing supply chain. The best problems are the ones where the incumbents are asleep and the customers are quietly furious.

**Alex:** But there is a darker version of this, right? Because I have seen people pick huge, painful problems and still flame out. They chose a real monster and it ate them.

**Jordan:** Yes, and that is the part nobody warns you about. A problem can be big and real and still wrong for you. Some problems need capital you do not have. Some need credentials the gatekeepers will never give you. Some take twenty years and you needed it to work in two. Choosing what to build means choosing something you can actually finish. Judgment is not just seeing the mountain. It is knowing whether you brought the right boots.

**Alex:** So how do you check yourself? How do you know you picked a problem worth your life before you spend five years on it?

**Jordan:** Three questions. First, do people already spend money trying to solve this badly? If they pay for a clumsy workaround, they will pay for a good solution. Second, does the problem grow if you ignore it? The best problems are rivers, not puddles — they keep flowing whether you build or not. Third, can you start small and still be useful? If the only version that works costs ten million dollars, it is not your problem. If a scrappy version helps ten people tomorrow, it might be.

**Alex:** And the life part? Picking problems worth your life. That is a heavy phrase.

**Jordan:** It is heavy because it is true. You get a finite number of deep efforts. Maybe five or six real all-in bets in a working life. Each one costs you years you never get back. So the question is not just "is this a good business." The question is "is this worth three years of mornings." If the answer is no, put it down, no matter how clever it is. The cleverest trap in business is a brilliant idea for a problem you do not actually care about, because you will quit at the first hard winter.

**Alex:** Let me land this. Everything in this book has been a multiplier. Ownership, trade, money, markets, leverage, networks, compounding. But a multiplier times zero is still zero. Choosing what to build is the number you multiply everything by. Pick well, and every tool in this book works for you. Pick badly, and you just get faster at going nowhere.

**Jordan:** That is the takeaway. Taste is trained judgment. Judgment picks problems. And problems are the one thing in this whole book you cannot outsource, cannot automate, cannot delegate. The machines will do the building. Only you can choose what is worth building. So choose like your life depends on it. It does.
