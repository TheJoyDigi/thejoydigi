**Alex:** In eighteen thirty nine, a hardware store owner from Connecticut named Charles Goodyear had an obsession. Rubber, the tree sap the world was excited about, melted in summer and froze in winter, and Goodyear believed he could fix it. For five years he experimented in a borrowed laboratory, then in his kitchen, then in debtors' prison, twice. He sold his children's schoolbooks to buy chemicals. Creditors seized his furniture while his family went hungry. And then, in a legendary accident, he dropped a mixture of rubber and sulfur onto a hot stove, and it did not melt. It vulcanized. He had invented the process that made the modern rubber industry possible, the process behind every tire, every seal, every rubber sole on earth. And Charles Goodyear died penniless. He owed two hundred thousand dollars when he died, a fortune in his time, and he never saw a cent of the rubber billions. Nearly forty years later, a tire company took his name, Goodyear, and stamped it on factories around the world. The most famous name in rubber belonged to a man the rubber industry never paid.

**Jordan:** I am Jordan.

**Alex:** I am Alex. This is Value, Created. Chapter fifteen: Creating Isn't Enough.

**Jordan:** Here is the sentence this whole chapter hangs on. Creating value and capturing value are two different games, with two different rulebooks. Creating is making something people want: the invention, the recipe, the song, the software. Capturing is arranging the world so the money flows back to you: the contract, the brand, the distribution, the price. Goodyear won the first game completely and lost the second game completely. And he is not the exception. He is the pattern. The people who make the things are very often not the people who get rich from them. Because the market does not pay you for creating value. It pays you for capturing it.

**Alex:** Nobody illustrates the two games better than the summer of nineteen eighty. IBM was building its first personal computer, and it needed an operating system. The obvious choice was C P M, made by a company called Digital Research, run by a brilliant programmer named Gary Kildall. C P M ran on nearly every small computer in America. IBM's executives flew out to meet him. Legend says Kildall went flying that day instead, and his lawyer argued with IBM over a nondisclosure agreement, and IBM walked away. They walked across the street, almost literally, to a tiny software shop called Microsoft, run by a twenty four year old named Bill Gates. Microsoft had no operating system. So Gates bought one, a rough program called Q D O S, from a small Seattle company, for fifty thousand dollars. Then he licensed it to IBM, but with one masterstroke buried in the contract: Microsoft kept the right to sell it to anyone else. When the clone makers arrived, every machine ran Microsoft's system. Kildall made the better product. Gates made the better deal. The best product did not win. The best position won.

**Jordan:** Once you see the pattern, it is everywhere. The research lab at Xerox invented the graphical interface, the mouse, and the laser printer, the building blocks of the modern computer, and Xerox captured almost none of it, while Apple and Microsoft turned those ideas into fortunes. The farmer who grows your coffee captures pennies per cup. The cafe on the corner captures five dollars, for the cup, the chair, and the logo. The app developer does the creating. The app store takes thirty percent for owning the shelf. None of this is a conspiracy. It is physics. Value flows to the scarcest piece of the chain. If ten thousand people can make it, but only one can sell it, the seller eats. Creation is abundant. Capture is scarce. And the scarce piece sets the price.

**Alex:** This is the second law of the chapter. Follow the bottleneck. In every chain, from raw material to customer, there is one step that is hard to replace: the patent, the brand, the distribution network, the relationship with the buyer, the one machine nobody else owns. Whoever controls the bottleneck captures the margin, and everyone else splits the scraps. Goodyear had no bottleneck. His process could be copied, his patents were tied up in court, and he had no factory, no brand, no customers of his own. He created enormous value and captured none of it, because there was nothing about his position that the world had to pay for. So here is the question to ask about everything you make: who captures this, and is it me? If the honest answer is someone else, you do not have a business yet. You have a donation.

**Jordan:** Which brings us to the practical part. Three rules for capturing what you create. Rule one: own the scarce piece. Before you build anything, look at the whole chain and ask what will be hard to replace. If the answer is nothing, stop, and change the plan. The best time to build a moat is before you need one: a patent filed early, a brand people remember, a customer list that is yours, a distribution channel you control. Tesla understood this. The batteries were not the scarce piece. The charging network was, the Supercharger stations nobody else had, so Tesla built those first. Apple understood this. The phone was not the scarce piece. The app store was. Do not ask how good your product is. Ask what part of your chain the world cannot route around. Then own that part.

**Alex:** Rule two: get it in writing, before it works. Goodyear spent his last years in court, fighting over patents while his creditors circled. Kildall's deal with IBM died over a nondisclosure agreement nobody would sign. Robert Kearns invented the intermittent windshield wiper, watched the car companies take it, and spent decades suing them to get paid. The pattern is always the same: the deal gets made when the value is small and everyone is friendly, or it gets fought over when the value is huge and nobody is friendly. Amateurs negotiate after the value exists. Professionals negotiate before. Who owns what, who gets paid how much, what happens if it works: decide it in writing while it is cheap to decide. The handshake is not the deal. The contract is the product.

**Jordan:** Rule three: build the business, not just the product. Most creators spend ninety percent of their energy on the thing, and ten percent on everything that captures its value: the pricing, the selling, the distribution, the brand. Flip the ratio. A mediocre product with great distribution beats a great product with no distribution, every single time, because the customer cannot buy what they never see. The iPod was not the first M P three player. It was the first one with a store, a brand, and an ad campaign. So when you finish making something, your work is half done, at best. The second half is pricing it, placing it, and telling the world it exists. Which is exactly where we are going next.

**Alex:** Here is the takeaway. Creating is only half the battle. The value you create is a seed, and capture is the harvest, and a seed nobody harvests feeds someone else. Goodyear vulcanized rubber and died in debt. Kildall built the operating system era and watched it from the sidelines. The difference between them and the people who got rich was never talent, and it was never the quality of the work. It was position, paperwork, and the scarce piece. So honor your creations enough to capture them. Ask of everything you make: who captures this, and is it me? Make sure the answer is you.
