**Alex:** In 1844, Samuel Morse tapped out the first telegraph message, from Washington to Baltimore. It was a marvel of the age. Copper wire, electromagnetic clicks, words traveling faster than any horse. And for a while, it connected exactly two places on Earth. The machine was extraordinary. The network was worth almost nothing, because there was almost nobody on it. A few decades later, the first fax machine sat in an office like a paperweight. Then the second fax machine was sold, and the first one turned into a business. That is the subject of this chapter: products that get more valuable the more people use them. And the strange economics underneath, where the users are the ones manufacturing the value.

**Jordan:** Here is the sentence this chapter hangs on. A product's value is not just what it does. It is who else is on it. Every new user of a network does not add one unit of value. They add value to every user who was already there. The hundredth person to join makes the previous ninety-nine richer, just by showing up. That is why networks feel like they appear out of nowhere and then feel inevitable. The value was being built by the crowd, quietly, one arrival at a time.

**Alex:** Let's start where it started. When Alexander Graham Bell demonstrated the telephone, the most common reaction from investors was polite dismissal. It was a toy for the rich, they said. A curiosity for people with money to burn. And they were staring at the device, which meant they were staring at the wrong thing. The device never mattered. What mattered was that within a few decades, a telephone in your house meant you could reach anyone who also had one. Bell's company did not really sell mouthpieces and earpieces. It sold the size of the crowd. Every new subscriber was a gift to all the existing ones.

**Jordan:** That is the first rule of networks. Value lives in the connections, not in the nodes. A telephone with one subscriber is a sculpture. A telephone with a million subscribers is a necessity. The same machine. Completely different value. And here is the part most people miss: the price of the product can stay fixed while its value keeps rising, because the users are doing the manufacturing. Nobody at the phone company had to invent a new feature to make your phone more valuable. Your neighbors joining did it for free.

**Alex:** Watch what happened with the fax machine, because it makes the mechanism visible. The company that built the first commercial fax machines nearly gave up. They sold a handful of units, and the buyers complained. Not because the machine was bad. Because there was nobody to fax. Then a few large companies bought the machines in bulk, for use inside their own offices. And suddenly, every fax machine that had ever been sold became more useful, including the ones sitting in offices that had already given up on theirs. No software update. No new feature. No price change. Just more people on the network, and the old machines woke up like they had been plugged in for the first time. This is the moment network builders live for. The product you already sold gets better after the sale, and you did not lift a finger.

**Jordan:** And once you see it, you see it everywhere. A marketplace with ten sellers is a ghost town. A marketplace with ten thousand sellers is a destination, and every buyer who arrives makes it more attractive to the next seller, who makes it more attractive to the next buyer. The flywheel spins both ways. A language nobody speaks is trivia. A language a billion people speak is a career. The value was never in the dictionary. It was in the speakers.

**Jordan:** Now here is the trap, and it is the part the cheerleaders leave out. Networks have a dark mirror. The same mechanism that makes them valuable makes them fragile. Every new user adds value, but only if they add signal. When the new arrivals bring noise, spam, scams, or cruelty, the network gets bigger and worse at the same time. More users, less value. A network is a commons. It rewards the builder who protects it and quietly punishes the one who just counts heads. The platforms that last are the ones that learned to defend the conversation, not just grow the crowd.

**Alex:** There is a second story here, and it is about starting, because every network begins as two paperweights. The fax company solved it by selling to big companies first, so each buyer was really buying a small network, not a single machine. The telephone companies ran their wires down the busiest streets first, where each new subscriber could immediately reach the most people. The lesson is always the same. Do not seed a network where people are scarce. Seed it where people are already gathered, so the very first users feel the value on day one, and they bring the second wave with them.

**Jordan:** So the practical part. Three rules, whether you are building something or choosing something to join. First, build for the second user, not the first. If your product is only useful when other people join, then your real product is the invitation, the reason to bring someone along. Second, seed where people gather. The cold start is the whole game. Borrow someone else's crowd before you try to build your own. Third, protect the signal. The moment the network fills with noise, the value drains out of every single connection, and no feature, no redesign, no marketing budget will put it back.

**Alex:** So the takeaway is simple. The most valuable thing you can build is not a product people use alone. It is a place people bring each other. Every user who arrives makes it better for everyone who is already there. That is the compounding engine of networks. And it is why the winners look inevitable in hindsight. They were not inevitable. They were just the ones who understood, early, that value lives in the crowd, and they built accordingly.
