**Alex:** In the nineteen fifties, a market vendor in Paris sat down for lunch at a cafe, and noticed Pablo Picasso doodling on a napkin at the next table. The man asked Picasso to draw something for him, right there, on the napkin. Picasso did. It took him maybe thirty seconds. Then he folded the napkin, slid it across the table, and said, that will be ten thousand francs. The man laughed. Ten thousand francs, he said, for thirty seconds of work? And Picasso answered: no. Forty years and thirty seconds. That napkin had a price and it had a value, and the price had nothing to do with the thirty seconds. Most of us price like the man at the table. We count our costs, add a little margin, and call that the price. Picasso priced like Picasso. He charged for the forty years.

**Alex:** I am Alex. This is Value, Created. Chapter sixteen: Pricing, Naming Your Worth.

**Jordan:** Here is the sentence this chapter hangs on. Your costs decide what you must charge. The buyer's value decides what you can charge. And the distance between those two numbers is where fortunes are made or lost. Almost every beginner prices backward. They add up their time and their materials, tack on a polite margin, and hope the customer agrees. That is called cost-plus pricing, and it is the most expensive mistake in business, because it has nothing to do with the customer. The customer never asked what it cost you. The customer is asking one question: what is this worth to me? And the answer to that question sets the ceiling on your price. Your costs set the floor. The ceiling is set by the buyer. Most businesses live on the floor when they could be living near the ceiling.

**Alex:** Let us see the two pricing worlds side by side. In the cost-plus world, you are a plumber who spends two hours fixing a burst pipe, so you charge for two hours plus parts, maybe three hundred dollars. The homeowner, who was imagining a flooded kitchen and a ruined floor, would have happily paid eight hundred. You just donated five hundred dollars to a stranger. In the value-based world, you are the same plumber, and you quote the job by the problem it solves: the kitchen stays dry, the floor stays intact, dinner is on time. You charge six hundred, the homeowner feels relieved, and you go home with twice the money for the same two hours. Nothing about the work changed. Only the price did. And notice the deeper truth hiding inside that story. When you charge by cost, you are telling the customer that your work is a commodity, interchangeable hours. When you charge by value, you are telling the customer what they are actually buying: the outcome.

**Jordan:** This is why luxury goods break people's brains. A handbag costs maybe two hundred dollars to make and sells for three thousand. Critics call that a rip-off, and they are missing the point completely. The buyer is not purchasing stitched leather. She is purchasing the feeling of arrival, the signal, the craft story, the forty years. The bag's value lives in her, the beholder, just like we learned back in chapter two. Cost-plus says the bag should sell for two hundred and fifty dollars. Value-based says the bag sells for whatever the beholder's eye sees. The company that understands this keeps the margin. The company that does not gives it away and calls itself honest. And here is the part nobody wants to hear. If you refuse to charge for value, the market will not reward you with gratitude. It will simply assume you are worth less.

**Alex:** There is a famous experiment that proves how deep this goes. A wine shop put two bottles on the shelf, identical wine, one priced at ten dollars and one at forty. Almost everyone bought the forty dollar bottle, and rated it better. Same wine. The price changed the taste. Economists call this the price-quality signal: when people cannot judge quality directly, they use price as the judge. So your price is not just a number. It is a sentence about your worth, spoken to the customer's subconscious. Price too low and you are telling the world you are cheap. Price at the level of the value you deliver and you are telling the world what you actually are. The market listens to that sentence more carefully than it listens to your marketing.

**Jordan:** Which brings us to the practical part. Three rules for naming your worth. Rule one: price the outcome, not the input. Before you name a number, write down the problem you solve in the customer's words, then write down what that problem costs them if it stays unsolved. A consultant who saves a company a million dollars a year in wasted software should not charge by the hour. She should charge a fraction of the million. A photographer who shoots the wedding should not charge by the shutter click. She should charge for the only day that can never be repeated. Find the customer's pain in dollars, price against that, and your quote will look like a bargain even when it is ten times your old rate.

**Alex:** Rule two: offer tiers, and let the middle win. Put three options on the table: a basic version, a standard version, and a premium version. Almost everyone picks the middle one, and the premium makes the middle look reasonable. This is called anchoring, and it is not a trick, it is clarity. The customer does not know what a fair price is until you give them a frame. A single price is a guess. Three prices are a menu, and menus let people choose their own value. The freelancer who used to sell one website for two thousand dollars now sells three packages, at fifteen hundred, three thousand, and six thousand. Same clients, same work, and most of them choose the middle. Her income nearly doubled, and the clients are happier, because they chose.

**Jordan:** Rule three: raise your prices until someone says no. This is the rule everyone fears and nobody regrets. If every quote you send gets accepted, your prices are too low. That is not success. That is a leak. The market is telling you that you left money on the table, and the only way to find the ceiling is to touch it. So raise your rates, ten percent, twenty percent, and watch. Some clients will walk, and that is the point: the ones who stay are paying you closer to what you are worth, and you will work fewer hours for more money. A pricing consultant named this the courage tax, except it runs in reverse. The tax is paid by people who never raise their prices. They pay it in exhaustion, in resentment, in the quiet knowledge that they are worth more than their invoice says.

**Alex:** And there is one more move, the simplest one in the chapter. Stop apologizing for your price. The moment you discount before being asked, you have confessed that even you do not believe the number. State the price like Picasso stated his: calmly, as a fact about the world. Forty years and thirty seconds. The customer can say yes or no. What they must not hear is you flinching. Because here is the takeaway. Pricing is not arithmetic. It is autobiography. Every number you name is you telling the world what you believe you are worth, and the world, being polite, will believe you. Name it low and you train everyone, including yourself, to expect little. Name it at the level of the value you create and something remarkable happens. The right customers appear, the wrong ones leave, and the work you were already doing finally pays what it was always worth.
