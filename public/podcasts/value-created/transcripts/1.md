**Jordan:** Alright, let us go back to Adam Smith's riddle, because the trap inside it is the entire lesson. The trap is assuming that value lives inside things. That a diamond has value the way it has weight, like it is a physical property baked into the rock itself.

**Alex:** It feels natural to think that way. A diamond is rare, it is hard, it sparkles. It feels like those qualities are what make it valuable. Water is everywhere, it is plain, it falls from the sky. So it feels cheap. The value feels like it is in the object.

**Jordan:** So value cannot be inside the thing. If it were, it would not move around like that. The diamond did not get less diamondy in the desert. The water did not get more watery. The only thing that changed was the human being, and what that human being needed in that moment.

**Alex:** That is the big idea of this chapter, and maybe of this whole book. Value is not in things. It is in people.

**Jordan:** So if value lives in people and not in things, the next question is obvious. What makes something valuable to a person? Let us build it with three ingredients. **Alex:** A glass of water has enormous value to the thirsty person because it ends their suffering. It has almost no value to someone who just drank a full bottle, because there is nothing left for it to do. Same water. Different person, different moment, different value.

**Jordan:** You can feel this in your own life with the most ordinary objects. Think about your phone charger. Sitting in a drawer at home, it is worth almost nothing to you. You changed.

**Alex:** The second ingredient is scarcity. And I do not mean how rare something is in the universe. I mean how hard it is for this person, right here, right now, to get it.

**Jordan:** Water in your kitchen is nearly free because it is abundant and effortless. Water in the desert is priceless because it is nearly impossible to find. The water did not change. Its availability to you changed.

**Alex:** And diamonds are the mirror image. A diamond is valuable in a jewelry store, and not because it is useful. It is valuable because it is scarce and because people desire it. Desire plus scarcity. Take away either one and the value collapses.

**Jordan:** Think about that. If diamonds were as common as gravel, nobody would pay a fortune for them, no matter how beautifully they sparkle. You need both, meeting in a person.

**Alex:** The third ingredient is context, the situation, the moment. An umbrella is worth a fortune in a downpour and worth nothing on a sunny day. Same umbrella. A warm meal is precious to someone who is hungry and barely interesting to someone who just ate. The thing stays the same. The context moves the value.

**Jordan:** There is your recipe. Desire, what it does for the person. Scarcity, how hard it is for them to get. Context, the situation they are in. Move any one of those three, and the value moves, even though the object never changed at all.
