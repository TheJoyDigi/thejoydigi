**Alex:** The story starts on a chessboard. A king in ancient India, delighted by the new game of chess, told its inventor to name his reward. The inventor asked for something modest. A single grain of rice on the first square, then double it on every square after, all the way to the sixty-fourth. The king laughed. Rice was cheap. Then the royal accountants started counting. The first half of the board was nothing. A few handfuls. But the second half emptied the granaries, then the treasury, then the kingdom. He owed the inventor more rice than the country could ever grow. The king had been tricked, not by the inventor, but by the flat beginning of an exponential curve. The first thirty-two squares asked for almost nothing. The last three squares asked for almost everything. That is this chapter in a single image. Value, doubling quietly, until it does something nobody believed was possible.

**Jordan:** Here is the sentence this chapter hangs on. Compounding is time, turned into a multiplier. Everything in this book so far has been about creating value once. Compounding is what happens when value starts creating value. Interest earns interest. A skill attracts opportunities, and every opportunity sharpens the skill. A reputation opens doors, and every open door adds to the reputation. The growth rate matters. But the time horizon is the whole game. A good rate over a long time beats a great rate over a short one. Every time.

**Alex:** The most famous compounding story ever told is Warren Buffett's, and he tells it on himself. He bought his first stock at eleven years old. And he has said his whole life has been a product of compounding, quote, the building of a snowball. But here is the part people always skip. He always adds that the snow was wettest in the earliest years. The snowball had decades of hill ahead of it. A dollar put to work early was not one dollar. It was every doubling that came after. Time was not a background detail of the story. Time was the story. Ninety-nine percent of his fortune arrived after his fiftieth birthday. The decades did the heavy lifting.

**Jordan:** Money is only the most famous snowball. Here is the one most people live inside without naming it. Picture a student who reads for one hour a day, every day. At first, nothing seems to change. The hours feel invisible. Then the vocabulary compounds. Then the reading gets faster, which means more reading per hour, which compounds again. Three years later, she is not a little sharper. She is unrecognizably sharper. Because knowledge compounds exactly like money. Each new idea earns interest on every idea it connects to. The first hundred hours are the boring part. Everything after is the magic part.

**Alex:** So why does almost nobody live like this? Because the early curve of compounding is flat, and flat looks like nothing. The first half of the chessboard is boring. Sixteen grains here, thirty-two there. The human brain sees the flat part and concludes the whole thing is small. Scientists call it exponential growth bias. We are wired to draw straight lines into the future, so a curve that stays flat and then explodes feels like a trick. But here is the deeper reason, the one that belongs in this book. Compounding requires you to leave your winnings on the table. You have to not cash out. Every withdrawal murders a little of the future. And humans are spectacularly bad at not cashing out. Here is the math the brain refuses to feel. Something growing at ten percent a year doubles every seven years. Seven years feels like forever, so nobody starts. But seven years, four times, is a sixteenfold return in twenty-eight years. The rate was never the impressive part. The patience was.

**Jordan:** That is why the practical part of this chapter is not about finding a higher rate. It is about protecting the time. Three rules. Rule one: start early, and if you cannot start early, start now. The first year of a snowball is worth more than the tenth, because it gets doubled the most times. Rule two: never interrupt it. Every interruption resets the most powerful part of the curve. Patience is not a virtue here. It is the strategy. Rule three: pick snowballs that do not melt. Your skills compound. Your reputation compounds. Your relationships compound. Trust compounds as well. Debt compounds too, only in the wrong direction. The question is not how hard you push. The question is what you let time multiply. Charlie Munger, Buffett's partner for sixty years, put the whole thing in one sentence. The first rule of compounding is to never interrupt it unnecessarily. Notice he did not say find the highest return. He said protect the streak. A twenty percent return that survives beats a thirty percent return that gets cashed out, spent, and restarted. The interruption is always more expensive than it looks, because you do not just lose the money. You lose the doublings the money would have bought.

**Alex:** Let me make that question concrete, because it is the question this chapter exists to ask. Look at your own week. Somewhere in it is something you do once and it is gone.
