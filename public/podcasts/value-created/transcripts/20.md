**Alex:** In 1976, three men started a company in a garage in California. Two of them you have heard of. The third was named Ronald Wayne. He was forty-one, the adult in the room, and twelve days later he sold his ten percent of the new company back to the other two for eight hundred dollars. Eight hundred dollars for ten percent of Apple. If he had kept it, that stake would be worth hundreds of billions today. Now the easy lesson is never sell. That is not the lesson, because Wayne was not stupid. He was scared, and he had reason to be. The company had debts, the other two were twenty-somethings with no money, and if it failed, the creditors could come after him personally. He traded a claim on the future for safety in the present, and almost everyone would have done the same. But look at what the trade reveals. That ten percent was not eight hundred dollars of work. It was a claim, a legal right, to one tenth of everything Apple would ever create, forever. Wages pay you for the hours you already worked. Ownership pays you for everything that happens next. That is this whole chapter in one story.

**Jordan:** I am Jordan.

**Alex:** I am Alex. This is Value, Created. Chapter twenty: Ownership.

**Jordan:** Here is the sentence this chapter hangs on. Workers rent out their time. Owners own the asset. When you work for a wage, you sell hours, and the deal ends when the hours end. When you own equity, you hold a claim on all the future value the thing creates, whether you are in the room or not. That is why owners keep what workers create. It is not a conspiracy and it is not a moral judgment. It is the structure of the deal. Think of it as two different contracts. The wage contract says, I will pay you for your past. The ownership contract says, I own a share of your future. The worker's contribution has an expiration date. The owner's claim does not.

**Alex:** Watch it happen with two engineers, same skill, same startup, same year. Maya takes the safe offer. One hundred sixty thousand a year, salary, benefits, no drama. Tom takes one hundred ten thousand plus half a percent of the company. For five years they sit ten feet apart and do roughly the same work. Then the company sells for four hundred million dollars. Maya walks away with eight hundred thousand dollars in wages, and she earned every cent. Tom walks away with five hundred fifty thousand in wages plus two million in equity. Two point five five million dollars. Same work. Same years. Three times the money. The difference was not talent and it was not hours. Maya sold her time. Tom bought a piece of the future. And here is the part people skip, because it makes the story less comfortable. Tom took the risk. If the company had died, and most of them do, Maya keeps her eight hundred thousand and Tom gets almost nothing. Owners keep the upside because owners absorb the downside. That is the trade at the heart of every equity deal. The wage is a floor under your life. Equity is a ceiling that does not exist, but the floor comes with it. You cannot have the upside without standing where the downside can hit you.

**Jordan:** And it is not just startups. Once you see the pattern you see it everywhere. The tenant pays rent every month for ten years and owns nothing at the end. The landlord collects the rent and owns the building, which is now worth twice what it was. Same building. The tenant funded it. The owner kept it. The bestselling author gets fifteen percent royalties. The publisher keeps the rest, on every copy, forever. The musician gets the advance. The label owns the masters. In every case the worker creates the value and the owner keeps the larger share, because the owner holds the asset, and the asset is what compounds. Wages do not compound. You cannot reinvest last year's salary into this year's salary. But assets compound. A business, a building, a brand, a share of a company, they grow while you sleep. We talked about compounding with brand in the last chapter. Ownership is compounding with a legal contract attached. And notice who takes the risk in each of these stories. The landlord is on the hook when the roof leaks. The publisher gambles on ten books to find one hit. The label fronts the recording cost. The owner keeps more because the owner can lose more. That does not make it fair. It makes it structural.

**Alex:** So what does a worker actually do about it. You do not have to quit your job and found a startup tomorrow. Ownership is a ladder, and the first rung is small. Rung one, buy equity where you already work. If your company offers stock, options, or an employee purchase plan, take it seriously. It is the cheapest ownership you will ever buy, because you already understand the business from the inside. Most people ignore the purchase plan and then buy a stranger's stock at full price. Do not be most people. Rung two, negotiate for a piece instead of just a raise. A ten thousand dollar raise is ten thousand dollars, once. A tenth of a percent of a growing company can pay you ten thousand dollars a year for as long as it grows. When you are offered a choice between cash now and a piece of the future, at least do the math before you take the cash. Rung three, convert wages into assets on your own time. The index fund is the simplest version. A rental property is the classic version. A small business, a book, a piece of software that sells while you sleep, those are the ambitious versions. The rule is simple. Every year, turn some of what you earned by working into something that earns without you working. And rung four, the one most people never climb, build something you own from the start. Remember Marcus, the painter with the checklists from two chapters ago. The checklists made the business run without him. But the deeper move was that he owned the business the checklists were running. The system was the engine. Ownership was the title deed. Start this week. Pick the lowest rung you have not climbed and take one step. The ladder only works if you get on it.

**Jordan:** So the takeaway is simple, and it is a little uncomfortable. Every dollar of value created flows somewhere, and the address it flows to is decided by ownership, not by effort. The hardest worker in the building does not keep the most. The owner does. That is not a moral claim. It is plumbing. And plumbing does not care how you feel about it. So the question is not whether you work hard. You probably do. The question is what you own. If the answer is nothing, your income stops the day you stop, and everything you built keeps paying someone else. The workers will always create the value. Make sure you are standing on the side that gets to keep it.
