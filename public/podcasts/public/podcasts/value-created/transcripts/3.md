**Alex:** Picture this. It is two in the morning. You are lost in the mountains. The temperature is dropping past freezing, the wind is cutting straight through your jacket, and you have started shivering in a way that honestly scares you.

**Jordan:** Ahead of you, sitting on a flat rock, someone has left two things. A thick, warm winter coat. And a stock certificate for a company worth a hundred million dollars. You can only carry one. Which do you take?

**Alex:** You take the coat. Instantly. No hesitation, no spreadsheet, no second opinion. On that mountain, on that night, the coat is worth more to you than a hundred million dollars. It might literally save your life.

**Jordan:** Now rewind. Same two objects. But you are sitting in a warm office in July, and a buyer walks through the door. The coat? Maybe ten dollars at a garage sale. The stock certificate? Still a hundred million dollars. Nothing about the objects changed. Everything about their value changed.

**Alex:** That is the riddle of this chapter. Every valuable thing has two faces. Use value, and exchange value. And confusing the two will cost you money, and sometimes much more than money.

**Jordan:** I am Jordan.

**Alex:** And I am Alex. This is Value, Created. Chapter three: Two Faces, Use versus Exchange.

**Alex:** Let us name the faces. Use value is what a thing does for you directly. The coat keeps you warm. Bread feeds you. A hammer drives nails. A song you love steadies your nerves before the big meeting. Use value is personal. It lives in the relationship between you and the thing, in one specific moment.

**Jordan:** Exchange value is what a thing can be traded for. It is the price the market will pay. The stock certificate never kept anyone warm, never fed anyone, never sheltered anyone. Its use value is a piece of paper. But its exchange value is enormous, because of what other people will trade for what it represents.

**Alex:** Here is chapter one, still true. Value lives in people, not in things. Both faces are still about people. Use value is what it does for you. Exchange value is what it does for someone else, someone willing to trade with you.

**Jordan:** And here is chapter two, knocking on the door. Remember the margin. The coat's use value is not one fixed number stamped on the label. On the freezing mountain, it is priceless. On a mild spring afternoon, it is clutter in your closet. Same coat. The beholder changed, the moment changed, so the use value changed.

**Alex:** Exactly. Use value is always marginal. It depends on your situation right now. Exchange value, though, looks steady. The certificate reads a hundred million whether you are freezing or comfortable. That steadiness is useful. You can plan a life around it. Mortgages, savings, contracts all run on exchange value holding still long enough to trust.

**Jordan:** Useful, yes. But also dangerous. Because that steady number starts to feel like the real value, the true value, the only value. And the moment you believe that, you start making the expensive mistakes. Let us walk through them.

**Alex:** Mistake number one. The pawn shop. Your grandmother passes away, and she leaves you her ring. To you, it is irreplaceable. You watched her wear it every Sunday of your childhood. Its use value to you, its meaning value, is enormous.

**Jordan:** Then hard times hit. You carry it to a pawn shop. The man behind the glass weighs the gold, checks the stone under his little microscope, and offers you two hundred dollars. Two hundred dollars for the most precious object you own.

**Alex:** Are you being robbed? No. You are watching the two faces split apart in real time. The ring's use value to you is sky high. Its exchange value is whatever a stranger will pay for gold and a stone. The pawnbroker is not buying your memories. He cannot resell your memories. He is buying metal.

**Jordan:** The cost of confusion here is heartbreak and bad decisions that echo for years. People sell the ring for two hundred dollars and feel cheated every time they think about it. Or they swing the other way. They refuse to sell anything, ever, and slowly drown in objects they cannot use, because they priced their memories at exchange value inside their own heads.

**Alex:** The rule is simple to say and hard to live. Never sell something with high use value at its exchange price unless you truly have to. And never expect the market to pay you for your feelings. The market does not have feelings. It only has bids.

**Jordan:** Mistake number two. Status goods. The designer handbag. The luxury watch. The car with the badge on the front.

**Alex:** Ask the buyer why they bought it and listen to the words. Craftsmanship. Quality. How long it lasts. Attention to detail. They are speaking pure use value. They are telling themselves a use story.

**Jordan:** Now watch what they actually bought. They bought what it signals. They bought the exchange face, the social price tag, the message it sends to everyone who sees it. The use story is just the costume the exchange motive wears to feel respectable.

**Alex:** Quick test, and be honest with yourself. Would you still buy it if no one would ever see you own it? If the answer is no, you were never buying the craftsmanship. You were buying the signal. There is nothing evil about buying signals. We all do it. The cost comes from lying to yourself about which face you paid for.

**Jordan:** Because then you pay the exchange price, ten times the use value, and you are genuinely angry that it does not make you happy. Of course it does not. You bought a signal and expected a coat. Wrong face, wrong expectation, real disappointment.

**Alex:** Mistake number three. Businesses make it from the other side of the counter, and it quietly kills them. Picture a bakery. It costs three dollars to bake a beautiful loaf of bread. The owner thinks, my cost is three dollars, so five dollars is a fair price. Cost-plus pricing. It feels honest. It feels decent.

**Jordan:** But the customer does not care about your cost. The customer was never shown your cost. The customer cares about the use value to them. A warm loaf on a cold morning. The smell filling the kitchen. Breakfast for the family they love, unhurried, together. That experience might be worth fifteen dollars to them.

**Alex:** Price on cost, and you leave ten dollars of value on the table with every single loaf. Worse, a competitor who understands use value charges twelve dollars, makes the customer happier than you did, and still earns triple your margin. You were not being honest. You were being blind. You priced the wrong face.

**Jordan:** Now flip it, because the same mistake runs in reverse. A software company builds a tool that saves a business a hundred thousand dollars a year. It costs them almost nothing to serve one more customer. If they price on cost, they charge twenty dollars a month and slowly starve while creating fortunes for other people.

**Alex:** Price on the use value delivered, and they charge two thousand a month, and the customer thanks them for it. Same product. Different face. Ten times the business. The cost was never the point. The cost is just what you spent. The value is what they received.

**Jordan:** Mistake number four. Investors confuse the faces, and this one costs people their retirements. You fall in love with a company. You love the product. You use it every single day. The founder is charismatic, the mission moves you, the commercials make you feel something. So you buy the stock with your heart full.

**Alex:** You just bought a use feeling. But a stock is not a product. A stock has no use value. You cannot eat it, wear it, drive it, or live inside it. A stock is pure exchange value. It is a claim on future cash, nothing more, nothing less.

**Jordan:** And loving the product tells you exactly nothing about the cash flows. Some of the most beloved companies in history were terrible investments. Some of the most boring companies on earth made their shareholders rich. The product is the coat. The stock is the certificate. Never, ever confuse them.

**Alex:** Keep that sentence somewhere you will see it. The product is the coat. The stock is the certificate. Tattoo it on the inside of your eyelids if you invest.

**Jordan:** So the faces are different, and mixing them up is expensive. But here is the beautiful part, the part that makes this a chapter about creating value and not just avoiding mistakes. The gap between the two faces is where every business lives.

**Alex:** Say it slowly, because this is the engine of the whole book. Create use value. Capture exchange value. The gap between the two is where businesses live.

**Jordan:** Think about a great restaurant. The use value of a perfect evening is enormous. Warmth, flavor, laughter with people you love, a memory you will carry for years. Now ask what it cost the restaurant to create that evening. A fraction of the bill. A small fraction.

**Alex:** The customer gladly pays the exchange price, because the use value dwarfs it. They would pay it again tomorrow. The restaurant keeps the gap. That gap is profit. That gap is the business. No gap, no business. It really is that simple, and that hard.

**Jordan:** This is why the best businesses obsess over use value and price on exchange value. They pour everything into making the thing genuinely useful, genuinely delightful, genuinely worth far more to the customer than the price on the menu. Then they charge what the market will bear, and everyone walks away feeling like they won.

**Alex:** And the worst businesses do the exact opposite. They pour everything into the exchange face. The branding, the packaging, the signal, the unboxing experience, the influencer campaign. Meanwhile the use value is hollow. Now, signals sell, so it works for a while. But hollow use value always gets found out. Always. The reviews come. The refunds come. The silence comes.

**Jordan:** And you start building in the gap. Create more use value than you capture in exchange value, and customers will beat a path to your door. That is not a slogan for a poster. That is the arithmetic of every business that has ever lasted more than a season.

**Alex:** Every lasting business you admire lives in that gap. The restaurant, the toolmaker, the teacher, the software company that charges two thousand and gets thanked for it.
