**Alex:** In the mid two thousands, an online retailer had a mystery on its hands. Shoppers were filling their carts, clicking checkout, and vanishing. The prices were fine. The site worked. But somewhere between buy and bought, a river of money was disappearing into thin air. A researcher named Jared Spool was called in to find out why, and he did what good researchers do: he sat down next to real shoppers and watched. Within minutes, he found the leak. It was a single button on the checkout page, and it said one word: Register. Before you could spend a dollar, the site demanded that you create an account. User name, password, password again. Shoppers froze. Some thought it was a commitment they did not want. Some tried to log in, failed, and gave up. And here is the frightening part: the button was not broken. It worked exactly as designed. It asked for one small thing at exactly the wrong moment.

**Jordan:** Spool's team changed almost nothing. They replaced the word Register with the word Continue. They added one line: you do not need to create an account to shop. The account became optional, at the very end. No redesign. No sale. No new features. Purchases jumped forty five percent. Fifteen million dollars in new sales in the first month. Three hundred million in the first year. They called it the three hundred million dollar button, and it is the most expensive lesson ever learned about a single screen. One demand, at the wrong moment, had been standing between the company and a fortune. The money was always there. The friction was hiding it.

**Alex:** That is our subject today. Friction. The waits, the clicks, the forms, the fine print, the seventeen taps to do the thing you already decided to do. Every one of them is a small tax, and your customer pays it in the scarcest currency they own: their patience. And here is the promise of this chapter: remove the friction, and you do not just make people happier. You unlock value that was already sitting there, waiting behind the hassle.

**Alex:** I am Alex. This is Value, Created. Chapter thirteen: Removing Friction.

**Jordan:** Start with the definition, because friction hides in plain sight. Friction is anything that stands between a person and the thing they want. The extra click. The line at the counter. The form that asks for your life story to deliver a pizza. The password reset email that never arrives. None of these feel dramatic. Each one feels small. But small is the whole trick, because friction is never one big wall. It is a thousand tiny toll booths, and every booth takes its cut in attention, in time, in willpower. A customer who wanted your product at the first booth can be gone by the fifth.

**Alex:** The best demonstration of this is nearly a hundred years old, and it happened in a grocery store. In nineteen thirty seven, a grocer in Oklahoma City named Sylvan Goldman watched his customers shop, and he noticed something. People stopped buying when their arms were full. Not when their wallets were empty. When their arms were full. The basket they carried was the ceiling on the sale. So Goldman invented the shopping cart: a folding chair frame with wheels and two wire baskets. And then something strange happened. Nobody used it. Men thought it looked sissy. Women thought it was a baby stroller. The invention sat there, ignored. So Goldman hired models, well dressed men and women, to push the carts around his store and fill them up. Real shoppers watched the models, got curious, and started copying them. Sales soared. Goldman had not changed his products, his prices, or his advertising. He had removed one physical limit, the carrying capacity of two arms, and the value that had been trapped behind it poured out.

**Jordan:** That is the first law of this chapter, so write it down. The value was already there. Goldman did not create new groceries. The three hundred million dollar button did not create new shoppers. Friction does not destroy value; it locks it up. Every wait, every click, every hassle is a lock on a door, and behind the door sits money, loyalty, and goodwill that already belongs to you, if you would just open it. Which means removing friction is the closest thing business has to free money. You are not building something new. You are collecting what is already yours.

**Alex:** The second law is that friction has a price tag, and it is bigger than you think. In two thousand six, an Amazon engineer named Greg Linden ran an experiment. He added artificial delays to search results, a tenth of a second, two tenths, half a second, and measured what happened to sales. Every one hundred milliseconds of delay cost Amazon one percent of its sales. One percent, per tenth of a second. Around the same time, Google found that a half second delay in loading search results caused traffic to drop twenty percent. Half a second. Not five seconds. Half of one second. Your customers are not patient people making careful trade offs. They are busy people, and the moment a page stalls, their attention is already somewhere else.

**Jordan:** Jeff Bezos understood this at the level of instinct. In nineteen ninety nine, Amazon patented one click ordering: buy the thing with a single click, no cart, no checkout, no forms. It was so valuable that Amazon sued a competitor over it, and Apple later licensed it for its own store. Think about what that patent really is. It is not a technology. It is the legal ownership of a removed hassle. One click instead of six. And Apple built an entire music empire on the same principle. Buying a song on iTunes took seconds, while the alternative was driving to a store or wrestling with piracy. The friction of the old way was so high that removing it created an industry.

**Alex:** So the physics are settled. Friction is a tax, the tax is heavy, and it never appears on any spreadsheet in the building. The company only sees the customers who made it through the checkout. It never meets the ones who gave up at the password field. So it congratulates itself on a fine process, while a fortune quietly walks out the back door. Which brings us to the practical part. Three rules for finding friction and killing it.

**Jordan:** Rule one: watch where people stall. Do not ask them. Watch them. Customers will tell you everything is fine on a survey, and then you will watch the recording of their session and see them click the same button four times, sigh, and leave. Spool found the three hundred million dollar button by sitting next to shoppers, not by polling them. Goldman found the carrying limit by watching arms, not by reading comment cards. Friction lives in behavior, not in opinions. Once a month, sit next to a real customer and watch them use what you built. You will find a toll booth within the hour. Guaranteed.

**Alex:** Rule two: delete a step before you add a feature. Every business loves to add. Add a field to the form, add a screen to the flow, add a verification for safety. Almost nobody subtracts. But subtraction is where the money is. There is a famous story about a travel site that had one optional field on its booking form, labeled Company, sitting right under the address line. Customers kept typing their company name into the address box by mistake, the credit card check failed, and the booking died. One confusing field. Twelve million dollars a year, gone. The fix was not a better field. It was no field. Every step you delete is a toll booth demolished. Before your next launch, do not ask what to add. Ask what to remove.

**Jordan:** Rule three: measure the wait in seconds, and make somebody own it. What gets measured gets managed, and right now, in most companies, nobody owns the waiting. The page load time belongs to engineering, the hold music belongs to support, the line at the counter belongs to nobody. Pick the three waits your customer feels most, put a number on each, and put one name next to each number. Amazon's one hundred millisecond rule was not a slogan. It was a budget, enforced like money. Because waiting is money. Every second of hold music is a second of your customer's life you are spending without permission. Treat their time like your money, and they will treat your product like it matters.

**Alex:** Here is the takeaway, and it is a strange one. Friction is invisible to the people who built it. You know your own checkout flow because you designed it, so it feels obvious to you. It is not obvious to a tired parent on a phone at midnight, one eye on a crying baby. The three hundred million dollar button looked perfectly reasonable to everyone inside that company, right up until someone watched a stranger try to use it. Your business is full of toll booths you cannot see, because you built them and then stopped looking. The value on the other side is already yours. Go collect it.
