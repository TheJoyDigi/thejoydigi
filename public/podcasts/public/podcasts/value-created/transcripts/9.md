**Alex:** In two thousand one, Dean Kamen unveiled the Segway. The hype was unreal. Steve Jobs said it would be bigger than the personal computer. Investors poured in over a hundred million dollars. The engineering was genuinely brilliant: gyroscopes, self balancing, a machine that seemed to read your mind. It was, by every technical measure, a better product than anything around it.

**Jordan:** And then the world shrugged. After a decade, Segway had sold about thirty thousand units total. That is fewer than a single midsize car dealership sells in a year. The most hyped invention of the decade became a punchline, mostly seen under mall security guards. Brilliant engineering. Nobody wanted it.

**Alex:** I am Alex.

**Jordan:** I am Jordan. This is Value, Created. Chapter nine: Making Things People Want.

**Alex:** Last chapter we said knowledge is the raw material that weighs nothing. But raw material is useless until you aim it. And here is where most builders go wrong. They fall in love with the building and forget the wanting. The Segway team built the right thing brilliantly. They just never checked whether anyone was asking for it.

**Jordan:** This is the most expensive mistake in business, and it has a name: building in a vacuum. It looks like this. A founder has an idea in the shower. They spend a year building it in secret. They polish every detail. Launch day comes, confetti, press release, and then silence. Crickets. Not because the product is bad. Because nobody was waiting for it. They built a solution and went looking for a problem.

**Alex:** Contrast that with the opposite story. In the nineteen seventies, a man named Gary Dahl looked at the pet industry and noticed something strange. People loved their pets, but pets were expensive, messy, and demanding. So he sold them a rock. A literal rock, in a cardboard box with air holes, with a thirty two page instruction manual. The Pet Rock. He sold over a million of them at four dollars each.

**Jordan:** Was the Pet Rock a better product than the Segway? Technically, it is a rock. But it understood something the Segway team missed. People do not buy products. They buy what the product does for them. Nobody wanted a self balancing transportation platform. But a million people wanted a funny gift, a joke to share, a little moment of delight for four dollars. The rock won on want. The Segway lost on want. Want is the whole game.

**Alex:** This is the big idea of the chapter, and it is worth tattooing on your forehead. The best product does not win. The best fit wins. Fit means the thing you built matches a want that already exists in the world, strong enough that people will reach for their wallets. You cannot manufacture want with marketing. You can only discover it, like a prospector discovering gold. It is already in the ground. Your job is to find where.

**Jordan:** So how do you find it? Here is the uncomfortable truth. You do not find it by asking people what they want. People are terrible at predicting their own behavior. Henry Ford supposedly said that if he had asked people what they wanted, they would have said faster horses. Whether he said it or not, the point stands. What people say and what people do are two different countries.

**Alex:** What you do instead is watch. Watch what people already do, and where they struggle. The best product ideas come from watching someone do something the hard way and thinking, there has to be a better way. The founders of a famous vacation rental company started by watching conference attendees sleep on air mattresses in their apartment because hotels were sold out. They did not invent the desire for a cheap place to sleep. They watched it happening and built the fit.

**Jordan:** And then you build the smallest version that tests the want. Not the polished product. Not the full vision. The smallest thing that lets a stranger pay you, or sign up, or even just click. If nobody reaches for the small version, the big version will not save you. This is why smart builders charge early. A dollar of revenue tells you more about want than a thousand compliments. Compliments are free. Money is a vote.

**Alex:** There is a second test, and it is brutal. Ask yourself: if this disappeared tomorrow, who would complain? If the answer is nobody, you have not made something people want. You have made something people tolerate. The products that win are the ones with real grief attached to their absence. Think of the app you would genuinely miss. That grief is the sound of fit.

**Jordan:** And notice what this means for all the knowledge we talked about last chapter. Knowledge aimed at a real want becomes value. Knowledge aimed at nothing becomes a hobby. The Segway engineers were not unskilled. They were not ignorant. They were just pointed at a want that did not exist. Aim is everything. The arrow matters less than the target.

**Alex:** So the takeaway. Stop asking, what can I build? Start asking, what do people already want, and where are they settling for something worse? Watch behavior, not opinions. Build the smallest test. Charge early. And measure everything by one question: if this vanished tomorrow, who would grieve? Build for the grievers.
