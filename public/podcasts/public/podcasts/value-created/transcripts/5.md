You walk past a shop window and see a leather bag, hand-stitched, beautiful, completely unnecessary. You stand there for ten minutes doing math you would never do for bread. Moment three. **Alex:** Every act of value creation in human history draws from one of three wells. Well number one: needs. The things you must have. Food, water, shelter, sleep, medicine. Without them you suffer, and eventually you stop functioning. Needs are the oldest well. They are also the most crowded.

**Jordan:** Needs are powerful, but they have a cruel property. They are mostly already met. In a modern economy, almost nobody is bidding for their first loaf of bread. They are choosing between forty loaves. And when a need is met forty ways, the sellers start competing, and competition turns the well into a commodity market. Bread is deeply valuable and nearly worthless at the same time. Valuable because you must eat. Worthless because everyone sells it.

**Alex:** That is the paradox of the first well. Needs create enormous volume and thin margins. The farmer, the baker, the landlord all draw from it, and all of them fight over pennies. If you build only on needs, you had better be the cheapest or the closest, because the customer is not grateful. They are comparing.

**Jordan:** Well number two: wants. The things you desire but do not require. The leather bag in the window. The sports car. The corner office with the view. The phone upgrade when your old phone works fine. Nobody needs these. That is exactly why they are profitable.

**Alex:** Wants are where the margins live. A need has a ceiling, because the body only needs so much bread. A want has no ceiling, because desire has no ceiling. The bag is not priced on leather and thread. It is priced on how the owner feels walking into a room with it. Identity, status, beauty, belonging. You are not selling the object. You are selling the feeling of being the person who owns it.

**Jordan:** But wants have their own cruel property, and chapter two is knocking again. Desire fades at the margin. The tenth slice of pizza thrilled you less than the first, and the second designer bag thrills you less than the first one did. Wants are fashion. Fashion changes. This year's must-have is next year's embarrassment. Build on wants and you live on a treadmill, always inventing the next desire before the old one expires.

**Alex:** Which brings us to well number three. Pain. The things that hurt, right now, and that you would pay almost anything to make stop. The burst pipe. The toothache at two in the morning. The laptop with your whole business on it, frozen, the night before the big presentation. The ransomware note on the screen.

**Jordan:** Pain is the deepest well, and here is why. A need can wait until morning. A want can wait until payday. Pain cannot wait. Pain has a clock, and the clock is now. The person with water raining through the ceiling is not comparing plumbers. They are not reading reviews. They are not negotiating. They are buying the fastest rescue available, at whatever price rescue costs.

**Alex:** Notice what happened to price sensitivity. At noon, that plumber's quote is outrageous. At two in the morning, it is a bargain, because the price is no longer being compared to other plumbers. It is being compared to the cost of doing nothing. A flooded kitchen. A ruined floor. A night of no sleep before the biggest day of your career. Against that, almost any number looks small.

**Jordan:** This is the big idea, so say it carefully. Value tracks the intensity of feeling, and nothing a human feels is more intense than pain. We are wired this way. Psychologists have measured it for decades. People work roughly twice as hard to avoid losing something as to gain the same thing. The fear of loss beats the hope of gain, every time, in every culture tested.

**Alex:** Put the three wells in one line. Needs keep you alive. Wants make life sweet. Pain makes you act now. Alive is negotiable, sweet is optional, but now is now. That is why the person selling relief from pain names their price, while the person selling bread takes whatever the market gives.

**Jordan:** Look at the great fortunes and you will see the third well everywhere. The locksmith at midnight is not selling a twenty dollar lock. He is selling the end of standing outside your own door in the cold. The dentist is not selling thirty minutes of drilling. She is selling the end of the worst night of your month. The cybersecurity company is not selling software. It is selling the end of the nightmare where the business dies overnight.

**Alex:** And the deepest veins of pain are the ones that come back. A burst pipe is once. Payroll is every two weeks. Taxes are every year. Backups, security, accounting, insurance. Recurring pain is why the subscription exists. Solve a pain that returns on schedule, and the customer does not buy once. They pay you to never feel it again, month after month.

**Jordan:** Now the practical part. How do you find the wells? Stop brainstorming products. Start listening for pain. Three questions. One, whose pain is this? Name a real person, not a market. Markets do not feel pain. People do. If you cannot name the person and describe their worst Tuesday, you have not found a well. You have found a guess.

**Alex:** Two, what does the pain cost them for every day it continues? Not in feelings, in numbers. The flooded kitchen costs a floor. The frozen laptop costs the contract. The slow checkout page costs the store a percentage of every single day's sales, forever. Price your solution against the cost of the pain, not the cost of your effort. The plumber's wrench turns for twenty minutes. The flooded kitchen would have cost thousands.

**Jordan:** Three, would they pay to make it stop today, not someday? Someday is where wants live. Today is where pain lives. If the answer is today, you have found the deepest well. If the answer is someday, you are selling a want, which is fine, but price it like a want and do not be surprised when the customer sleeps on it.

**Alex:** A warning, because this cuts both ways. There are two ways to work the third well. Relieve real pain, and you are a creator of value. Manufacture fake pain, and you are something else. The countdown timer that resets when you refresh the page. The warning that your computer has a virus, sold by the people who put it there. The rumor of scarcity invented to force your hand. Chapter four taught us to hear rumors. Invented urgency is a rumor, and customers learn to hear it too.

**Jordan:** Real pain is discovered. Fake pain is manufactured. The test is simple. If you stopped selling tomorrow, would the pain still be there? The burst pipe does not care about your marketing. The toothache does not need your funnel. Build on pain that exists without you, and the well never runs dry.

**Alex:** And here is the takeaway to carry. Find the pain, and you have found the value. Needs are crowded. Wants are fickle. Pain is urgent, personal, and priced against the cost of doing nothing. Every business you admire, every product you cannot live without, every fortune that looks like luck from the outside, is a rescue operation in disguise. Someone found a pain and ended it.
