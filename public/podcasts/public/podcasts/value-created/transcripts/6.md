**Alex:** Say it as a formula. Value equals problem solved, minus the cost of solving it. That is the entire discipline in one line. Everything else in this book is commentary. Find a real problem, solve it well, charge less than the pain it caused, and you have created value. There is no other source. There never was.

**Jordan:** Test it against the fortunes. The shipping container did not invent trade. It solved the problem that loading a ship took days and broke everything. The barcode did not invent selling. It solved the problem that checkout was slow and inventory was guesswork. The search engine did not invent curiosity. It solved the problem that finding anything took forever.

**Alex:** Go further back. The plow solved the problem that human backs could not feed a village. The printing press solved the problem that books took a year to copy by hand. The vaccine solved the problem that children died of things nobody could see. Every one of them looks like an invention from the outside. From the inside, every one is a rescue.

**Jordan:** And notice the shape. The problem comes first, always. Nobody woke up wanting a shipping container. They woke up with cargo rotting on the dock. Pain is a problem with a clock on it.

**Alex:** Which means the creator's job is not to invent. It is to notice. The locksmith did not invent locks. Invention is just what happens after you stare at a problem long enough.

**Jordan:** Here is where most people get it backwards. They start with a solution and go hunting for a problem. **Alex:** Problem-first thinking runs the other way. Live inside the problem before you touch the solution. The nurse copying charts by hand, that software was not designed in a meeting room. **Jordan:** There is a test for whether you have found a real problem, and it is brutal. Would someone pay you to make it stop, today? Not download your app. Not try your free trial. **Alex:** The size of the value tracks the size of the problem, multiplied by how many people have it. A headache pill solves a small problem for billions of people. **Jordan:** But there is a trap inside the arithmetic, and chapter two is knocking. The tenth headache pill is worth less than the first. Problems get solved, and solved problems stop paying. The locksmith's best day is the city's worst lockout day. Once everyone has a spare key hidden somewhere, the emergency fades. Value is not a permanent property of the solution. **Alex:** Which is why the great creators do not solve one problem. They build a machine for finding problems. The plumber fixes your pipe, then notices your water heater is dying, then offers the maintenance plan. The software company ships the tool, then watches where users still struggle, then ships the next tool. One problem is a job. A pipeline of problems is a business.

**Jordan:** Now the practical part. How do you find problems worth your life? Three moves. First, live where the problems are. Not in the brainstorming session, in the nurse's twelfth hour, the restaurant's empty Friday, the rain outside the locked car. Problems do not announce themselves in meetings. They leak out in complaints, workarounds, and the things people apologize for.

**Alex:** Second, listen for the workaround. Every workaround is a problem wearing a disguise. The spreadsheet that three people maintain by hand because the real system cannot do it. The drawer full of phone chargers because the battery never lasts. The notebook where the nurse writes the things the software cannot capture. Wherever humans have built a clumsy bridge, there is a river that needs a real one.

**Jordan:** Third, price the problem before you price the solution. Ask what the problem costs per day, per month, per year, while it stays unsolved. The empty restaurant loses a Friday. The hand-copied charts lose hours and risk lives. The locked car loses the meeting, the flight, the day. The cost of the unsolved problem is the ceiling on what your solution can charge. Know that number before you build anything.

**Alex:** A warning, because this cuts both ways. Not every problem wants your solution. Some problems are load-bearing. The couple that argues about the dishes does not have a dish problem. **Jordan:** And some problems are not yours to solve. The test is not whether you can see it. The test is whether you are the one who should fix it. The best problem for you is the one you are already close to, already understand, already equipped to end.
