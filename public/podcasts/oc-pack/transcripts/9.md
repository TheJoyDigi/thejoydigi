**Jordan:** Okay, so layer one is just: paws down, good things happen. What does a practice session actually look like? Give me the boring drill, because you promised me a boring drill.

**Alex:** Happily, because boring is the whole secret. Layer one happens nowhere near the front door. In a quiet room, with your dog already calm, have small treats ready. Stand still. The moment your dog is standing with all four paws on the floor, say yes, in a bright voice, and deliver a treat low, at the height of the mouth of the dog, or drop it on the floor between the front paws. Delivering low matters, because a treat held high is an invitation to jump for it. Then take one step, pause, and reward paws down again. You are building a simple idea: standing on the floor near a person makes treats rain. Two or three minutes is plenty.

**Jordan:** And when he jumps during that?

**Alex:** Then you become furniture. Turn slightly away, fold your arms, look at the ceiling, and say nothing. No eye contact, no talking, no pushing. The second all four paws are back on the floor, you come back to life: yes, treat, warmth, praise. Dogs learn this contrast fast. Jumping makes the person switch off. Paws down switches the person back on.

**Jordan:** I love the image of me as a vending machine with an on-off switch. What is layer two?

**Alex:** Layer two is teaching a real sit, away from the door, if your dog does not already have one. Use the treat as a lure, raise it slowly above the head of the dog until the rear end settles to the ground on its own, then say yes and reward. Never push the rear end down; that teaches the dog your hand is part of the cue, and it is not kind to the hips. Practice the sit in several rooms, in the yard, and eventually near the front doorway on a quiet day. Dogs do not generalize well. A sit that works in the kitchen does not automatically exist at the front door until you have practiced it there.

**Jordan:** That explains so much about my life. Kitchen genius, front door chaos. Layer three?

**Alex:** Layer three is the doorway drill, and this is where a helper earns their dinner. Put your dog on a leash, held loosely, a few steps back from the door. Your helper knocks softly, or rings the bell once, and you ask for the sit before anyone opens anything. Reward the sit. Then the helper opens the door a crack. Reward again. Then the helper steps in calmly, ignoring the dog completely, while you reward paws on the floor. If the dog launches, the helper steps back out, the door closes, and the fun pauses for ten seconds. Then you run it again, a little easier. Veterinary training guidance suggests repeating this greeting routine about four to six times for each practice visitor. The door has to become boring through practice, the way a fire drill becomes boring.

**Jordan:** You mentioned methods we should not use. Say that part plainly, because the internet has opinions.

**Alex:** Plainly, then. Do not knee a jumping dog in the chest. Do not shove the dog backward, squeeze the paws, or shout the dog down. And do not reach for punishment tools like shock collars or prong collars for a greeting habit. The American Veterinary Society of Animal Behavior, the veterinarians and doctorate level behaviorists who study this professionally, reviewed the evidence and concluded that only reward based methods should be used for dog training, including behavior problems. Aversive methods carry real risks to welfare and to the bond between the dog and the person, and there is no evidence they work better. For a behavior made of joy and a wish to be close to you, pain and startling are aimed at the exact relationship you are trying to build.
