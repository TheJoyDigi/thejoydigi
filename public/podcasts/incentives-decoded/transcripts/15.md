**Alex:** Welcome to Incentives, Decoded. I am Alex.

**Jordan:** And I am Jordan. This is chapter fifteen, Tragedy of the Commons. Last time, you learned the cobra effect, how incentives backfire and create the problem they were solving. Today, we look at what happens when there is no incentive to care at all. Shared things get drained.

**Alex:** The tragedy of the commons is simple. When a resource is shared but nobody owns it, everyone takes and nobody maintains. The incentive is to grab before others do. And the result is ruin.

**Jordan:** This is the idea that explains overfishing, traffic, climate change, and your office kitchen. Same logic, different scale.

**Alex:** The classic example is fishing. Picture a lake, ten boats, plenty of fish. Each fisherman thinks: if I do not catch them, someone else will.

**Jordan:** So everyone fishes harder. Bigger nets, longer hours. The catch is great, for a while. Then the fish get scarce. Then they are gone.

**Alex:** No single fisherman destroyed the lake. Each one was rational. Take the fish now, because waiting just means someone else takes them. The incentive is to grab first.

**Jordan:** And nobody has the incentive to hold back. If you conserve, you just leave more fish for your competitors. Restraint is punished. Extraction is rewarded. The lake does not stand a chance.

**Alex:** This is not about greed. It is about structure. Put good people in a commons without rules, and they will often drain it. Not because they are bad, but because the payoff says take. The commons often loses without rules, though communities that build them can beat the tragedy.

**Jordan:** Here is what makes this tragic. Everyone can see it happening. The fishermen know the lake is dying. They just cannot stop.

**Alex:** Because stopping alone does not help. If nine boats keep fishing and you stop, you just lose money while the lake dies anyway. The only thing worse than participating in the tragedy is being the noble sucker who abstains while everyone else takes.

**Jordan:** This is why the commons needs rules, not virtue. Appeals to conscience do not work. The incentive to defect is too strong. You need enforcement, ownership, or a structure that changes the payoff.

**Alex:** Notice the pattern. The cobra effect was about bad incentives creating new problems. The tragedy of the commons is about missing incentives. Nobody gets rewarded for maintaining the shared thing. So nobody does.

**Jordan:** And the modern commons are invisible. Your attention is a commons, drained by every app. Your team's time is a commons, drained by every unnecessary meeting. The atmosphere is a commons. The ocean is a commons. The pattern scales from the fridge to the planet.

**Alex:** What commons are you draining?

**Jordan:** Your own attention. Every scroll, every notification, every just one more video. The apps are the fishermen. Your focus is the lake. And it is getting overfished.

**Alex:** Your relationships. The friend you only call when you need something. The family group chat you ignore. Attention is a commons too, and withdrawal without deposit drains it.

**Jordan:** Your workplace. The meeting you call that could have been an email. You are fishing in the shared lake of everyone's time. Multiply by ten people, one hour each. That is the tragedy, in calendar form.

**Alex:** And the bigger ones. The flights. The plastic. The water. You are one boat on a very large lake. Your individual impact is tiny. But everyone thinks that, and that is exactly the problem.

**Jordan:** So here is the move. Commons do not fix themselves. They need structure.

**Alex:** One. Assign ownership. The fridge gets clean when one person owns the job. The lake recovers with fishing quotas and enforcement. Ownership creates the incentive to maintain. Even rotating ownership works. Somebody must be responsible.

**Jordan:** Two. Make the cost visible. People overfish because the cost is invisible and shared. Show the lake's fish count. Show the meeting's dollar cost. When everyone sees the drain, behavior shifts. What gets measured gets managed, carefully, per Goodhart.

**Alex:** Three. Set rules with teeth. Quotas, limits, norms with consequences. The honor system does not survive contact with incentives. The commons that survive all have boundaries. National parks have rangers. Fisheries have inspectors. Your team needs norms. And personally? Be the owner, not the drainer. Clean the fridge. Decline the meeting. Put the phone down. You cannot fix the ocean alone, but you can stop overfishing your own lakes.
