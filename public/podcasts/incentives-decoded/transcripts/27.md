**Alex:** This is the most practical idea in the whole book. Every system that depends on people being heroic fails. Every system that makes the right thing effortless wins.

**Jordan:** Willpower is a terrible incentive designer. Alignment is a great one.

**Alex:** Let me show you alignment in action. A hospital wanted its doctors to wash their hands more. They tried posters. They tried training. They tried shaming. Nothing moved.

**Jordan:** Then someone changed the game. They put the hand sanitizer dispensers right in the doorway, at hand height, impossible to miss. Hand washing went up sharply. Same doctors. Same knowledge. Different design.

**Alex:** Nobody got more disciplined. Nobody got a lecture that finally worked. The right move just became the easy move.

**Jordan:** Here is another one. A company was drowning in late expense reports. Finance sent reminders. Managers sent warnings. People still filed them months late.

**Alex:** Then they changed one thing. The expense app started auto-filling from the corporate card. Filing a report went from a forty-minute chore to a two-minute review. Late reports dropped to almost zero.

**Jordan:** They did not fight human nature. They stopped requiring heroism and started rewarding the path of least resistance.

**Alex:** Here is the deeper pattern. Every failed system has the same flaw. It was designed for the people the designer wished existed.

**Jordan:** The diet that assumes you will never crave sugar. The budget that assumes you will never impulse-buy. The meeting policy that assumes everyone reads the agenda. These systems do not fail because people are weak. They fail because the designer was arrogant.

**Alex:** The designer imagined rational, disciplined, perfectly motivated humans. Real humans take the easy path. Always. That is not a bug. That is the iron law doing its work.

**Jordan:** So the question is never how do I make people better. The question is how do I make the better choice the easier choice. You do not change the player. You change the game board.

**Alex:** This is why the hiking group beat the exercise bike. The bike required fighting yourself every morning. The hiking group made exercise the side effect of friendship. One fought human nature. The other surfed it.

**Jordan:** So where are you fighting instead of aligning?

**Alex:** Look at your own habits first. The diet you keep breaking. The savings plan you keep raiding. The inbox you keep drowning in. Each one is a game designed for a fantasy version of you.

**Jordan:** Now ask the alignment question. What would make the right move the easy move? Not through effort. Through design.

**Alex:** If you want to exercise tomorrow, lay out the gym clothes tonight. If you want to stop scrolling, put the router on a timer so the wifi dies at ten. Design the environment once, and laziness does the rest.

**Jordan:** Then look outward. Your team. Your kids. Your company. Where are you demanding discipline when you could be designing alignment? The parent who yells about screen time could just put the router on a timer. Same outcome. Zero fights.

**Alex:** So here is the move. Three steps to align any game.

**Jordan:** One. Stop asking how do I get people to do the right thing. Start asking what makes the right thing the easy thing. That one reframe changes everything.

**Alex:** Two. Remove friction from the behavior you want. Auto-fill the form. Put the fruit at eye level. Make the good choice the default. Every click you eliminate multiplies compliance.

**Jordan:** Three. Add friction to the behavior you do not want. Not punishment, just speed bumps. The phone in another room. The junk food on the top shelf. The meeting that requires an agenda to get scheduled.

**Alex:** Notice what is missing from this list. Lectures. Willpower. Guilt. Alignment does not need any of them. It just quietly rearranges the board until winning feels like coasting.
