Today, we ask the question underneath every game in this book. Is someone winning because someone else is losing, or can everyone win?

**Alex:** Zero-sum means the pie is fixed. Your slice grows only if mine shrinks. Positive-sum means we can bake a bigger pie. This distinction changes how you negotiate, who you partner with, and which fights are worth having.

**Jordan:** Choosing which game to play is the biggest move in this entire book. Get this right, and everything else gets easier.

**Alex:** Watch a salary negotiation. Classic zero-sum framing. Every dollar of salary is a dollar of company profit. The pie is this year's budget. Fight over slices.

**Jordan:** But the smart negotiator changes the game. What if I take less salary and more equity? What if we tie my bonus to revenue I generate? Now my win is the company's win. The pie gets bigger because I am motivated to grow it.

**Alex:** Same with business. Two companies can fight over the same customers, racing to the bottom on price. Zero-sum. Blood on the floor.

**Jordan:** Or they can serve different customers, or partner, or create a new category. The smartphone did not just take Nokia's slice. It grew the whole pie: apps, maps, rides, a market ten times bigger. Positive-sum thinking does not just split value. It creates it.

**Alex:** Here is why this matters. Zero-sum thinking is the default, and it is usually wrong.

**Jordan:** Evolution wired us for zero-sum. Status, mates, territory, in the ancestral world, these were fixed pies. Your gain was my loss. That wiring is still running.

**Alex:** But the modern world is mostly positive-sum. Trade, technology, ideas, relationships. These are not fixed pies. When you teach someone something, you do not lose the knowledge. When two companies partner, both can grow.

**Jordan:** The tragedy is that zero-sum thinking creates zero-sum outcomes. If I assume you are trying to take my slice, I will fight you for it. Now we are in a fight, the pie shrinks from legal fees and distrust, and my assumption becomes true. Self-fulfilling.

**Alex:** And the reverse is true. Assume positive-sum, propose the bigger pie, and often the other side says yes. Not because they are nice. Because a bigger slice of a bigger pie beats a big slice of a shrinking one. Math convinces where morals do not.

**Jordan:** The negotiation where you are trying to win. What if winning the negotiation costs you the relationship? The best deal is not the one where you extract the most. It is the one where both sides want to do it again.

**Alex:** Social media. The status game. The followers, the likes, the comparisons. Pure zero-sum. Someone's highlight reel is your insecurity. You cannot win this game. You can only opt out.

**Jordan:** And the big one. Your career. Are you competing for a fixed number of slots, or building something new? The employee plays zero-sum for promotions. The founder plays positive-sum by creating value. Different games. Different ceilings.

**Alex:** So here is the move. Stop asking how do I win. Start asking which game am I playing.

**Jordan:** One. Spot zero-sum traps. Anytime you feel the urge to beat someone, pause. Is the pie actually fixed? Or am I assuming it is? Most workplace rivalries, most negotiations, most arguments are positive-sum games being played with zero-sum rules.
