**Alex:** A meeting is just a game with chairs. Players, rules, payoffs. The players are the people. The rules are the agenda. And the payoffs are what each person actually wants, which is almost never what the agenda says.

**Jordan:** Learn to see the payoffs, and meetings stop being confusing. They become readable. Predictable. Even steerable.

**Alex:** Picture a product meeting. The team is deciding whether to kill a feature that nobody uses.

**Jordan:** The engineer wants to kill it. Her payoff is less maintenance, fewer late-night pages, cleaner code. Simple.

**Alex:** The product manager wants to keep it. His payoff is different. He launched that feature. Killing it means admitting it failed. His payoff is not the product. It is his reputation.

**Jordan:** The designer does not care about the feature. Her payoff is leaving early today. She will agree with whoever speaks last and sounds confident.

**Alex:** The manager wants consensus. His payoff is a quiet team and a good review from his boss. He will support whatever avoids conflict.

**Jordan:** Same meeting. Four players. Four different games. The agenda says decide the feature's fate. The real game is everyone protecting their own payoff.

**Alex:** And the feature? Nobody is playing for the feature. That is why bad features survive for years. The game was never about the product.

**Jordan:** Here is the deeper insight. People never argue about what they are actually arguing about.

**Alex:** The budget fight is not about the budget. It is about whose team matters more. The timeline debate is not about dates. It is about who gets blamed if it slips. The strategy disagreement is not about strategy. It is about who gets to decide.

**Jordan:** Once you see this, you stop taking objections at face value. When someone says this will not work, translate it. What they mean is this threatens my payoff. And now you know what to address.

**Alex:** This is why logic loses arguments. You bring data. They have a payoff. Data does not beat incentives. Ever. You have to speak to the payoff, not the point.

**Jordan:** The person who says let us look at the numbers wants delay. The person who says we need more research wants cover. The person who says I just have a bad feeling is protecting something they will not name. Read the payoff, not the words.

**Alex:** So let us turn this around. Think about the last meeting you were in.

**Jordan:** Who was there? Not their titles. Their payoffs. What did each person actually want walking in?

**Alex:** The person who talked the most. Their payoff was visibility. They needed the boss to see them contributing. Their words were not for the room. They were for one person.

**Jordan:** And you. What was your payoff? Be honest. Were you playing for the outcome? Or were you playing to look smart, avoid conflict, or get out early? No judgment. But you cannot scan the room if you will not study yourself.

**Alex:** So here is the move. Before your next meeting, do this in five minutes.

**Jordan:** One. List the players. Write down every person who will be there. Not their roles. Their names. Each one is playing their own game.
