# Chapter 11: The Optimization Loop

Chapter 10 built the machine. This chapter is the habit that makes the machine better every week: a short loop of looking, cutting, feeding, and writing things down. Optimization is a grand word for a modest practice. Thirty minutes a week, the same few moves, repeated until the account quietly becomes very good.

Set the scene. Same time each week works best, and Monday has a real advantage, because it catches whatever the weekend searches did. You need three things open: the search terms report, your negative keyword lists, and a simple change log. That is the whole workshop.

## The first ten minutes: read the search terms, sorted by cost

The search terms report shows what people actually typed, which is never quite what your keywords assumed. Open it for the last seven days, turn on the column that shows which keyword matched each search, and sort by cost, highest first.

Sorting by cost is the discipline. The most expensive searches sit at the top, and expensive is where the money leaks. A strange search that cost 40 cents is a curiosity. A strange search that cost 40 dollars is a decision. Read from the top down and ask one question of each line: could this person plausibly book a stay with a sitter in Irvine, Costa Mesa, Santa Ana, or San Juan Capistrano? Yes means leave it alone or promote it. No means it goes on the negative list. Maybe means it gets one more week of evidence. Not every line deserves a verdict today.

## The Blue Sock waste list

Most waste falls into a handful of families, and once you can name them, you will spot them in seconds. Keep them as shared negative lists, built once and applied to every campaign, so one cleanup protects the whole account.

First, jobs and hiring. Dog boarding jobs. Pet sitter hiring. Kennel attendant salary. Dog daycare careers. These people want work, not care, and some of them click the ad on top before the job board below it. They will never book. They are often the single biggest leak in a young pet account.

Second, free and cheap in the wrong spirit. Free dog boarding. Free pet sitting. How to get someone to watch my dog for free. Your sitters are professionals in their own homes at 55 to 89 dollars a night. A searcher hunting for free is not a discount customer waiting to be persuaded. They are a different market entirely.

Third, do it yourself and how to. How to board a dog at home. How to start a pet sitting business. Dog boarding license requirements. Franchise searches belong here too. Interesting people, some of them future competitors, none of them customers.

Fourth, wrong services. This family needs care, because it sounds close. Dog grooming irvine: you do not sell grooming. Vet near me, dog teeth cleaning, pet store: not you. And the big one for The Blue Sock, dog walking. People search for walkers constantly, and a broad system will happily match your boarding ads to them. The Blue Sock does not sell a standalone walking service. Walks exist only as little adventures inside a stay. So walking searches, on their own, are waste, however friendly they look.

Fifth, wrong cities. Anaheim, Tustin, Mission Viejo, Newport Beach, Huntington Beach, Laguna, Fullerton. Lovely cities, real dog parents, and no Blue Sock sitter in any of them yet. Your blog may rank there for free, which is fine. Paid clicks there buy bookings you cannot fulfill. One exception in spirit: if a pattern shows people in a border city happily driving to a sitter, that is a Chapter 12 expansion clue, not a reason to leave the leak open today.

Sixth, competitor accidents. Someone types the name of a big marketplace or a specific kennel, clearly hunting for that business, and your ad appears because one of your keywords loosely matched. That click is usually expensive and rarely converts, because the searcher already chose. This is different from a searcher comparing options in general words. It is a person calling a name that is not yours. Add those names to the waste list as they show up in the report, unless you one day decide, deliberately and with its own campaign, to compete for comparison shoppers. Deliberate is the key word. Accidents are what you are cleaning.

## The honest nuance about negative keywords

Here is the part most beginners get wrong, and it costs them quietly. Positive keywords are generous. Add "dog boarding" and Google expands it to close variants, plurals, and close spellings on its own. Negative keywords do no such favor. A negative blocks only what its match type covers, and it does not stretch to synonyms, plurals, or misspellings by itself.

So the waste list has to be written out in its variations. Job and jobs are separate entries in practice. Hiring, hire, careers, career, salary, employment. If the report shows "dog boarding job" costing money in week one, blocking that one exact search stops that one search. The family stops when you block the pattern properly: the word job as a broad negative, so any search containing it is blocked, plus its plural and its cousins listed alongside.

Negative match types, in one breath. A negative exact match blocks that single search. A negative phrase match blocks any search containing that phrase in that order. A negative broad match blocks any search containing all of its words, in any order. Workhorse rule: block single odd searches exactly as seen, block whole junk families with phrase or broad, and when in doubt, look at what next week's report does and widen the block then. Also respect the opposite error. Over-blocking starves the account. If a term might be a real customer phrasing things oddly, watch it another week instead of banning it.

## Promoting winners, pausing losers, feeding what works

The search terms report giveth as well as taketh away. Some searches will arrive that you never wrote as keywords, convert into booking requests, and repeat. A phrase like "dog boarding for nervous dogs" or "overnight puppy care irvine" might show up carrying real requests. Promote those. Add the search term as its own keyword, in phrase or exact match, inside the ad group it belongs to, and make sure the ad and the landing page speak its language. A promoted winner gets its own clean data from then on, instead of hiding inside a broader keyword's averages.

Losers get the other treatment, but define loser honestly. An ad group is not a loser because it spent money. It is a loser when it has spent clearly more than a booking request should cost, several times over, with no requests to show. Use your own number as the ruler. If a booking request is worth about 50 dollars to you as a working target, an ad group that has spent 150 dollars with zero requests has told you a story three times. Pause it, note it in the log, and let its budget flow to the groups that produce. Pausing is not deleting. A paused group can return for a holiday season or a new sitter. It just stops spending while it has nothing to say.

Feeding winners is the pleasant third move. When a city or a need clearly produces requests at a good cost, it earns more room: a little more budget at the campaign level, a second ad to rotate, or the first careful test of a broader keyword inside that group only. Feed slowly. A winner doubled overnight is often just a winner diluted, because the next clicks Google finds are rarely as good as the first ones.

## Minutes ten to thirty: cut, feed, write it down

The middle of the half hour belongs to the waste list you just read about: turning the report's expensive lines into negatives, in families, at the shared list level, so the fix covers every campaign at once. Ten honest minutes here is where most of the month's savings are made.

The next ten minutes belong to the other direction. Promote the searches that earned it, pause the ad groups that have spent several times a booking's value with nothing to show, and move a little budget toward whatever produced requests at the best cost. Small moves only. You are steering, not rebuilding.

The last few minutes are unglamorous and non-negotiable: write the changes in the log while you still remember why you made them. A loop you do not record is just a series of opinions. A loop you record becomes a system the account, and anyone who helps you run it, can learn from.

## The monthly routine: balance, scorecards, freshness, capacity

Once a month, give the account an hour and ask bigger questions.

First, budget balance between boarding and daycare. These two services breathe on different calendars. Boarding swells before summer and the holidays, when trips get booked. Daycare is steadier, a weekday rhythm. So the monthly question is not "did each campaign spend its share," it is "does the split still match demand and capacity?" If boarding requests are being turned away for full calendars in July while daycare has open room, the split should move, on purpose, in the log.

Second, the city scorecard, read aloud in words. For each city: what did we spend, how many booking requests came, what did each request cost, and does a sitter there have room for more? Irvine might read: the biggest spend, the most requests, a cost per request near target, capacity healthy across three sitters. Santa Ana might read: tiny spend, a handful of requests, but every one of them precious, because Olivia takes one family at a time and her calendar is the whole supply. A scorecard is not a ranking to punish the bottom city. It is a matching exercise between demand and the real calendars behind it.

Third, ad refresh. Ads tire. The same headlines shown to the same neighborhoods for months slowly earn fewer clicks, and seasons change what people need to hear. Once a month, retire the weakest headline or two in your busiest ad groups and write replacements with fresh proof: a newer review count, a seasonal line about holiday stays, a sitter's name where it earns trust. Small, regular freshness beats a frantic rewrite twice a year.

Fourth, the sitter capacity check, the step no dashboard will suggest and the one that matters most for this business. Your inventory is seven people's calendars, not a warehouse. Before any budget increase, ask the simple question out loud: who actually has room? Do not scale ads for a sitter who is full. Every click you buy for a full calendar purchases a disappointed customer and a refund conversation. If Johnny's calendar is booked solid for the holiday week, the Irvine boarding ads should coast, not climb. If a sitter just opened two free weekends, that is where next month's extra fifty dollars wants to go. Ads follow capacity. Never the other way around.

## One change at a time, and a log that proves it

The loop only teaches if you can tell what caused what. So the rule is one meaningful change at a time per campaign, and a change log simple enough that you will actually keep it. A notebook page or a plain document is fine. Each entry needs four things, spoken plainly: the date, what changed, why, and what you expect to happen. For example: "March 3. Added jobs and salary as broad negatives account wide. Search terms showed 90 dollars of job clicks last month. Expect waste clicks to fall this month."

That is the entire format. Thirty seconds per entry. A month later, the log turns arguments into evidence. Without it, every review meeting becomes two people remembering different accounts. With it, the account has a memory, and compounding has a paper trail.

## Why this compounds

None of these moves is dramatic. That is the point. Take a labeled example. Say an account spends 1,500 dollars a month and, like many young accounts, wastes about a fifth of it on searches that can never book. That is 300 dollars a month buying nothing. The weekly loop does not find all 300 at once. It finds 40 dollars of waste in week one, 25 more in week two, a small leak the week after. Each find is permanent, because a negative keyword keeps blocking while you sleep. Within a quarter, most of that 300 has quietly moved from job seekers and wrong cities into real Irvine dog parents. Same budget. Same ads, mostly. Noticeably more booking requests.

That is compounding in work clothes: small edges, kept. The waste you cut in March still pays you in December. The winner you promoted in spring gathers its own history all year. Do the loop for six months and the account you have is not the account you built. It is the account the loop built, one honest half hour at a time.

## Play It: Your Move

Monday, ten minutes into your weekly review. The top of the search terms report shows one line glaring at you: "dog walker near me," 214 dollars spent over the month, zero booking requests. Just below it: "dog boarding jobs irvine," 96 dollars spent, also zero.

A. Add "dog walker near me" as an exact negative, so that exact search never shows your ad again.
B. Add walker and walking as broad negatives on the shared wrong service list, plus job and jobs as broad negatives, and log both changes.
C. Leave the walking line alone. Those people have dogs, and maybe one day they will need boarding.

What happens with each: With A, that one exact search stops, and next week "dog walkers near me," "dog walking irvine," and "dog walker needed" spend another 180 dollars between them, because negatives do not expand to variants the way positive keywords do. You paid tuition for the nuance in this chapter. With C, the line repeats every month. In a year, at that pace, walking searches alone eat more than 2,500 dollars, enough to have bought sixty real boarding clicks many times over, all for a service The Blue Sock does not sell. With B, the whole family closes in one move, the job family closes beside it, and next Monday the top of your report is finally about customers. The lesson: block families, not single searches, and let the log remember why.

## The Short Version

Thirty minutes a week: search terms by cost, top down, verdict on each line.
Block the waste families: jobs, free, how to, wrong services, wrong cities, competitor accidents.
Negative keywords do not stretch, so list plurals and spellings yourself.
Promote winning searches into keywords, pause proven losers, feed winners slowly.
Monthly: rebalance boarding and daycare, read the city scorecard, refresh ads, check sitter capacity first.
