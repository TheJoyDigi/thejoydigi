# Chapter 11: The Optimization Loop

Chapter 10 set up the machine. This chapter is the habit that makes it better every week: a short loop of looking, cutting, feeding, and writing things down. Optimization is a grand word for a modest practice. Thirty minutes a week, the same few moves, repeated until the account quietly becomes very good.

One thing to know about scale first. At 20 dollars a day, as of November 19, 2026, the account buys about thirty clicks a week. That is a small report. You can read every line of it, and you should. It also means one week rarely proves anything. Weekly is for cleaning. Monthly is for judging.

## The weekly thirty minutes

Pick one time and keep it. Monday works well, because it catches what the weekend searches did. Open three things: the Google Ads account, the booking portal, and your change log. Then walk through five steps, in this order.

### Minutes 0 to 5: is the counting alive?

Before anything else, compare two numbers for last week. How many booking requests did the Conversions column record? How many requests arrived in the portal? Google's number should be equal or lower, because some requests come from other places. If Google says zero while the portal shows requests from people who clicked ads, stop and fix tracking before you touch anything else. Every other step this morning depends on the counting being true.

This step exists because of this account's own history. As of November 19, 2026, the Conversions column had read zero for thirty days while the campaign kept spending, and two requests sat in the All conversions column where Google's bidding never looks.

### Minutes 5 to 15: read the search terms, sorted by cost

The search terms report shows what people actually typed, which is never quite what your keywords assumed. Open it for the last seven days, add the column showing which keyword matched, and sort by cost, highest first.

Sorting by cost is the discipline. A strange search that cost 40 cents is a curiosity. A strange search that cost 8 dollars, two clicks of your daily budget, is a decision. Read from the top and ask one question of each line: could this person plausibly book a stay with a sitter in Irvine, Costa Mesa, Santa Ana, or San Juan Capistrano? Yes means leave it alone or promote it. No means it goes on a negative list. Maybe means one more week of evidence.

Here are real searches from the last thirty days, as of November 19, 2026, to train your eye. Good ones: dog boarding irvine, dog day care costa mesa, small group dog daycare near me. Waste: dog walker, dog walker app, dog kennels riverside, dog boarding in garden grove ca, and the bare words dog and dogs. Judgment calls: dog boarding cost per day, affordable doggy daycare, and competitor names like the dog stop tustin. More on those below.

### Minutes 15 to 20: add negatives to the shared lists

Turn the report's No lines into negatives, in families, on the shared lists described in the next section, so one fix covers every campaign at once.

### Minutes 20 to 25: check the groups and the map

Glance at three things. Clicks by ad group, so you notice a group that gets nothing or a group that eats everything. Quality Score on the main keywords, especially the near me ones, which sat at 1 and 2 as of November 19, 2026. And the geography report, the one showing where the people who clicked actually were, so you see what the three border cities, Tustin, Lake Forest, and Mission Viejo, are taking.

Do not pause anything on one week of thirty clicks. Note what you see. Act on it monthly, unless something is plainly broken.

### Minutes 25 to 30: write it down

Write every change in the log while you still remember why you made it. A loop you do not record is just a series of opinions.

## Shared negative keyword lists

As of November 19, 2026, the live campaign has 42 negative keywords, all attached to that one campaign, and no shared lists at all. That works while there is one campaign. It breaks the day a second one appears, because the new campaign starts with no protection, and someone has to remember to copy 42 words by hand.

A shared list fixes that. You build it once, in the account's shared library, and attach it to as many campaigns as you like. An account can hold up to twenty lists, each with up to five thousand words. You will need six. Build them from what is already live plus what the search terms have shown.

The first list: Jobs and Careers. Job, jobs, hiring, careers, salary, employment, become a dog sitter, how to start, franchise, business plan, course, certification. These people want work, not care, and in a young pet account they are often the biggest single leak.

The second list: Services We Do Not Sell. Dog walker, dog walkers, dog walking, walker, walkers, drop in, house sitting, cat, cats, cat sitting, grooming, groomer, pet taxi, bird boarding, rabbit boarding. The Blue Sock sells boarding and daycare. Walks exist only as optional experiences during a stay, so a search for a walker is never your customer.

Training needs care. Dog training and board and train are live negatives today, and that is right, because training is not advertised. But McKenna offers Board and Train as an add-on to boarding, and Chapter 12 describes a training campaign that might one day exist. The day it launches, remove those negatives from anything attached to the training campaign, or it will block its own best searches. That is one more reason to keep training words out of the shared lists, which reach every campaign, and on the boarding and daycare campaign alone, where removing them is one deliberate step.

The third list: Free and Do It Yourself. Free, diy, kennel plans, equipment. A parent hunting for free care, or for plans to build a kennel, is a different market.

The fourth list: Places and Outings. Dog park, dog beach, hikes, trails. These are people looking for somewhere to take their own dog, not somewhere to leave it.

The fifth list: Wrong Geography. Riverside, garden grove, anaheim, and whatever other cities the geography report and the search terms show. Presence targeting already keeps people in those cities from seeing your ads. This list catches a different case: a person inside your area typing another city's name. Two cautions. Do not add Tustin, Lake Forest, or Mission Viejo while you are deliberately testing them. And since Wildomar, in Riverside County, left the ads on November 17, riverside searches may fade on their own. Keep the negative anyway. It costs nothing.

The sixth list: Adoption and Sale. Adoption, shelter, puppies for sale, and rescue, handled carefully. A parent searching boarding for my rescue dog is a perfect customer. So block phrases like dog rescue and rescue near me, not the word rescue on its own.

Keep one thing out of every shared list: your brand terms. Blue sock, thebluesock, and the old name are negatives on the live campaign today, and that is fine. If you ever build a brand campaign, a shared list carrying those words would block it from its own name.

## Two gotchas that cost real money

The first gotcha: negatives do not stretch. Positive keywords are generous. Exact and phrase match now reach close variants and same meaning searches on their own. Negative keywords get no such favor. A negative blocks only what it literally says. Walker does not block walkers. Groomer does not block grooming. A misspelling slips past both. So every family gets written out in its plurals and its common spellings, by hand. That is why the lists above say walker and walkers, cat and cats, job and jobs.

The second gotcha: a broad negative needs all its words. Negative broad match blocks a search only if every word in the negative appears in it, in any order. The live campaign has one negative written as cheap cheapest, entered as a single broad negative. It blocks only searches containing both cheap and cheapest, which almost nobody types. In practice it blocks nothing. If you meant to block cheap searches, cheap and cheapest must be two separate entries.

Negative phrase match blocks the phrase in that order. Negative exact match blocks that one search only. The workhorse rule: block whole families with broad or phrase, block odd one-off searches exactly, and when unsure, watch one more week.

## Competitor and price searches, on purpose

On November 9, 2026, under Steph's plan, the price and competitor negatives were removed on purpose. So searches like dog boarding cost per day, affordable doggy daycare, the dog stop tustin, and barkley suites pet hotel daycare, all of which appeared in the last thirty days, are allowed to show your ads.

There is a real case for it. A parent comparing prices is still a parent who needs a stay, and an honest price in your ad lets the wrong budget skip the click. A parent who typed a competitor's name may still be shopping.

There is a real case against it too. A person who typed a specific business name has often already chosen, and those clicks tend to cost more and convert less. So treat it as a judgment to revisit with data, not a rule. After a month or two, read those lines in the search terms report. If price searches bring requests, keep them. If competitor names cost money and bring nothing, block them by name. Write the decision in the log either way.

## Promote winners, pause losers, feed slowly

The search terms report gives as well as takes. When a search you never wrote as a keyword keeps arriving and converts, promote it. Add it as its own exact keyword in the group it belongs to, and make sure the ad and landing page speak its language.

Losers get the other treatment, but only once the counting is proven. Until the Conversions column is trustworthy, every ad group looks like a loser, and pausing on that evidence is pausing on a broken instrument. Once tracking works, use your own ruler. As a labeled example, if a booking request is worth about 50 dollars to you as a target, an ad group that has spent 150 dollars with zero requests has told you its story three times. Pause it, log it, and let its share flow to groups that produce. Pausing is not deleting. A paused group can come back for the holidays or a new sitter.

Feed winners slowly. A winner doubled overnight is often a winner diluted, because the next clicks Google finds are rarely as good as the first.

## The monthly hour

Once a month, give the account an hour and ask bigger questions.

First, the boarding and daycare balance. Inside one campaign you cannot give each service its own budget. What you can do is see the split, ad group by ad group. If daycare is taking most of the clicks while boarding calendars sit open, or the reverse, that is the moment Chapter 10 described for splitting into two campaigns, budget permitting.

Second, the city scorecard, read in words. For each city: what did we spend, how many requests came, what did each cost, and does a sitter there have room? Read the four sitter cities first, then the three border cities as a test: did they produce requests that a neighboring sitter accepted?

Third, the near me groups. Have their Quality Scores moved off 1 and 2? Are they bringing requests? If not, the fix is usually relevance, a landing page and headlines that answer near me with a real city, not more money.

Fourth, ad refresh. Update the review count, which grows. Swap the seasonal headline, so Request Holiday Stays Early comes out in January. Check ad strength, and retire a weak headline or two in the busiest groups.

Fifth, and most important, sitter capacity. Your inventory is seven sitters' calendars. Before any budget increase, ask who actually has room. If Johnny is booked solid for the holiday week, Irvine boarding should coast, not climb. Ads follow capacity. Never the other way around.

## One change at a time, and a log that proves it

The loop only teaches if you can tell what caused what. So make one meaningful change at a time, and keep a log simple enough that you will actually keep it. Each entry needs four things: the date, what changed, why, and what you expect. For example: "November 24. Built the Jobs and Careers shared list and attached it. Search terms showed job searches last month. Expect them to disappear."

That is the whole format. Thirty seconds an entry. A month later, the log turns arguments into evidence, and when Steph asks why something changed, the answer is already written down.

## Why this compounds

None of these moves is dramatic. That is the point. Take a labeled example. Say a fifth of the 608 dollar month goes to searches that can never book. That is about 120 dollars a month buying nothing. The loop does not find it all at once. It finds 15 dollars of waste in week one, 10 more in week two, a small leak after that. Each find is permanent, because a negative keeps blocking while you sleep. Within a quarter, most of that 120 has moved into real parents in your four cities. Same budget, more requests.

That is compounding in work clothes: small edges, kept. The waste you cut in November still pays you next November.

## Play It: Your Move

Monday, ten minutes into the weekly review. Near the top of the search terms report sit three lines: dog walker, dog walker app, and dog walker near me. Say, as a labeled example, they cost 26 dollars between them last week with no requests. You check the live negatives. Dog walking is already there, as a phrase negative. You wonder how these got through.

A. Add dog walker near me as an exact negative, so that search never shows your ad again.
B. Add walker and walkers as broad negatives on the Services We Do Not Sell list, attach it, and log the change.
C. Do nothing. Dog walking is already a negative, so these must be flukes.

What happens with each: With C, nothing changes, because dog walking as a phrase negative blocks only searches containing those two words in that order, and dog walker is a different word. Negatives do not stretch. As long as any campaign can match these searches, they keep coming, and the money keeps going. With A, that one exact search stops, and next week dog walker and dog walkers irvine keep spending, because an exact negative blocks one search only. With B, the whole family closes in one move: walker and walkers, in any search, in every campaign the list touches. And you note one more thing in the log: the ended experiment campaign once carried dog walking as a broad keyword, which explains where these searches came from. Retiring it and adding the list closes both doors. The lesson: block families, not single searches, and remember a negative only blocks the exact words it says.

## The Short Version

Weekly: check the counting first, then search terms by cost, top down, a verdict on each line.
Build six shared negative lists, and keep training and brand words off them.
Negatives do not stretch, and a broad negative needs every word, so cheap cheapest blocks almost nothing.
Price and competitor searches are allowed on purpose; revisit that with data.
Monthly: balance the services, read the city scorecard, refresh ads, and check sitter capacity first.
