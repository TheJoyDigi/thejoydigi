# Chapter 13: The Owner's Seat

Here is the honest news at the end of this book. Since November 14, 2026, you run this. Brandon is out, and the account is yours, operated day to day by you, working with Claude. Steph advises on strategy. Johnny owns the business.

That makes this chapter more important, not less. When the operator and the owner were two people, the monthly review had a natural referee. Now both chairs are yours, and nobody will tap you on the shoulder when operator-you has let something slide. So the owner's seat becomes a discipline you schedule. Once a month, for fifteen minutes, you stop operating and judge. Not tweak, not rebuild. Judge. This chapter gives you the five numbers, nine questions, and the flags, red and green, that tell you whether the account is still the one Chapter 10 set out to run. And it is short enough to share with Johnny and Steph when you are done.

## The five numbers

Fifteen minutes is enough because only five numbers truly matter.

Number one: spend. What did the account spend, and was it near the budget you approved? At 20 dollars a day, a full month is about 608 dollars. Spend far under that usually means something is capped, paused, or broken. Spend over it often means a second campaign is drinking from the same budget, which has happened here before.

Number two: clicks. Clicks are a knock on the door, not a booking. You are checking that the flow is alive and moving with the spend. At about 4 dollars a click, 608 dollars buys roughly 145 clicks.

Number three: booking requests. The count in the Conversions column, checked against the requests that arrived in the portal. Once a call asset exists, calls from ads sit beside it. This is the number the whole book exists to move.

Number four: cost per booking request. Spend divided by requests. Compare it with what a request is worth, the ceiling of about 161 dollars that Chapter 8 worked out, and with your own target, far below that. Watch its direction over three months, not its mood in one week.

Number five: requests by city. Where did the requests come from? Read the four sitter cities first: Irvine, Costa Mesa, Santa Ana, San Juan Capistrano. Then the three border cities under test, Tustin, Lake Forest, and Mission Viejo, with one extra question: did a sitter in a neighboring city accept those dogs? This is the number that ties the ad account back to real calendars.

## One month, read aloud

Here is a month read the owner's way. Every number is a labeled example, not a report from the real account.

Say the month spent 590 dollars against 608. Spend is on plan. It bought 140 clicks, at a little over 4 dollars each. The flow is alive. The Conversions column shows 9 booking requests, and the portal shows 11 in all, so Google's count sits sensibly below the total. Divide, and one request cost about 66 dollars, down from 80 the month before. The direction is right, and it sits far below the ceiling. Then the map: Irvine 5, Costa Mesa 2, San Juan Capistrano 1, Santa Ana none, and Lake Forest 1, a dog an Irvine sitter accepted. The border test produced one real booking, and Santa Ana's zero becomes a question for the city scorecard, not a panic.

Four minutes, and the month's story is clear: money on plan, counting healthy, cost improving, one border city earning its keep.

## Your baseline: November 19, 2026

Your first real reading was not that tidy. Keep this one as the baseline every future month gets compared against.

Spend: in the thirty days to November 19, 2026, the live campaign spent 419 dollars, but the whole account spent about 626, because an ended experiment campaign still listed as enabled had spent about 167 dollars from the same budget, and a dynamic search campaign, paused since, about 40 more. Number one fails, not on size but on where the money went. Clicks: 99 on the live campaign, about 4 dollars and 23 cents each. Number two passes. Booking requests: the Conversions column read zero. Two requests appeared only in All conversions, outside what Google bids on. So number three could not be trusted, and number four did not exist. You cannot divide by a number you do not trust. Number five could not be read at all, with no requests to place on the map.

The story was clear anyway: the spending machine works, the counting machine does not, and the housekeeping needs doing. That is why Chapter 10's ninety days start with tracking.

## The nine questions

Numbers start the review. Questions finish it. Ask these, in this order, of the account and your change log.

One. What did we spend, and what did it buy in booking requests? Two. Does the Conversions column agree with the portal, and when did we last place a test request? Three. What did one request cost, compared with the last two months? Four. Which sitter city gave us the most requests, and which gave none that should have? Five. Did the border cities produce requests that a neighboring sitter accepted, and is the test still inside its window and its spending limit? Six. What waste did we cut, and what did the search terms teach us? Seven. What is the biggest change we made, why, and what happened after? Eight. Which sitters are close to full, and are we still pushing ads toward their calendars? Nine. If we ran one test next month, what would it be?

None of these needs you to know where a setting lives. All of them need the account to be run honestly. And notice what they reward: counting checked, waste cut, changes logged, capacity respected. The questions you ask monthly become the priorities you run by weekly.

If you ever add a brand campaign, report it on its own line and keep it out of the main judgment. Those people already knew your name. Boarding and daycare are the honest score.

## Red flags: the smells of trouble

You do not need to diagnose these in the seat. You need to notice them and ask. The first five have already happened in this account.

Conversions at zero for weeks while spend continues. As of November 19, 2026, the Conversions column read zero for thirty days while the campaign spent hundreds of dollars. Real demand almost never falls to zero. Tags break all the time. A silent Conversions column is a broken instrument until a test request proves otherwise. This flag outranks all the others.

A finished campaign still listed as enabled. The "Max Clicks vs Max Conv" experiment ended, yet it still showed as enabled and had shared the live campaign's budget. Anything listed as enabled can spend. If it is done, pause it or remove it.

A negative keyword that blocks nothing. The live campaign carried cheap cheapest as one broad negative, which only blocks searches containing both words. It looks like protection and does almost nothing. Any negative written as two words that rarely appear together deserves a second look.

Generic callouts. Phrases like Pet Care With Love and Trusted In-Home Dog Care fill space without saying anything a searcher can check. Proof belongs there: a rating, a review count, a free request.

No call asset. As of November 19, 2026, the phone number appears on no ad, even though calls from ads are set up to count. Either add the asset, or decide on purpose not to and write down why.

Then the classic ones. Clicks from places nobody chose: money from people in cities you never meant to reach, or a border city test running past its end date with no verdict. Note what is not on this list: spend in Tustin, Lake Forest, or Mission Viejo. That was a deliberate choice on November 11, 2026. The flag is not that they spend. It is spending there without ever asking whether those cities produce requests a neighboring sitter can serve. One ad group eating the budget, often a near me group with a low Quality Score, while the tight city groups share the crumbs. Ads pointing at the homepage instead of the city landing page. And a slow one: cost per request rising three months in a row with no story attached. Not a crisis. A summons.

## Green flags: what healthy looks like

Good news has a shape too. The Conversions column moves every week and agrees with the portal. Requests arrive steadily at a cost that holds or gently improves. The waste share of the search terms report shrinks month over month, because the shared negative lists keep growing. A border city gets a clear verdict, in or out. The change log is filled in, with results written beside the changes. Budget moves toward sitters with room and away from full calendars, on purpose. And the best green flag of all: your own log catching a problem before the numbers force it into the open, with a proposed fix written beside it.

Look back once, and you will see this whole book was building your fifteen minutes. Chapter 1, the business behind the ads, became the capacity question: who has room for the bookings we are buying? Chapter 2, the search moment, became: are we showing up for searches with a real trip and a real deadline? Chapter 5, the auction, became: is our relevance still beating bigger budgets? Chapter 6, the ads, became: do our ads say what is true, with fresh proof? Chapter 7, location and assets, became: are we present only where we can serve, with the phone one tap away? Chapter 8, bidding and budgets, became: is our bidding chasing clicks or bookings, and does our data support that chase yet? Chapter 9, tracking, became the simplest question of all: can we trust the counting? Chapter 10, the setup, became the ninety day plan you measure progress against. Chapter 11, the loop, became: is the loop still turning, with a log to prove it? Chapter 12, growing up, became: is growth arriving in the right order, and does every test have an end?

Twelve chapters. Twelve questions. Fifteen minutes. That is the whole job, and nobody can do it better than the person who knows which sitters are real, which cities are home, and what a booking is worth.

## The last page: what compounds here

You have a book of your own about this, so you will recognize the shape of the closing idea. Value, Created argues that durable advantage is rarely bought in one grand move. It is built from small, real advantages, delivered honestly, repeated until they compound into something a competitor cannot simply purchase.

Google Ads, done The Blue Sock way, is the same argument in a smaller arena. A headline that says the exact city and the exact service. A location setting that refuses to pay for the wrong map. A negative list that grows by a few true entries every Monday. A landing page that answers the search in its first breath. A sitter with fifty-plus honest reviews standing behind a modest bid. None of these is a hack. Each is worth almost nothing alone. Together, repeated weekly, season after season, they become a local relevance so specific, so earned, that a national chain with ten times the budget has to overpay for every click it wins against you, and it still cannot offer the one thing your ad promises truthfully: a real neighbor, in the searcher's own city, who will treat their dog like family.

That is the moat. Not the budget. Not the tool. The accumulated, compounding fit between a real business and the people searching for exactly it.

So keep the loop turning. Ask the nine questions. Protect the counting. Fund the cities where your sitters actually live, test the edges on purpose, and let the map grow only as fast as the roster does. The account will take care of the clicks. You take care of the trust, and the trust, as it has from the first page of this book, takes care of everything else.

## Play It: Your Move

It is your monthly fifteen minutes. The report looks cheerful at first glance. Clicks are up a third, and cost per click is down. Then you look closer. The Conversions column has read zero for four straight weeks, while All conversions shows three booking requests. Most of the new clicks came from the dog boarding near me group, whose Quality Score is still 1. Spend is right on plan.

A. Celebrate the cheaper clicks and raise the budget, since traffic is clearly growing.
B. Ask the questions: why are requests landing in All conversions but not Conversions, when was the last test request, and what are the near me clicks actually buying? Fix the counting before next month.
C. Switch to Maximize Conversions today, so Google starts hunting for requests instead of clicks.

What happens with each: With A, you pour more money into a machine that cannot see its own results. In a labeled example, another 300 dollars goes mostly to the cheapest clicks Google can find, which are cheap for a reason. With C, you hand Google a goal it has recorded zero times. It hunts blind, in a direction it cannot measure, and you lose the clean click data you had. With B, you do the owner's actual job. You put the broken counting at the top of the list, where Chapter 9 says it belongs, and you hand operator-you a clear question about the near me group, to answer with evidence before next month. The fix still happens. It just happens diagnosed, logged, and aimed. The lesson: in the owner's seat, questions are the steering wheel.

You run the account now, so judge it fifteen minutes a month like an owner anyway.
Five numbers: spend, clicks, booking requests, cost per request, requests by city.
