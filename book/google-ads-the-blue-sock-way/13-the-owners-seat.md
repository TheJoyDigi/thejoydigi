# Chapter 13: The Owner's Seat

Here is the honest news at the end of this book. As of November 14, 2026, you do run this. Brandon is out, and the account is yours now, operated day to day by you, working with Claude.

That makes this chapter more important, not less. When the operator and the owner were two people, the monthly review had a natural referee. Now both chairs are yours, and nobody will tap you on the shoulder when operator-you has been neglecting something. So the owner's seat becomes a discipline you schedule, not a meeting someone else calls. Once a month, for fifteen minutes, you stop operating, sit in the owner's seat, and judge. Not tweak, not rebuild, not second guess settings. Judge. This chapter gives you the five numbers to look at, the exact questions to ask of the account and your own change log, and the flags, red and green, that tell you whether the machine you built in Chapter 10 is still the machine that is running.

## The five numbers

Fifteen minutes is enough because only five numbers truly matter, and every one of them can be read in plain words.

Number one: spend. What did the account spend this month, and was it near the budget you approved? Spend far under budget is not thrift. It usually means something is capped, paused, or broken. Spend over your ceiling is a conversation. Spend roughly on plan is the first green light.

Number two: clicks. How many people clicked? Clicks are the raw material. You are not judging clicks on their own, because a click is only a knock on the door. You are checking that the flow is alive and roughly proportionate to the spend. Spend up a third with clicks flat is a question forming. Spend and clicks moving together is normal weather.

Number three: booking requests. How many real requests came in, the primary conversion from Chapter 9, counted once each, with the 60-second calls beside them. This is the number the whole book exists to move. Everything else is commentary on this number.

Number four: cost per booking request. Spend divided by requests, the plainest arithmetic in advertising. This is your unit economics ruler from Chapter 12, the number you compare against what a booking is actually worth at 55 to 89 dollars a night across several nights. Watch its direction over three months, not its mood in one week. A cost per request drifting slowly upward, month after month, is the account asking for attention. A single odd month is usually a holiday, a heat wave, or a sitter on vacation.

Number five: requests by city. The same requests, sorted by where they came from. Irvine should carry the most weight, because Irvine carries the roster. A city producing requests with no sitter capacity behind them, or a covered city producing nothing at all, is information you can act on in one sentence. This is the number that connects the ad account back to the real business, the calendars, the neighborhoods, the map you actually serve.

Read those five, in that order, once a month, and you have seen the account the way an owner should. Everything else in the dashboard is detail the operator in you watches during the week.

## One month, read aloud

To make the fifteen minutes concrete, here is a month read the owner's way, with every number a labeled example, not a report from the real account.

Say the month spent 1,470 dollars against an approved 1,500. Spend is on plan, first check passed. Those dollars bought 340 clicks, at a little over 4 dollars a click, right around the national pet category estimate. The flow is alive. Those clicks produced 31 booking requests, calls included. Divide, and one request cost about 47 dollars. Against last month's example figure of 52, the direction is right, and against what a multi-night booking is worth, the economics hold. Then the map: Irvine brought 17 of the requests, San Juan Capistrano 5, Costa Mesa 6, Santa Ana 3. Every request came from a city with a sitter, and the smallest city still produced, which matters, because Santa Ana's three requests are Olivia's whole solo calendar filling exactly as designed.

That reading took about four minutes, and it already tells you the month's story: money on plan, requests real, cost improving, geography honest. The remaining minutes belong to the questions, and if anything in the five numbers had smelled wrong, the questions are where it would have to explain itself.

## Your first real month in the seat

Your first real reading was not the tidy example above. It was the audit of November 14, 2026, the day you took the account over, and it is worth keeping here as the baseline every future month gets compared against.

Spend: 611 dollars in thirty days, on plan against a 20 dollar daily budget. Number one passes. Clicks: 155, at 3 dollars and 94 cents each, right around the national pet category estimate. Number two passes. Booking requests: here the reading broke. The campaign columns said zero. The conversions report said two, valued at a dollar each. The headline conversion's tag was inactive, and no conversion action in the account was verifiably recording. So number three could not be read at all, which means number four, cost per booking request, did not exist either. You cannot divide by a number you do not trust. And number five, requests by city, had its own warning: the live campaign was spending in Lake Forest, Mission Viejo, and Tustin, cities with no sitters, so some of the map money was buying bookings that cannot happen.

Four minutes, and the month's real story was clear: the spending machine works, the counting machine does not, and the map leaks. That ordering, counting first, then the map, became your first work plan as the account's manager. Every monthly reading from here on is you checking whether that story has changed.

## The nine questions

Numbers start the review. Questions finish it. Ask these, in roughly this order, in these plain words, of the account and of your own change log, and a fifteen minute review will surface anything that matters.

What did we spend this month, and what did it buy us, in booking requests? What did one booking request cost us, and how does that compare with last month and with the month before? Which city gave us the most requests, and which city gave us none that should have? Did any of our spend land in cities where we have no sitter? What waste did you cut this month, and what did the search terms report teach you? What is the biggest change you made, why did you make it, and what happened after? Is tracking healthy, and when did you last place a test booking and a test call to prove it? Which sitters are close to full, and are we still pushing ads toward their calendars? And finally: if you had one test to run next month, what would it be?

Nine questions. None of them requires you to know where a single setting lives. All of them require the account to be run honestly, because every one of them is hard to answer well from an account that is being neglected, and now the person answering is you. Notice, too, what the questions reward. They reward cutting waste, logging changes, checking tracking, and respecting capacity. The questions you ask monthly become the priorities the account is run by weekly. That is not an accident. That is management.

One more habit while you are in the seat: keep brand traffic out of the main judgment, the way Chapter 12 warned, and report it in one line of its own. Its clicks are cheap and its requests are easy, because those people already knew your name. The Boarding and Daycare numbers are the honest score. Judge those.

## Red flags: the smells of trouble

You do not need to diagnose these. You need to notice them and ask. Each one maps to a failure you have already studied somewhere in this book.

Spend with no requests. Money went out, clicks arrived, and the request count is zero or nearly zero across weeks, not days. Either the traffic is wrong, or the counting is broken, or both. This flag outranks all others. Nothing else gets discussed until it is explained.

Clicks from cities with no sitters. The geography report shows real money landing in Anaheim, Tustin, or anywhere else the roster does not reach. Somewhere, a location setting drifted, a campaign expanded, or an exclusion was never added. Every dollar there bought a booking that cannot happen.

One ad group eating the budget. A single group, often a broad and hungry one, consuming most of the spend while the tight, proven groups share the crumbs. The account has not been rebalanced in a while, and the starvation is invisible until you ask for the split.

Tracking gone silent. Conversions drop to zero while clicks carry on as usual, and nobody can tell you when a test was last placed. Real demand almost never falls off a cliff overnight. Tags break constantly. Silence from tracking is a broken instrument until proven otherwise, and Smart Bidding flying on it is spending blind.

Ads pointing at the homepage. Somewhere in the account, ads send boarding searchers to the front door of the site instead of the boarding page. It sounds small. Chapter after chapter in this book has argued it is not: the page must say back what the searcher said, in the first screen, or the click was half wasted before it began.

And a slow one: cost per request rising three months in a row with no story attached. Not a crisis. A summons.

## Green flags: what healthy looks like

Good news has a shape too, and you should be able to recognize it as fast as trouble. Requests arriving steadily at a cost per request that holds its line or gently improves. The waste share of the search terms report shrinking month over month, because the negative lists are growing. A new city producing its first requests inside its patience window. The change log filled in, in plain words, with the results written beside the changes. Budgets that move toward full sitters slowly and away from full calendars on purpose. And perhaps the best green flag of all: your own change log surfacing a problem before the numbers force it into the open, with a proposed fix written beside it. An account reported that honestly is an account being run well, even in a month when the numbers dip.

## Every chapter was a question in disguise

Look back once, and you will see this whole book was building your fifteen minutes. Chapter 1, the business behind the ads, became the capacity question: who has room for the bookings we are buying? Chapter 2, the search moment, became: are we showing up for the searches that carry a real trip and a real deadline? Chapter 3, the map of the account, became: does our structure still match our services and our cities? Chapter 4, keywords, became: are our words the words our neighbors actually type?

Chapter 5, the auction, became: is our relevance still beating bigger budgets, or are we quietly outbidding ourselves? Chapter 6, the ads, became: do our ads still say what is true, with fresh proof? Chapter 7, location and assets, became: are we present only where we can serve, with the phone one tap away? Chapter 8, bidding and budgets, became: is our bidding chasing clicks or bookings, and is that still the right chase for our data? Chapter 9, tracking, became the simplest question of all: can we trust the counting? Chapter 10, the setup, became your comparison point whenever the running account drifts from the blueprint. Chapter 11, the loop, became: is the loop still turning, every week, with a log to prove it? Chapter 12, growing up, became: is our growth arriving in the right order, sitter first, ads second, automation last?

Twelve chapters. Twelve questions. Fifteen minutes. That is the whole job, and it is a job nobody can do better than the person who knows which sitters are real, which cities are home, and what a booking is worth.

## The last page: what compounds here

You have a book of your own about this, so you will recognize the shape of the closing idea. Value, Created argues that durable advantage is rarely bought in one grand move. It is built from small, real advantages, delivered honestly, repeated until they compound into something a competitor cannot simply purchase.

Google Ads, done The Blue Sock way, turns out to be the same argument in a smaller arena. A headline that says the exact city and the exact service. A location setting that refuses to pay for the wrong map. A negative list that grows by a few true entries every Monday. A landing page that answers the search in its first breath. A sitter with fifty honest reviews standing behind a modest bid. None of these is a hack. Each one is worth almost nothing alone. Together, repeated weekly, season after season, they become a local relevance so specific, so earned, that a national chain with ten times the budget has to overpay for every click it wins against you, and it still cannot offer the one thing your ad promises truthfully: a real neighbor, in the searcher's own city, who will treat their dog like family.

That is the moat. Not the budget. Not the tool. The accumulated, compounding fit between a real business and the people searching for exactly it.

So keep the loop turning. Ask the nine questions. Protect the counting. Fund the cities where your sitters actually live, and let the map grow only as fast as the roster does. The account will take care of the clicks. You take care of the trust, and the trust, as it has from the first page of this book, takes care of everything else.

## Play It: Your Move

It is your monthly fifteen minutes. The report looks cheerful at first glance: total requests are up. Then your eye catches the split. The Brand campaign produced most of the growth, the Boarding cost per request has risen for the third straight month, and one loose daycare ad group has quietly taken half the daycare budget. Total spend is right on plan.

A. Celebrate the totals, approve another month as is, and maybe raise the budget, since requests are up.
B. Ask the questions: brand reported separately, why has boarding's cost risen for a quarter, what is that one ad group buying, and what is the fix before next month?
C. Order the loose ad group paused on the spot and move its budget to boarding before the meeting ends.

What happens with each: With A, the blended total keeps smiling while the core quietly weakens. Three more months of drift, at a labeled example, costs you a few thousand dollars of boarding spend at worsening prices, and you find out in the season when boarding matters most. With C, you might be right about the ad group, but you have just made an operator's decision with an owner's information, in the middle of a meeting, with no log entry and no chance for the data to argue back. Sometimes that pause amputates your best experiment. With B, you do the owner's actual job. You separate the easy brand wins from the honest score, you put the three month trend on the table where it cannot hide, and you hand operator-you a question that must be answered with evidence before next month. The fix still happens. It just happens diagnosed, logged, and aimed. The lesson: in the owner's seat, questions are the steering wheel. Keep your hands on it, and off the machinery.

## The Short Version

You run the account now, so judge it fifteen minutes a month like an owner anyway.
Five numbers: spend, clicks, booking requests, cost per request, requests by city.
Nine plain questions, asked of the account and the log, cover waste, geography, tracking, changes, and capacity.
Red flags: spend without requests, wrong cities, one hungry ad group, silent tracking, homepage ads.
Small relevance advantages, repeated weekly, compound into a moat money cannot buy.
