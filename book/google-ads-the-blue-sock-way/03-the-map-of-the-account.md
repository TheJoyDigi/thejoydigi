# Chapter 3: The Map of the Account

Every Google Ads account has the same shape.

It does not matter if the advertiser is a global airline or a six sitter dog boarding platform in Irvine. The shape is a set of nesting boxes, one inside the next, and each box has its own job.

If you learn the map, you can walk into any account, including your own, and know where you are standing. You can find where the money is set, where the targeting is set, and where the words live. If you do not learn the map, every screen looks like every other screen, and every conversation about ads turns foggy fast.

So let us build the map slowly, box by box, and then draw your map, the Blue Sock map, in words.

## The biggest box: the account

The outermost box is the account itself.

One business, one account. The account holds the things that belong to the whole business. Your billing. Your basic identity as an advertiser. The people who are allowed in, and what each of them is allowed to do.

For The Blue Sock, there is one account, under the business, for the whole platform. Not one account per sitter. Not one account per city. Johnny does not have an account. Irvine does not have an account. The platform advertises as one business, because the platform is what a pet parent is trusting when they book any of your sitters.

That sounds obvious, and it is, but beginners break it all the time. They open a second account for a side project, or an old account from an earlier attempt sits forgotten. Then budgets split, history splits, and nobody sees the whole picture. One account, one business. Everything else in this chapter lives inside that box.

Inside the account sit the campaigns.

## The next box: campaigns

A campaign is the box where the big decisions live.

Here is the rule that organizes everything. One campaign per goal.

A goal means a real business aim, with its own money and its own audience. Boarding is a goal. Daycare is a goal. They are different services, bought for different reasons, often by the same person at different moments. A trip creates a boarding need. A busy work stretch creates a daycare need. Because the goals differ, they get separate campaigns, and that separation is the spine of your whole map.

What lives at the campaign level? The settings that should apply to everything inside that campaign, all at once.

Budget lives here. Each campaign carries its own average daily budget. Say, as an example, a boarding campaign is given thirty dollars a day. That is roughly nine hundred dollars a month, and it is a teaching example, not your real budget. The point is the level. Budget is not set on a single ad. It is set on the campaign, and every ad group inside shares it. That sharing matters. It means the groups inside a campaign are teammates drawing from one pool, and a hungry group can drink more than its share if you are not watching.

Location lives here too. The campaign says where in the world its ads may show. For your boarding campaign, that means the cities where your sitters host, and the areas right around them. Set the location wrong at this level, and every keyword and ad inside inherits the mistake, faithfully, at full speed. Chapter 7 gives location its full treatment.

Bidding lives here as well. Bidding is the instruction you give Google about what to hunt for. Clicks, at first, while an account is learning. Booking requests later, once the counting is trustworthy. You set that strategy at the campaign level, and Chapter 8 will walk through the switch in detail.

Timing and campaign wide choices live here too. When ads may run. Which kinds of searches the campaign is for. The campaign is the manager of its box. It does not write the ads. It sets the conditions they work under.

One more campaign fact, because beginners feel it in their wallet. Splitting has a cost. Each campaign needs its own budget, and modern bidding learns from results. A campaign that gets only a trickle of clicks and requests learns slowly and judges poorly. Ten tiny campaigns, each starved, will usually lose to two healthy ones. Hold that thought. It comes back when we talk about how simple to keep all this.

## The next box: ad groups

Inside each campaign sit the ad groups.

An ad group is a tight theme. One theme, one group. That is the whole rule, and it does more work than any other sentence in this chapter.

Tight means the searches inside one group are close cousins. They want the same thing. They would be happy landing on the same page and reading the same ad. Boarding in Irvine is a theme. Daycare in Costa Mesa is a theme. Boarding for a dog who needs a solo stay is a theme. Boarding and daycare mixed together is not a theme. It is a drawer full of unrelated things, and Google cannot write a good ad for a drawer.

Why does tightness matter so much? Because of what an ad group holds. Two things live at this level, and they must match each other.

The first is keywords, which get the next section and a full chapter after that.

The second is the ads themselves. The actual text a searcher reads. When a group is tight, one set of ads can speak directly to every search in it. A pet parent searching for daycare in Costa Mesa sees words about daycare in Costa Mesa. Not boarding words. Not generic dog words. Her own words, echoed back, which is the oldest persuasion trick in this book and still the best one.

When a group is loose, the ads have to become vague to cover everything, and vague ads earn vague glances at full price. Structure is not tidiness for its own sake. Structure is what lets your ads sound specific, and specific is what a worried pet parent clicks.

## The smallest pieces: keywords and ads

At the bottom of the map, inside each ad group, sit the two pieces the searcher actually touches.

Keywords are your guesses about what people type. A keyword is not the search itself. It is a standing instruction that says, when someone searches something like this, consider showing my ad. Dog boarding Irvine is a keyword you would hold. Doggy daycare Costa Mesa is another. Puppy daycare might be another, in the right group. Chapter 4 takes keywords apart properly, including the different ways a keyword can match a search, loosely or tightly.

Ads are the text that shows when a keyword wins its moment. In modern search ads, you do not write one fixed ad. You write a set of headlines and descriptions, and Google mixes them into combinations for each search. Chapter 6 covers writing them well. For the map, what matters is the address. Ads live in ad groups, beside the keywords they serve, and an ad can only be as relevant as the group it lives in allows.

So the full nesting, said once, in one breath. The account holds campaigns. Each campaign holds ad groups. Each ad group holds keywords and ads. Four levels. That is the entire skeleton of Google Ads, and every advanced topic later in this book is just detail hung on those four bones.

## The Blue Sock map, drawn in words

Now draw yours. Remember, this is the teaching map. It shows what a great Blue Sock setup looks like. The live account looks different, and the next section describes it as of November 19, 2026. Chapter 10 lays the two maps side by side.

At the top, one account. The Blue Sock.

Inside it, two main campaigns to start.

The first campaign is Boarding. Its goal is overnight stays, your flagship service. Its budget is its own, because boarding money should not get quietly eaten by daycare clicks. Its locations are your boarding cities. Its bidding hunts, first for clicks and later for booking requests, as later chapters explain.

Inside the Boarding campaign, the ad groups follow your roster and your real themes. One ad group for boarding in Irvine, where Johnny, Jennifer and David, and Denise host, and where your deepest reviews live. One for boarding in Costa Mesa, built around McKenna and the dogs she is best with. One for boarding in Santa Ana, built around Olivia and the solo stay promise. One for boarding in San Juan Capistrano, around Mariia and Nataliya. A theme group can also cut by need where the need is strong, like a group for senior dog boarding, speaking to medication, calm homes, and experienced hands. Each group holds its own keywords, city words and need words together, and its own ads that echo those exact words.

The second campaign is Daycare. Its goal is daytime care. Its budget is separate and, at the start, likely smaller, because a daycare day, from about thirty five dollars, is worth less on day one than a multi night boarding stay. Its locations mirror where daycare is actually offered. Inside it, an ad group for daycare in Irvine. One for daycare in Costa Mesa, where McKenna's daycare is fifty four dollars a day. One for daycare in San Juan Capistrano, where Mariia and Nataliya's daycare is forty two dollars a day. Same pattern as boarding. City and service, tight and clear.

Notice what is not on this map. There is no dog walking campaign, because you do not sell dog walking. There are no campaigns for Anaheim, Tustin, Newport Beach, or Mission Viejo, even though people search there and even though your own blog writes helpful guides for some of those cities. Reading a free guide costs you nothing. Paying for a click in a city with no sitter costs you every time. A training campaign for McKenna could join the map later, as a third box with very different math, and Chapter 12 will give it that moment. It does not belong in the starter map.

Count the boxes and feel how manageable it is. One account. Two campaigns. A handful of ad groups in each. A focused set of keywords and ads in every group. A beginner can hold that whole map in his head, and an owner can check it in minutes. That holdability is a feature, not a limitation.

## The live account today

Now compare the drawing to the real thing. This is the live account as of November 19, 2026.

It has one live campaign, named The Blue Sock, Dog Sitting, Search with Geo's. It runs on Google search only. Its budget is twenty dollars a day, about six hundred dollars a month. Its ad schedule runs from eight in the morning to ten at night. That schedule is a campaign level setting, the kind this chapter said lives at the top.

The campaign has seven locations. Four are the cities where your sitters host: Irvine, Costa Mesa, Santa Ana, and San Juan Capistrano. Three are Tustin, Lake Forest, and Mission Viejo. Those have no sitter, but they border the sitter cities, and they were added back on November 11 at Steph's suggestion. Wildomar was removed on November 17.

Inside that one campaign sit ten ad groups. Eight are city groups, a boarding group and a daycare group for each of the four sitter cities. Two are general groups, one for overnight searches like near me and Orange County, and one for daycare near me. All sixty seven keywords are exact match.

Three old campaigns sit beside it. One is an experiment that has ended but still shows as enabled. One is a paused dynamic search campaign. One is a paused Performance Max campaign.

So the live map already follows this chapter's best idea, tight city and service groups. What it does differently is keep boarding and daycare in one campaign. At twenty dollars a day, that is a fair choice, because one budget feeds one pool of learning. Think of the two campaign map above as the recommendation for when budget and tracked booking requests can feed both.

## A word from the modern playbook

If you read current Google guidance, you will hear a push that can sound like it argues with this chapter. The push is toward simpler, more consolidated structures. Fewer campaigns. Broader groupings. Let Smart Bidding, Google's automated bidding, work across bigger pools of searches and results, because automated bidding learns better with more data feeding it.

That push is real, and for big advertisers with thousands of conversions, it is often right. Tiny fragments starve the learning. One keyword per ad group, the old craftsman style, mostly belongs to the past. Rebuilding structure again and again is also costly, because reorganizing an account throws away accumulated history and can hurt performance while things relearn. Get the shape roughly right early, then let it be.

But consolidated does not mean shapeless, and this is where your size becomes the deciding factor. Google's own guidance still tells advertisers to build ad groups around narrow themes, with keywords and ads that match that theme. For a small local account, the tight theme is not old fashioned fussiness. It is how a boarding search gets a boarding ad, how a Costa Mesa search gets Costa Mesa words, and how a solo stay search finds Olivia instead of a generic promise. Your budget is not big enough to buy your way out of vague ads.

So the Blue Sock answer is both, in the right order. Consolidate at the campaign level. Two strong campaigns, not twelve weak ones, so budget and learning stay concentrated. Stay tight at the ad group level, where relevance is made. Simple spine, specific groups. That balance is the modern structure for a business your size, and every later chapter assumes it.

When you sit down with the real account, this map is your ruler. Not to demand it match box for box, but to ask good questions. Where does boarding money live. Which group is this keyword in, and does the ad in that group say what the searcher said. Are we paying to show anywhere our sitters are not. Good structure makes those questions easy to ask, and hard questions easy to answer.

Next, we go one level deeper, into the smallest and most powerful piece on the map. The keywords, the exact words standing between a stranger's late night worry and your sitters' doors.

## Play It: Your Move

You are sketching the account on a blank page, as an example exercise, before a single dollar moves. A helpful friend offers three plans.

Plan A. One campaign called Dogs. Inside it, one ad group called Everything, holding boarding keywords, daycare keywords, puppy words, senior words, and every city mixed together. One set of ads tries to cover it all.

Plan B. Eighteen campaigns. One for every service and city pair you can think of, including cities with no sitter yet, each with a small slice of budget, in this example about two dollars a day each.

Plan C. Two campaigns, Boarding and Daycare, each with its own budget and its sitter cities. Inside each, tight ad groups by city and need, each group with matching keywords and matching ads.

Which map do you choose?

Here is what happens with each.

If you picked A, the machine runs, and that is the danger. It looks simple and it spends happily. But the daycare searcher reads a boarding flavored ad. The Santa Ana searcher sees no hint of the solo stay that would have won her. The ads go generic because they must, clicks cost full price, and the wrong fit clicks anyway. You pay boarding prices to learn that a drawer is not a theme.

If you picked B, you built a map no budget can feed. Two dollars a day buys a click or two, then the campaign goes dark until tomorrow. No group gathers enough results for the bidding to learn anything. Worse, several campaigns point at cities where you cannot host a dog at all, so their rare clicks are guaranteed waste. The map looks thorough. The money starves in eighteen places at once.

If you picked C, you chose the spine from this chapter. Boarding money stays boarding money. Each search meets an ad written for that search. The budgets are big enough to learn from, and every targeted city has a real home in it. This map can be managed in a weekly half hour, and it can grow by adding a group or a campaign only when a new sitter or a new goal truly arrives.

The lesson. Structure is strategy you can draw. Consolidate the campaigns, keep the themes tight, and never build a box for a city or a service you cannot sell.

## The Short Version

The map nests four levels: account, campaigns, ad groups, then keywords and ads.
Budget, location, and bidding live at the campaign level, one campaign per goal.
Ad groups are tight themes, and keywords and ads live inside them together.
The recommended map is two campaigns, Boarding and Daycare. The live account is one campaign with city groups.
Consolidate campaigns for learning, keep groups tight for relevance.
