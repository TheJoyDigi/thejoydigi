# Chapter 6: Ads People Want to Click

Here is a shift that makes writing ads much easier. You do not write an ad anymore. You write the pieces of many ads, and Google assembles them.

Years ago, an advertiser wrote one headline and a couple of lines of text, and that exact ad showed to everyone, every time. That world is gone. Today, the standard format is called a responsive search ad. You hand Google a set of headlines and descriptions, and for each search, Google picks the combination it believes fits that person, that phone, and that moment best. The next searcher, ten seconds later, may see a different mix of your words making the same promise.

At first that can feel like losing control. Sit with it a moment longer and you will see it is a gift. You could never guess which three lines will move a nervous puppy owner versus the organized owner of a senior dog. Now you do not have to guess. You supply strong, honest, varied pieces, and the system learns which combinations earn clicks and bookings. You are no longer polishing one perfect ad. You are a coach putting a good team on the field, where every player can play next to every other player. Every piece must stand on its own, and make sense next to any other piece.

## The pieces, and their sizes

Let us get the hard numbers out of the way, in words.

A responsive search ad can hold up to fifteen headlines. Each one gets a maximum of thirty characters. That is characters, not words. Thirty characters is roughly five or six short words. Dog Boarding in Irvine is twenty-two characters. That fits, with room to spare. A headline like The Finest Overnight Dog Boarding Experience in All of Irvine does not fit, and honestly, it should not. The limit is a kindness. It forces you to say one true thing at a time.

You can also write up to four descriptions. A description is the longer line under the headlines, and each gets up to ninety characters, about fifteen words. Enough for one full idea, with a little proof inside it. Something like this fits: Boarding in a real Irvine home. Meet your sitter first, then send a booking request.

When your ad shows, Google displays up to three headlines and up to two descriptions at once. The first two headline spots and the first description are the reliable ones. The third headline and second description show when Google judges there is room, but they are not guaranteed. So build as if the first pieces must carry the message alone, because sometimes they will. Service and city early. The clearest promise in the first description.

You can have up to three responsive search ads running in one ad group, and Google suggests at least two strong ones. For a small account, one excellent ad per group, cared for properly, beats three lazy ones. Grow to two when you have genuinely different things to say, like a general Irvine boarding ad and one that leans into senior dog care.

## The first rule: echo the searcher

Back in the auction chapter, the chain bid three dollars with a generic ad and lost to your two dollars and a perfect match. Now you get the writing rule that made the match perfect.

Echo the searcher's own words.

When a person types dog boarding Irvine and the first headline she sees says Dog Boarding in Irvine, something small and powerful happens. She thinks, this is the thing I asked for. Not something like it. The thing itself. That recognition takes half a second, and it is worth money, because relevant ads get clicked, and clicked ads earn better expected click-through, better relevance, and cheaper clicks in the auction. Chapter 5's quality math starts right here, at the keyboard.

So every ad group starts with keyword headlines, headlines that contain the words of the searches in that group. The Irvine boarding group leads with Dog Boarding in Irvine. The Costa Mesa daycare group leads with Doggy Daycare in Costa Mesa. This is not a lack of creativity. It is the ad doing its first and most important job: answering the exact question that was just asked.

Notice what this rule forbids. It forbids falling in love with your own slogans at the top of the ad. The Blue Sock has real brand color, lines like, do not choose a sitter, choose an experience, and the lovely idea of a dogcation. Those lines belong on the website, where a visitor has time to enjoy them. In a thirty character headline fighting for a click at ten at night, the searcher's words beat your poetry. The answer goes first.

Echoing also means echoing the need, not just the service. In a senior dog group, Gentle Care for Senior Dogs echoes what that owner typed and what she fears. In Olivia's solo stay world, Solo Stays, One Dog at a Time echoes the search for a house with no other dogs. The more precisely a headline mirrors the worry, the more the ad feels written for one person. And it was. Write each headline for the one person whose search triggers that group.

## Proof beats promises

After the keyword headlines, your next headlines should carry proof. Any advertiser can promise great care. Words like best and number one are cheap, and readers slide right past them. Proof is specific. Proof can be checked. Proof is where a small platform with real numbers quietly outguns a big chain's adjectives.

Here is your proof shelf. Use these facts, because they are true.

Five point zero stars. The platform carries a 5.0 star rating. Five Star Rated Sitters fits in a headline with room to spare. Local reviews persuade for you, in your neighbors' voice rather than your marketing's.

In-home care. Boarding and daycare happen in a sitter's home, not a kennel facility. Boarding in a Real Home separates you from every cage and concrete run in the results, in five words. For many pet parents, that single fact is the decision.

One-on-one attention. Your sitters care for a small number of dogs, and Olivia cares for exactly one family at a time. One-on-One Dog Care is a complete argument in nineteen characters.

Fast replies. Most booking requests on the site are answered in under two hours, and Olivia's profile promises replies in about an hour. Fast, Personal Replies tells a stressed traveler she will not be left refreshing her phone. Speed of reply is trust, delivered early.

Named sitters. This one is yours alone. A big platform cannot put a person in a headline and mean it the way you can. Johnny has fifty-plus five-star reviews behind his name. A headline that names a sitter, or simply says Meet Your Sitter First, turns an anonymous transaction into an introduction. The Blue Sock sells trust in specific local people. Headlines should sound like it.

Price honesty. Boarding starts at fifty-five dollars a night, and daycare starts at forty-two dollars a day. Boarding From $55 a Night will not win every click, and it should not. It wins the right clicks, and saves you paying for people whose budget was never going to work. An honest price is a filter, and filters protect small budgets.

Spread these proof points across different headlines. Remember, Google mixes. If all your proof lives in one headline, most combinations show no proof at all. If five headlines each carry one true proof point, nearly every combination contains something convincing.

## A boarding ad, taken apart

Let us look at one example ad, slowly. This is a teaching example for the Irvine boarding group, not a claim about the live account.

The keyword headlines come first. Dog Boarding in Irvine. Overnight Boarding in Irvine. Irvine Dog Boarding, Home Stays. Each echoes the search in slightly different words, because people type it different ways.

Then the benefit headlines. Boarding in a Real Home. No Kennels, Just Home Care. Your Dog Sleeps in a Home. One-on-One Attention. Each answers the kennel fear from a different angle, and any one can sit next to a keyword headline and make sense.

Then the proof headlines. Five Star Rated Sitters. Trusted Local Dog Sitters. Fast, Personal Replies. Boarding From $55 a Night. Meet Your Sitter First.

Count those up and you have used most of your fifteen slots without repeating yourself. That is harder than it sounds. Google asks for unique headlines, not fifteen costumes on the same sentence. Dog Boarding in Irvine, Best Dog Boarding in Irvine, and Top Dog Boarding in Irvine are not three headlines. They are one headline and two echoes, wasting slots that could have carried your senior dog story, your solo stay story, and your price. When you catch yourself rearranging the same words, ask a different question. What else is true here that this owner cares about? Puppy experience. Senior experience. A certified trainer in Costa Mesa. The roster is your copy bank.

The descriptions get to breathe a little. Ninety characters each, two likely to show. A good first description completes the promise and names the next step: Boarding in a real Irvine home with a local sitter. Send a booking request and meet first. A good second carries proof: Local sitters rated 5.0 stars. One-on-one care for senior and anxious dogs. Neither begs or shouts. They sound like a calm, competent neighbor, which is exactly the brand. And notice the action they name. Not buy now. Not instant book, because your bookings are requests a human accepts. Send a request. Meet the sitter. That honesty is not a limitation. It is the message.

## Boarding words for boarding. Daycare words for daycare.

This rule sounds obvious, and it is violated in small accounts everywhere. The ad must match its ad group, and the ad group must match the search.

A boarding search is written by a person leaving town. Her words are overnight, stay, trip, nights. Her ad should answer in that language, and her click should land on boarding. A daycare search is written by a person managing her week. Her words are daytime, while I work, pickup tonight. Her ad should talk about days, not nights, and land on daycare.

Mixing them feels efficient. One ad, both services, why not? Because the searcher experiences it as a near miss. She searched for daycare and got an ad about overnight stays. For half a second she wonders whether you even offer what she needs, and in that half second, the competitor whose headline says Doggy Daycare earns the click. Relevance is fragile, and Chapter 5 showed you what broken relevance costs. One group, one promise, kept from the search box to the page.

## Pinning: the control you should mostly leave alone

At some point, every advertiser discovers pinning, and it feels like getting the steering wheel back. Pinning lets you force a headline into a specific position. Pin one to position one, and Google must show it first, every time. If echoing matters so much, you might think, I will just pin Dog Boarding in Irvine first and be done. Google will let you. Google also advises against heavy pinning, in plain language, and for once the platform's advice and the advertiser's interest point the same way.

Here is why. Every pin shrinks the number of combinations Google can test. Pin one headline and you have not lost much. Pin three headlines to three fixed positions and you no longer have a responsive ad. You have an old fashioned fixed ad in a modern costume, and you have thrown away the learning that makes the format valuable. The system cannot discover that Fast, Personal Replies outperforms your favorite headline on Tuesday evenings, because you never let it try. Heavy pinning also tends to drag down your ad strength rating.

So the house rule is simple. Do not pin by default. Write headlines so strong, and so clearly ordered by importance, that the natural result is already good. If you ever pin, pin sparingly and for a real reason, like a must-show legal or defining fact. A smarter version is pinning two or three good options to the same position, so Google still has a choice within that spot. Start from zero pins, and make every pin argue for its existence.

## Ad strength: a guide, not a god

As you build an ad, Google grades your homework in real time with a rating called ad strength. The grades run from incomplete, through poor and average, up to good and excellent. Google is rating the completeness, variety, and relevance of your pieces. Did you provide enough headlines? Are they genuinely different? Do they include your keywords' words? Are the descriptions unique?

Treat that rating the way Chapter 5 taught you to treat Quality Score. It is a helpful coach, not a judge. Ad strength does not decide whether your ad can run, and it is not a lever inside the auction. An ad rated average that produces booking requests at a good cost is a good ad. An ad rated excellent that never produces a request is a well formatted failure.

That said, the coach is usually right about the basics. If your rating says poor because you supplied four headlines and two say the same thing, fix it. Aiming for good is sensible hygiene. Google reports that advertisers who lifted an ad from poor to excellent saw, on average, about fifteen percent more clicks and conversions, a real prize for writing work. Fifteen distinct headlines is effort, but you do it once, for free, and it pays you back on every auction afterward.

Just do not let the grade bully you into nonsense. The rating cannot tell whether a headline is true, or whether Meet Your Sitter First captures your brand better than a keyword stuffed sentence that scores one notch higher. You are the owner. The grade advises. You decide. A good rating with honest words beats an excellent rating with hollow ones.

## The writing habit that ties it together

If you take one method from this chapter, take this one. Write the ad backwards from the search.

Write down the exact searches the group exists for. Dog boarding Irvine. Senior dog boarding Irvine. Read them out loud. Write your first headlines by answering them, in their own words. Then ask what the person behind each search is afraid of, and write benefit headlines that calm that fear. Then open your proof shelf, the stars, the home, the named sitters, the fast replies, the honest price, and write the proof headlines. Then write descriptions that name the next step. Finally, read every headline against every other headline, the way Google will mix them. If two side by side sound silly, or like a promise you cannot keep, rewrite one. If any headline repeats another's idea in fresh clothes, replace it with a new truth from the roster.

Do that, group by tight group, and your ads stop sounding like advertising. They start sounding like the answer to a specific neighbor's specific question, offered by specific local people with the reviews to prove it. That is why people click them. Not because they are clever. Because they are about the searcher's own dog, in her own city, on the nights she actually needs help. An ad like that does not need to shout. It just needs to show up and say the true thing clearly.

## Play It: Your Move

It is a Friday afternoon, and you are approving a new ad for the Costa Mesa daycare group. McKenna's daycare is the heart of this group, fifty-four dollars a day, with a certified trainer watching puppies and energetic dogs burn off steam. The group exists for searches like doggy daycare Costa Mesa.

Three draft ads are on the table.

A. The brand ad. Its top headlines are Choose an Experience, A Dogcation Awaits, and The Blue Sock Way. It is charming, and it sounds like the website. But none of its headlines say daycare, Costa Mesa, or puppy.
B. The echo ad. Its top headlines are Doggy Daycare in Costa Mesa, Puppy Daycare With a Trainer, and Daycare From $42 a Day. Wait. That last price is the platform's daycare floor from another city, not McKenna's rate.
C. The echo ad, fixed. Same as B, but the price headline now says McKenna Daycare, $54 a Day, and the descriptions invite the reader to send a booking request and arrange a meet and greet.

Here is what happens with each.

If you pick A, you have written a lovely ad for people who already know you, and shown it to people who do not. Searchers type daycare Costa Mesa and see a slogan. The half second of recognition never happens. In this story, the ad earns clicks at about half the rate of the echo ads, and the clicks it wins cost more, because Chapter 5's tax applies. A month of example daycare budget buys a fraction of the meet and greets it should have. Charm is not the enemy. Charm in the wrong position is.

If you pick B, the shape is right and the detail is wrong, and in a trust business the detail is the product. A Costa Mesa owner clicks expecting daycare at forty-two dollars, lands on McKenna at fifty-four, and feels the small sting of a bait and switch, even though nobody meant to deceive her. Some of those owners leave. You paid for those clicks, and worse, you spent a little of the reputation the platform runs on. An almost right ad can cost more than a weak one, because a weak ad wastes money and an almost right ad wastes trust.

If you pick C, every headline is doing honest work. The searcher's words are echoed. The trainer proof is specific and true. The price is McKenna's real price, so the click arrives pre-sold and pre-qualified. In this story, this ad wins the best click rate of the three, pays the least per click, and produces booking requests from puppy owners who already know the rate, the city, and the sitter's name before they reach the page. There was no trick in it. Just care, applied thirty characters at a time.

The lesson: echo the search, prove it with true specifics, and check every number.

## The Short Version

A responsive search ad is built from up to fifteen headlines of thirty characters and descriptions of ninety characters, and Google mixes the pieces for each search.
Lead with headlines that echo the searcher's own words, service, city, and need.
Fill the rest with proof, not adjectives: 5.0 stars, in-home care, one-on-one attention, fast replies, named sitters, and honest prices.
Keep boarding ads in the boarding group and daycare ads in the daycare group, and never repeat the same idea in fresh clothes.
Pin sparingly if at all, and treat ad strength as a helpful guide, never as the boss of your own true words.
