# Chapter 12: Growing Up

A young account asks one question: does this work at all? A growing account asks a harder one: where can this grow without breaking what makes it work? The Blue Sock does not sell software. Every booking lands on a real person's calendar, in a real home, with a real dog on the rug. That is the charm of the business, and it is also the ceiling on its ads. This chapter walks the stages of growth in order, and at each stage, the ad move that fits, the money logic underneath it, and the trap that waits beside it.

## Stage one: more sitters in a city you already cover

The healthiest growth is the quiet kind, and you are living it right now. As of November 19, 2026, Irvine has three sitters: Johnny, Jennifer and David, and Denise, who is new to the roster. Nothing about the campaign needed rebuilding when she joined. The Irvine ad groups already exist, and the keywords already have history. What changed is capacity, and capacity is permission to spend a little more.

Raise budgets in steps, not leaps. As a working rhythm, an increase of about twenty percent, held for a few weeks while you watch the cost per booking request, teaches you far more than doubling ever will. If requests rise and the cost per request holds, the city was hungry and the new sitter is being fed. If the cost per request climbs hard, Google has run out of good searches in that city at that budget. Step back down. The budget dial is a question, and the account answers it in data.

One more wrinkle: a new sitter has no reviews yet, and your ads sell proof. Johnny has fifty-plus five-star reviews behind him. Denise and Olivia, both new, have none yet. Early on, let a new sitter ride the platform's general trust, the 5.0 rating from 86 pet parent reviews shown on the city pages as of November 19, 2026, and let the meet and greet do the persuading. As her own reviews land, her name can start appearing in ads the way the proven names do. Growth inside a covered city is mostly patience wearing a budget.

## Stage two: new cities, the rule and the test

The rule this book has argued from the start is sitter first, ads second. An ad is a promise that someone nearby can take the dog. If the promise runs ahead of the roster, you pay for clicks that end in apologies.

Now the honest part. As of November 19, 2026, the live account already targets three cities with no sitter: Tustin, Lake Forest, and Mission Viejo. That was a deliberate choice, made on November 11 at Steph's suggestion, and it is not a mistake to clean up. It is a different idea, worth testing. These three cities border the sitter cities. A parent in Lake Forest may live only a short drive from an Irvine sitter. If those parents book with neighboring sitters, the border cities are not empty map. They are an extension of real coverage.

A test is only a test if it has a question, a judge, and an end. The question: do these three cities produce booking requests that a sitter in a neighboring city accepts? The judge: the geography report, which shows where the people who clicked actually were, read beside the portal, which shows whether a sitter took the dog. The end: give it a fixed window, say sixty to ninety days, and a spending limit you write down, as a labeled example, no more than a fifth of the monthly budget. At the end, each city earns its place or leaves the targeting. Wildomar shows the decision can go either way: Trudy hosts there, and on November 17, 2026, Johnny decided to take it out of the ads anyway.

The sitter first rule still governs real launches. When the first sitter signs in a brand new city, say Tustin, the playbook runs in this order. Week zero, no new ads: her profile goes live, with photos and rates. Then the site gets city landing pages for Tustin, the way Irvine, Costa Mesa, Santa Ana, and San Juan Capistrano have theirs. Then Tustin boarding and daycare ad groups join the existing campaign, using the proven pattern, service word plus city word, with ads adapted from the best city ads. Joining the existing campaign matters. A newborn campaign of its own would start with no history and a starving budget. Inside the proven one, Tustin borrows the account's learning while it builds its own.

Then be patient on a schedule. Sixty to ninety days before any verdict. A new city starts with low search volume, a sitter with no reviews, and ads with no local proof. Judged at day twenty, every launch looks like a failure. Judged at day ninety, the honest ones have found their footing, and the hopeless ones have told you so cheaply.

## A brand campaign, and whether you need one

As of November 19, 2026, no brand campaign exists. Your own name, blue sock and thebluesock along with the old name, is a negative keyword in every campaign. So when someone searches for The Blue Sock, no ad of yours shows. Only your free listing does.

A brand campaign would bid on your own name. There are real reasons to want one. An ad sits above the free listing, and on a phone, above can mean the whole first screen. It lets you choose the message and the page. And it protects you if a competitor or a big marketplace starts buying the top spot on your name.

There are also real reasons to wait. With twenty dollars a day, every brand click is a dollar not spent finding new parents. And brand clicks flatter the numbers. They convert beautifully, because these people already chose you, and if they are blended into the main results, the whole account looks smarter than it is.

So check before you build. Search your own name now and then, on a phone, in your service area. If nobody else's ad sits above you, your free listing is doing the job. If a competitor appears, a small brand campaign becomes cheap insurance. If you build one, three rules. Keep the brand negatives on the other campaigns, so brand searches go only to the brand campaign. Keep brand words off any shared negative list, so the brand campaign does not block itself. And report its results on a separate line, always.

## Performance Max, honestly

Performance Max is a campaign type with no keywords and no ad groups in the Search sense. You give it a budget, locations, a conversion goal, and a bundle of headlines, descriptions, photos, and video. Google assembles ads and places them across Search, Maps, YouTube, Display sites, Gmail, and Discover, moving money toward whatever seems to work.

The black box has gained windows in recent years. As of late 2026, you can give it campaign level negative keywords. You can exclude your brand, so it does not harvest your own name and claim the credit. You can see reporting by channel, and search term insights showing roughly what people typed. And when a search exactly matches an exact match keyword in your Search campaign, the Search campaign wins. Your core stays yours.

The catch is appetite. Performance Max learns from conversions across all those surfaces, and it optimizes hard toward whatever you call a conversion. An account recording zero in its Conversions column, as this one did on November 19, 2026, has nothing to feed it. It would be steering by guesswork, across the whole internet.

As of that date, the account holds a paused Performance Max campaign with no spend in thirty days. Leave it paused. Whether to remove it entirely, so nobody can switch it back on by accident, is a small housekeeping call. If you remove it, its history stays in the reports. And when its time comes, build a fresh one rather than reviving this one, because the old one was set up before the counting was fixed, with goals and assets chosen for a different account. Its time comes only when Search produces steady, verified booking requests and you have money that Search cannot usefully absorb. Then one Performance Max campaign, beside Search and never instead of it, with brand exclusions set, the shared negative lists applied, Presence location only, and its conversion goal set to booking requests and nothing looser.

The same patience applies to Google's expansion setting for Search campaigns, which loosens keyword matching and rewrites ad text on its own. Leave it off until the conversion data is real.

## Remarketing: the gentle reminder

Between the search and the booking there is a gap, and in this business it is wide. A parent plans a June trip in March. She reads three sitter profiles, gets interrupted by dinner, and books nothing. She is not a lost customer. She is a future customer with a calendar.

The lists that would let you reach her exist, but they are small. As of November 19, 2026, the "All visitors" website list holds about 120 people for Search. Google sets a minimum number of active people before a list can serve, and a list this small sits at or near that bar. Rather than trust a remembered number, read the list's status column in the audience manager, which says plainly whether it can serve. The four Customer Match lists, built from customer emails, all show a serving size of zero. The live campaign excludes the All Customers list, but with nobody matched, that exclusion does nothing yet.

So remarketing is a later move. Keep the lists collecting quietly. Add a list for people who viewed sitter profiles and one for people who started a request and never sent it. Handle customer data under California privacy law, with consent, because Customer Match and remarketing are exactly the uses that law cares about. When the lists grow large enough to serve, a small campaign can show a gentle reminder: a real photo, a familiar sitter's face, a line about booking the summer stay before calendars fill. Keep the budget small and the frequency modest, and measure it humbly. Remarketing nudges bookings more than it creates them.

## The exception: McKenna's training

Every rule in this book was calibrated on boarding nights. McKenna's training breaks the calibration, in your favor, and it deserves its own math.

McKenna, in Costa Mesa, is a Certified Professional Dog Trainer. Her training comes as add-ons. Sessions run 50 dollars for thirty minutes and 90 for an hour. Board and Train is an add-on on top of the boarding nights: 600 dollars for one week, 1,125 for two weeks, 1,650 for three. So a two week Board and Train is fourteen nights of boarding at about 70 dollars, roughly 980 dollars, plus the 1,125 dollar add-on. About 2,100 dollars in one booking, roughly six times the average boarding stay of about 360 dollars from Chapter 8.

That changes what a lead may cost. It also changes the clock. Nobody buys a three week program from a phone search at midnight. They read, they call, they meet the trainer, they think. Weeks can pass between the first click and the booking, so a training campaign must be judged on a longer window.

The build, when its time comes, is a small campaign of its own, so its slow, valuable conversions never get averaged into boarding's faster ones. Keywords in McKenna's territory: dog training costa mesa, board and train orange county, puppy training costa mesa. Ads lead with her credential. And one setting must change first. As of November 19, 2026, dog training and board and train are both negative keywords on the live campaign, along with obedience, because training is not advertised. Those negatives must come off anything attached to the training campaign the day it launches, or it will block its own searches. Later, small, and separate. That is the whole training strategy.

## Protecting the unit economics at every stage

Growth tempts you to admire totals. Totals lie politely. The number that keeps you honest is the cost of one booking request against what one booking is worth.

You know the worth side better than any dashboard. A boarding booking is usually several nights, at 59 to about 89 dollars a night in the ad cities, plus the odd premium experience, and the platform takes its service fee, 11 percent on bookings up to 400 dollars, less on larger ones. A daycare customer is worth less per visit and more per year, because she comes back. A Board and Train booking can be worth six ordinary stays. Set your ceiling per service from those real numbers, and let no stage of growth talk you above it. If the cost per request rises through the ceiling, the answer is never "spend more and hope." It is cut waste, narrow geography, or pause and think, in that order.

## When to hire help, and what never leaves your hands

Today the doing is yours and Claude's, Steph advises on strategy, and Johnny owns the business. At some size, the weekly loop, new city launches, and reporting add up to a real job. That is the moment more help earns its keep, and the split is the strategy.

Hand over the doing. Someone else can run the weekly search terms pass, keep the negative lists, build new city ad groups inside the playbook, refresh ads, and assemble the monthly scorecard. That work is skilled, repetitive, and well served by a checklist and a change log.

Keep the judging. Which cities The Blue Sock enters and when, because that starts with sitters, not ads. The budget ceiling, and the unit economics it comes from. The monthly scorecard, read with your knowledge of whose calendar is actually full. Whoever controls both the spending and the report card will, with the best intentions, eventually grade their own homework. Your fifteen minutes a month, the subject of the final chapter, keeps that honest.

## The classic failure modes, by size

Small, the failures are leaks: no negatives, wrong cities, homepage landing pages, and counting that does not count. This account has met several of them already. Medium, the failures are ambitions: scaling a budget before the sitters behind it have room, launching three cities in a quarter because the first one worked, letting a brand campaign's easy numbers flatter a weakening core. Large, the failures are delegation without guardrails: a Performance Max campaign spending across the internet with no brand exclusion and a loose goal, several hands making unlogged changes, and an owner who stopped reading the scorecard because the totals kept going up.

Notice the thread. At every size, the failure is the same failure wearing a bigger coat: spending that drifts away from real capacity, real geography, and real bookings. The cure is the same too, and it is the next chapter.

## Play It: Your Move

Good news lands in spring: your first Tustin sitter signs, profile live, calendar wide open. Tustin has been in the targeting as a border city test since November, so some Tustin data already exists. The same week, a dashboard prompt suggests switching on the paused Performance Max campaign to "accelerate growth everywhere." Say, as a labeled example, the account now produces about fifteen solid booking requests a month from Search.

A. Launch Tustin ad groups and switch on Performance Max together. Strike while the momentum is hot.
B. Launch Tustin the playbook way: landing pages, ad groups inside the existing campaign, a written budget limit, ninety days of patience. Leave Performance Max paused.
C. Skip Tustin ad groups and put the growth budget into Irvine, where the reviews already are.

What happens with each: With A, Performance Max spreads money across YouTube, Gmail, and the open web on fifteen conversions a month, and two changes landed at once, so you cannot tell whether Tustin itself worked. In a labeled example, a few hundred dollars of the first month buys impressions you cannot trace to a single Tustin booking. With C, you stay safe and small, and the new sitter's calendar fills slowly by luck. With B, Tustin moves from border test to covered city with clean data, and you can even compare its requests before and after the sitter arrived. The lesson: growth has an order. Sitter first, Search second, scale third, automation last, and every test gets a question and an end date.

## The Short Version

Grow inside a covered city first: capacity up, budget up in small watched steps.
New cities: sitter first is the rule, and the border city test is judged by the geography report.
No brand campaign exists today; check who bids on your name before you build one.
Performance Max stays paused until Search produces steady, verified booking requests.
Training is the high ticket exception, and its live negatives must come off the day it launches.
