# Chapter 12: Growing Up

A young account asks one question: does this work at all? A growing account asks a harder one: where can this grow without breaking what makes it work? The Blue Sock does not sell software. Every booking lands on a real person's calendar, in a real home, with a real dog on the rug. That is the charm of the business, and it is also the ceiling on its ads. This chapter walks the stages of growth in order, and at each stage, the ad move that fits, the money logic underneath it, and the trap that waits beside it.

## Stage one: more sitters in a city you already cover

The healthiest growth is the quiet kind. A second sitter signs up in Costa Mesa, or a new Irvine sitter opens her calendar. Nothing about your campaigns needs rebuilding. The ad groups already exist. The keywords already have history. What changes is capacity, and capacity is permission to spend a little more.

Raise budgets in steps, not leaps. As a working rhythm, an increase of about twenty percent, held for a few weeks while you watch the cost per booking request, teaches you far more than doubling ever will. If requests rise and the cost per request holds near its old level, the city was hungry and the new sitter is being fed. If the cost per request climbs hard, Google has run out of good searches in that city at that budget, and the extra money is buying worse clicks. Step back down. The budget dial is a question, and the account answers it in data.

One more wrinkle: a new sitter has no reviews yet, and your ads sell proof. Fifty-plus reviews stand behind Johnny. A brand new sitter stands behind none. Early on, route the new sitter through the platform's general trust, the 5.0 stars and the forty-nine reviews, and let the meet-and-greet do the persuading. As her own reviews land, her name can start appearing in ads the way the proven names do. Growth inside a covered city is mostly patience wearing a budget.

## Stage two: launching a new city

Here is the rule that governs every expansion in this book, in the order it must happen: sitter first, ads second. Never the reverse. An ad is a promise that someone nearby can take the dog. If the promise runs ahead of the roster, you pay for clicks that end in apologies.

The worked example is sitting in your own service map. North Orange County, Anaheim, Fullerton, Huntington Beach, Newport Beach, has no Blue Sock sitter today. Neither do Tustin and Mission Viejo, closer to home. Your blog already writes guides for some of these cities, and those pages collect free search traffic, which quietly proves something valuable: people there are searching. That is demand evidence, free of charge. It is not permission to advertise.

So the launch playbook, when the first Tustin sitter signs, sounds like this. Week zero, no ads: her profile goes live, photos, rates, the "Especially Great For" tag, everything a landing page needs. Then the Tustin ad groups join the existing Boarding and Daycare campaigns, using the proven keyword pattern, service word plus city word, and ads adapted from the Irvine winners with the city name and her details swapped in. Joining the existing campaigns matters. A newborn campaign of its own would start with no history and a starving budget. Inside the proven campaigns, Tustin borrows the account's learning while it builds its own.

Give the launch a small, labeled budget of its own to watch, something like 200 dollars a month as an example, ring-fenced in your notes even if it shares the campaign budget. Then be patient on a schedule. Sixty to ninety days before any verdict. A new city starts with low search volume, a sitter with no reviews, and ads with no local proof. Judged at day twenty, every launch looks like a failure. Judged at day ninety, the honest launches have usually found their footing, and the hopeless ones have told you so cheaply. Cheap information is the entire point of the small budget.

What success looks like is modest and specific: the city produces its first few booking requests at a cost you can live with, the sitter's calendar shows her first stays, and her first review exists. Then, and only then, does Tustin graduate into the normal monthly scorecard and start competing for budget with Irvine on equal terms.

## The Brand campaign, in full

Chapter 10 gave the Brand campaign a paragraph. It deserves a section, because it is the cheapest insurance in the account and the easiest to misread.

The campaign bids on your own name: the blue sock, blue sock dog boarding, the blue sock irvine, thebluesock.com, and their close variants. Why pay for clicks on your own name, when you would rank first for free? Three reasons. First, position. An ad sits above the free listing, and on a phone, above can mean the whole first screen. Second, protection. Nothing stops a competitor or a big marketplace from bidding on your name, and some do. Without your own ad there, the top spot on your own name belongs to whoever bought it. Third, control. The ad lets you choose the message and the destination, sending a returning customer straight to the sitters page instead of wherever the free listing happens to point.

The economics are friendly. Searches for your name are few and the clicks are cheap, often well under the category average, because your relevance to your own name is perfect. A small budget, the 5 dollars a day from Chapter 10's labeled example, usually goes mostly unspent. Treat that as the premium on an insurance policy, not waste.

One warning, and it matters for Chapter 13. Brand clicks convert beautifully, because these people already chose you. If brand results are blended into your main numbers, the whole account looks smarter than it is. Judge the Brand campaign separately, always, and never let its easy wins excuse a sloppy month in Boarding or Daycare.

## Performance Max, honestly

Sooner or later, Google will suggest Performance Max, and the suggestion will sound like magic. Here is the plain version. Performance Max is a campaign type with no keywords and no ad groups in the Search sense. You give it a budget, a location, a conversion goal, and a bundle of materials, headlines, descriptions, photos, video, called an asset group. Google then assembles ads and places them everywhere it owns: Search, YouTube, Display sites, Discover, Gmail, and Maps, moving money toward whatever seems to work.

The honest 2026 update is that the black box has gained windows and doors. You can now exclude your brand, so Performance Max does not spend its days harvesting your own name searches and claiming the credit. You can give it campaign level negative keywords, up to ten thousand of them, though they bite mainly on its Search and Shopping placements. You can see reporting by channel, and you can offer it search themes, hints copied from your proven Search keywords. And a reassurance that matters: when a search exactly matches a keyword in your Search campaigns, the Search campaign still wins that auction first. Your core stays yours.

The catch is appetite. Performance Max learns from conversion volume across all those surfaces, and practitioner guidance in 2026 clusters around needing roughly thirty conversions a month, and a budget in the neighborhood of 3,000 dollars a month, before it can be judged fairly. Read those as experienced guidance, not Google law. A young Blue Sock account sending it a handful of booking requests a month is not feeding it. It is tipping it. Worse, Performance Max optimizes hard toward whatever you call a conversion. If raw form fills count, including the spam and the accidental, it will find you more of exactly that, at scale, across the whole internet.

So the verdict for this book: Performance Max is a Phase 2 tool. Phase 1 is Search, run cleanly, with tracking you trust. When the account is producing steady, verified booking requests and you have growth money beyond what Search can absorb, then one Performance Max campaign, alongside Search and never instead of it, with brand exclusions set, the starter negative list applied, Presence location only, and search themes copied from your winners. Guardrails on day one, or not at all.

## Remarketing: the gentle reminder

Between the search and the booking, there is a gap, and in this business the gap is wide by nature. A dog parent plans a June trip in March. She reads three sitter profiles, likes Olivia, gets interrupted by dinner, and books nothing. She is not a lost customer. She is a future customer with a calendar.

Start collecting that future during Phase 1, because audiences are free to build even before you spend a cent on them. Build lists of everyone who visited the site, people who viewed sitter profiles, people who started a booking request and never sent it, and, with proper consent, past customers. Those lists sit quietly gathering names while Search does its work.

Later, a small remarketing campaign shows those people a gentle reminder: real photos, a familiar sitter's face, a simple line about booking the summer stay before calendars fill. In 2026 the home for that kind of visual advertising is Demand Gen, Google's campaign type for YouTube, Discover, and Gmail, where you choose the channels yourself. Keep the budget small, the tone neighborly, and the frequency modest. A reminder says "we are still here." Shown nine times a day, it says something else entirely. And measure it humbly. Remarketing gets credit for bookings it nudged, not bookings it created, and that is fine. Nudges, at the moment a trip becomes real, are worth paying a little for.

## The exception: McKenna's training

Every rule in this book was calibrated on boarding nights at 55 to 89 dollars. McKenna's training breaks the calibration, in your favor, and it deserves its own math.

From the internal rate card: obedience sessions at 50 and 90 dollars, and Board and Train programs at 600 dollars, 1,125 dollars, and 1,650 dollars. Hold the middle program against boarding. At McKenna's boarding rate of 70 dollars a night, one 1,125 dollar Board and Train booking equals roughly sixteen nights of boarding. One booking. Sixteen nights.

That changes what a lead may cost. A booking request that would be ruinous at 100 dollars for a two night stay is perfectly sane if a fair share of training inquiries become four figure programs. It also changes the clock. Nobody impulse buys a 1,650 dollar training program from a phone search at midnight. They call, they talk, they meet the trainer, they think. Weeks can pass between the first click and the booking, so the campaign must be judged on a longer window and on calls and consultations, not just submitted forms.

The build, when its time comes, is a small campaign of its own, so its expensive, slow, valuable conversions never get averaged into boarding's fast, cheap ones. Keywords follow the same pattern, in McKenna's home territory: dog training costa mesa. Board and train orange county. Puppy training costa mesa. Reactive dog training, which is her specialty and the roster's only training offer. Ads lead with the credential in her own profile headline, Certified Trainer, Board and Train Available. Later, small, and separate. That is the whole training strategy, and it is enough.

## Protecting the unit economics at every stage

Growth tempts you to admire totals: total spend, total requests, total bookings. Totals lie politely. The number that keeps you honest is the cost of one booking request against what one booking is worth.

You know the worth side better than any dashboard does. A boarding booking is usually several nights, at 55 to 89 dollars a night, plus the odd add-on experience, and the platform takes its service fee, eleven percent on bookings up to 400 dollars, less on larger ones. A daycare customer is worth less per visit and more per year, because she returns weekly. A training booking can be worth sixteen boarding nights. Set your ceiling per service, from those real numbers, and let no stage of growth talk you above it. If the cost per request rises through the ceiling, the answer is never "spend more and hope." It is cut waste, narrow geography, or pause and think, in that order.

## When to hire help, and what never leaves your hands

At some size, the weekly loop, the launches, and the reporting add up to a real job. That is the moment help earns its keep, and it is worth being precise about the split, because the split is the strategy.

Hand over the doing. An operator can run the weekly search terms pass, keep the negative lists, build the new city ad groups inside the playbook, refresh ads, and assemble the monthly scorecard. That work is skilled, repetitive, and well served by a checklist and a change log.

Keep the judging. Strategy stays with you: which cities The Blue Sock enters and when, because that decision starts with sitters, not ads. The budget ceiling stays with you, and so does the unit economics ruler it comes from. The monthly scorecard stays on your desk, read with your knowledge of whose calendar is actually full. An operator who controls both the spending and the report card will, with the best intentions in the world, eventually grade their own homework. Your fifteen minutes a month, the subject of the final chapter, is what keeps that honest.

## The classic failure modes, by size

Small, the failures are leaks: no negatives, wrong cities, homepage landing pages, counting every form fill as a win, including the spam, the job seekers, and the man who wanted grooming. Medium, the failures are ambitions: scaling a budget before the sitters behind it have capacity, launching three cities in a quarter because the first one worked, letting a brand campaign's easy numbers flatter a weakening core. Large, for a local business, the failures are delegation without guardrails: a Performance Max campaign spending across the internet with no brand exclusion and a raw form fill as its goal, three operators making unlogged changes, and a founder who stopped reading the scorecard because the totals kept going up.

Notice the thread. At every size, the failure is the same failure wearing a bigger coat: spending that drifts away from real capacity, real geography, and real bookings. The cure is also the same at every size, and it is the next chapter. Sit in the owner's seat, look at the few numbers that cannot lie to you for long, and ask the plain questions.

## Play It: Your Move

Good news lands in spring: your first Tustin sitter signs, profile live, calendar wide open. The same week, a dashboard prompt suggests a shiny new Performance Max campaign to "accelerate growth everywhere," and your blog's Tustin guide is already bringing in free visitors every day. Your account currently produces about fifteen solid booking requests a month from Search.

A. Launch Tustin ads and Performance Max together. Strike while the momentum is hot.
B. Launch Tustin the playbook way: ad groups inside the existing campaigns, a small ring-fenced budget, ninety days of patience. Keep Performance Max on the shelf.
C. Skip Tustin ads entirely and put the whole growth budget into Irvine, where the reviews already are.

What happens with each: With A, Performance Max spreads the budget across YouTube, Gmail, and the open web, fed by only fifteen conversions a month, and starts claiming credit for brand searches it was never excluded from. In our labeled example, 600 dollars of the first month buys impressions you cannot trace to a single Tustin booking, and you cannot tell whether Tustin itself worked, because two changes landed at once. With C, you stay safe and stay small: the new sitter's empty calendar fills slowly by luck, and the demand your own blog proved exists in Tustin goes to whichever competitor advertises there first. With B, Tustin gets a fair, cheap test with clean data, one change at a time, and Performance Max waits for the conversion volume that would let it succeed instead of flounder. The lesson: growth has an order. Sitter first, Search second, scale third, automation last.

## The Short Version

Grow inside a covered city first: capacity up, budget up in small watched steps.
New city means sitter first, ads second, a small budget, and ninety days of patience.
The Brand campaign is cheap insurance on your own name. Judge it separately.
Performance Max is a Phase 2 tool: powerful, data hungry, and only with guardrails on.
Training is the high ticket exception. One Board and Train can equal sixteen boarding nights.
