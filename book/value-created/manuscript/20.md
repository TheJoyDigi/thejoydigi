# Ownership

In 1976, three men started a company in a garage in California. Two of them you've heard of. The third was named Ronald Wayne. He was 41, the adult in the room, and 12 days later he sold his 10 percent of the new company back to the other two for $800.

Eight hundred dollars for 10 percent of Apple. If he had kept it, that stake would be worth billions today.

The easy lesson is "never sell." That isn't the lesson, because Wayne wasn't stupid. He was scared, and he had reason to be. The company had debts, the other two were twentysomethings with no money, and if it failed, the creditors could come after him personally. He traded a claim on the future for safety in the present, and almost anyone would have done the same.

But look at what the trade reveals. That 10 percent wasn't $800 of work. It was a claim, a legal right, to one-tenth of everything Apple would ever create, forever. Wages pay you for the hours you already worked. Ownership pays you for everything that happens next. That's this whole chapter in one story.

## Two contracts

Here's the whole idea in two short sentences. Workers rent out their time. Owners own the asset.

When you work for a wage, you sell hours, and the deal ends when the hours end. When you own equity, you hold a claim on all the future value the thing creates, whether you're in the room or not. That's why owners keep what workers create. It isn't a conspiracy, and it isn't a moral judgment. It's the structure of the deal.

Think of it as two different contracts. The wage contract says, "I'll pay you for your past." The ownership contract says, "I own a share of your future." The worker's contribution has an expiration date. The owner's claim doesn't.

## Same work, three times the money

Watch it happen with two engineers: same skill, same startup, same year.

Maya takes the safe offer: $160,000 a year in salary, benefits, no drama. Tom takes $110,000 plus half a percent of the company. For five years they sit ten feet apart and do roughly the same work.

Then the company sells for $400 million. Maya walks away with $800,000 in wages, and she earned every cent. Tom walks away with $550,000 in wages plus $2 million in equity: $2.55 million in all. Same work. Same years. Three times the money. The difference wasn't talent and it wasn't hours. Maya sold her time. Tom bought a piece of the future.

Here's the part people skip, because it makes the story less comfortable. Tom took the risk. If the company had died, and most of them do, Maya would have kept her $800,000 and Tom would have gotten almost nothing.

Owners keep the upside because owners absorb the downside. That's the trade at the heart of every equity deal. The wage is a floor under your life. Equity takes away the ceiling, but it takes away the floor too: you can't have the upside without standing where the downside can hit you.

## The pattern everywhere

It isn't just startups. Once you see the pattern, you see it everywhere.

The tenant pays rent every month for ten years and owns nothing at the end. The landlord collects the rent and owns the building, which is now worth twice what it was. Same building. The tenant funded it. The owner kept it.

The bestselling author earns a royalty, often around 15 percent. The publisher keeps the larger share, on every copy. The musician gets the advance. The label owns the masters.

In every case the worker creates the value and the owner keeps the larger share, because the owner holds the asset, and the asset is what compounds. Wages don't compound. You can't reinvest last year's salary into this year's salary. But assets compound. A business, a building, a brand, a share of a company: they grow while you sleep. We saw compounding at work with brand in the last chapter. Ownership is compounding with a legal contract attached.

And notice who takes the risk in each of these stories. The landlord is on the hook when the roof leaks. The publisher gambles on ten books to find one hit. The label fronts the recording costs. The owner keeps more because the owner can lose more. That doesn't make it fair. It makes it structural.

## The ownership ladder

So what does a worker actually do about it? You don't have to quit your job and found a startup tomorrow. Ownership is a ladder, and the first rung is small.

**Rung one: buy equity where you already work.** If your company offers stock, options or an employee purchase plan, take it seriously. It's the cheapest ownership you'll ever buy, because you already understand the business from the inside. Most people ignore the purchase plan and then buy a stranger's stock at full price. Don't be most people.

**Rung two: negotiate for a piece instead of just a raise.** A $10,000 raise is $10,000, once. A tenth of a percent of a growing company can pay you $10,000 a year for as long as it grows. When you're offered a choice between cash now and a piece of the future, at least do the math before you take the cash.

**Rung three: convert wages into assets on your own time.** The index fund is the simplest version. A rental property is the classic version. A small business, a book, a piece of software that sells while you sleep: those are the ambitious versions. The rule is simple. Every year, turn some of what you earned by working into something that earns without you working.

**Rung four, the one most people never climb: build something you own from the start.** Remember Marcus, the painter with the checklists. The checklists made the business run without him. But the deeper move was that he owned the business the checklists were running. The system was the engine. Ownership was the title deed.

## What you own

Here's the uncomfortable truth underneath all of this. Every dollar of value created flows somewhere, and the address it flows to is decided by ownership, not by effort. The hardest worker in the building doesn't keep the most. The owner does. That isn't a moral claim. It's plumbing, and plumbing doesn't care how you feel about it.

So the question isn't whether you work hard. You probably do. The question is what you own. If the answer is nothing, your income stops the day you stop, and everything you built keeps paying someone else.

> The workers will always create the value. Make sure you're standing on the side that gets to keep it.

## The takeaways

1. **Wages pay for your past; ownership pays for what happens next.** Workers rent out their time, and the deal ends when the hours end. Owners hold a claim on the future value the thing creates, whether they're in the room or not.
2. **Owners keep the upside because they absorb the downside.** Assets compound and wages don't, so the owner keeps the larger share, and can lose more too. That doesn't make it fair. It makes it structural.
3. **The question isn't how hard you work. It's what you own.** If you own nothing, your income stops the day you stop. Climb the ownership ladder one rung at a time.

## Try this

Write down everything you own that earns without you working. If the list is empty, that's your answer for now, not forever. Then pick the lowest rung you haven't climbed (the purchase plan at work, a piece instead of a raise, a first small asset) and take one concrete step toward it this week.

That closes this part of the book. Creating isn't enough; you have to capture what you create. Price it right. Moat it. Systematize it. Brand it. And finally, own it. Once you own something valuable, though, the fastest way to grow it is to stop trying to do everything yourself. Which brings us to the oldest leverage there is: do one thing well, and trade for the rest.
