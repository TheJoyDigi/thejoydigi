# Two Faces: Use vs. Exchange

Picture this. It's two in the morning and you're lost in the mountains. The temperature is dropping past freezing, the wind is cutting straight through your jacket, and you've started shivering in a way that honestly scares you.

Ahead of you, on a flat rock, someone has left two things: a thick, warm winter coat, and a stock certificate for a company worth $100 million. You can only carry one. Which do you take?

You take the coat. Instantly. No hesitation, no spreadsheet, no second opinion. On that mountain, on that night, the coat is worth more to you than $100 million. It might literally save your life.

Now rewind. Same two objects, but this time you're sitting in a warm office in July, and a buyer walks through the door. The coat? Maybe $10 at a garage sale. The stock certificate? Still $100 million. Nothing about the objects changed. Everything about their value did.

That's the riddle of this chapter. Every valuable thing has two faces, use value and exchange value, and confusing the two will cost you money, and sometimes much more than money.

## Naming the faces

**Use value** is what a thing does for you directly. The coat keeps you warm. Bread feeds you. A hammer drives nails. A song you love steadies your nerves before the big meeting. Use value is personal. It lives in the relationship between you and the thing, in one specific moment.

**Exchange value** is what a thing can be traded for: the price the market will pay. The stock certificate never kept anyone warm, never fed anyone, never sheltered anyone. Its use value is a piece of paper. But its exchange value is enormous, because of what other people will trade for what it represents.

The book's first idea is still true here. Value lives in people, not in things, and both faces are still about people. Use value is what a thing does for you. Exchange value is what it does for someone else, someone willing to trade with you.

Marginal utility is knocking on the door too. Remember the margin. The coat's use value isn't one fixed number stamped on the label. On the freezing mountain it's priceless. On a mild spring afternoon it's clutter in your closet. Same coat. The beholder changed and the moment changed, so the use value changed. Use value is always marginal; it depends on your situation right now.

Exchange value, though, looks steady. The certificate reads $100 million whether you're freezing or comfortable. That steadiness is useful. You can plan a life around it. Mortgages, savings and contracts all run on exchange value holding still long enough to trust.

Useful, yes. But also dangerous. That steady number starts to feel like the real value, the true value, the only value. And the moment you believe that, you start making expensive mistakes. There are four of them worth knowing by heart.

## The ring and the handbag

**The first mistake happens at the pawn shop.** Your grandmother passes away and leaves you her ring. To you it's irreplaceable. You watched her wear it every Sunday of your childhood. Its use value to you, its meaning, is enormous.

Then hard times hit, and you carry it to a pawn shop. The man behind the glass weighs the gold, checks the stone under his little microscope and offers you $200. Two hundred dollars for the most precious object you own.

Are you being robbed? No. You're watching the two faces split apart in real time. The ring's use value to you is sky-high. Its exchange value is whatever a stranger will pay for gold and a stone. The pawnbroker isn't buying your memories and couldn't resell them anyway. The pawnbroker is buying metal.

The cost of confusing the faces here is heartbreak, and bad decisions that echo for years. Some people sell the ring for $200 and feel cheated every time they think about it. Others swing the other way. They refuse to sell anything, ever, and slowly drown in objects they can't use, because inside their own heads they priced their memories at exchange value.

The rule is simple to say and hard to live. Never sell something with high use value at its exchange price unless you truly have to. And never expect the market to pay you for your feelings. The market doesn't have feelings. It only has bids.

**The second mistake is the status good.** The designer handbag. The luxury watch. The car with the badge on the front.

Ask the buyer why they bought it, and listen to the words: craftsmanship, quality, how long it lasts, attention to detail. They're speaking pure use value. They're telling themselves a use story.

Now watch what they actually bought. They bought what it signals. They bought the exchange face: the social price tag, the message it sends to everyone who sees it. The use story is just the costume the exchange motive wears to feel respectable.

Here's a quick test, and be honest with yourself. Would you still buy it if no one would ever see you own it? If the answer is no, you were never buying the craftsmanship. You were buying the signal.

There's nothing evil about buying signals. We all do it. The cost comes from lying to yourself about which face you paid for. Because then you pay the exchange price, 10 times the use value, and you're genuinely angry that it doesn't make you happy. Of course it doesn't. You bought a signal and expected a coat. Wrong face, wrong expectation, real disappointment.

## Pricing the wrong face

**The third mistake is one businesses make from the other side of the counter**, and it quietly kills them.

Picture a bakery. It costs $3 to bake a beautiful loaf of bread. The owner thinks: my cost is $3, so $5 is a fair price. That's cost-plus pricing. It feels honest. It feels decent.

But the customer doesn't care about your cost. The customer was never shown your cost. The customer cares about the use value to them: a warm loaf on a cold morning, the smell filling the kitchen, breakfast for the family they love, unhurried, together. That experience might be worth $15 to them.

Price on cost and you leave $10 of value on the table with every single loaf. Worse, a competitor who understands use value charges $12, makes the customer happier than you did and still earns several times your margin. You weren't being honest. You were being blind. You priced the wrong face.

The same mistake runs in reverse. A software company builds a tool that saves a business $100,000 a year, and it costs the company almost nothing to serve one more customer. If they price on cost, they charge $20 a month and slowly starve while creating fortunes for other people.

Price on the use value delivered, and they charge $2,000 a month, and the customer thanks them for it. Same product. Different face. A hundred times the price. The cost was never the point. The cost is just what you spent. The value is what they received.

## The coat and the certificate

**The fourth mistake belongs to investors**, and it costs people their retirements.

You fall in love with a company. You love the product and use it every single day. The founder is charismatic, the mission moves you, the commercials make you feel something. So you buy the stock with your heart full.

You just bought a use feeling. But a stock isn't a product. A stock has no use value. You can't eat it, wear it, drive it or live inside it. A stock is pure exchange value: a claim on future cash, nothing more and nothing less.

And loving the product tells you nothing at all about the cash. Some of the most beloved companies in history have been terrible investments, and some of the most boring companies on earth have made their shareholders rich. The product is the coat. The stock is the certificate. Never confuse them.

Keep that sentence somewhere you'll see it. *The product is the coat. The stock is the certificate.* If you invest, tattoo it on the inside of your eyelids.

## Building in the gap

So the faces are different, and mixing them up is expensive. But here's the beautiful part, the part that makes this a chapter about creating value and not just about avoiding mistakes. The gap between the two faces is where every business lives.

> Create use value. Capture exchange value. The gap between the two is where businesses live.

Say it slowly, because it's the engine of this whole book.

Think about a great restaurant. The use value of a perfect evening is enormous: warmth, flavor, laughter with people you love, a memory you'll carry for years. Now ask what it cost the restaurant to create that evening. A fraction of the bill. A small fraction.

The customer gladly pays the exchange price, because the use value dwarfs it. They'd pay it again tomorrow. The restaurant keeps the gap. That gap is profit. That gap is the business. No gap, no business. It really is that simple, and that hard.

That's why the best businesses obsess over use value and price on exchange value. They pour everything into making the thing genuinely useful, genuinely delightful, genuinely worth far more to the customer than the price on the menu. Then they charge what the market will bear, and everyone walks away feeling like they won.

The worst businesses do the exact opposite. They pour everything into the exchange face: the branding, the packaging, the signal, the unboxing experience, the influencer campaign. Meanwhile the use value is hollow. Signals sell, so it works for a while. But hollow use value always gets found out. Always. The reviews come. The refunds come. Then the silence comes.

Pull the threads together. Value lives in people, not in things. It lives at the margin, in this person, in this moment, in this one more unit. And it wears two faces: what it does for you, and what others will trade for it.

Master both faces and the expensive mistakes stop. You stop selling the ring for the price of the metal. You stop pricing your work by what it cost you. You stop buying certificates because you love coats. And you start building in the gap. Create more use value than you capture in exchange value, and customers will beat a path to your door. That isn't a slogan for a poster. It's the arithmetic of every business that has ever lasted more than a season.

Every lasting business you admire lives in that gap: the restaurant, the toolmaker, the teacher, the software company that charges $2,000 and gets thanked for it. They all create use value by the bucket and capture exchange value by the cup, and the difference compounds into something durable.

## The takeaways

1. **Every valuable thing has two faces.** Use value is what it does for you, personal and always marginal. Exchange value is what others will trade for it, steady enough to plan around and easy to mistake for the only value.
2. **Confusing the faces is expensive.** It's how people sell a ring for the price of its metal, pay for a signal and expect a coat, price their work by what it cost them, and buy a stock because they love the product.
3. **Create use value; capture exchange value.** The gap between the two is where every lasting business lives. No gap, no business.

## Try this

Before your next trade, price or purchase, ask one question and answer it honestly: which face am I looking at? Am I valuing what it does, or what it fetches? Get the face right, and the confusion tax disappears.

There's one more layer to peel back. So far we've treated exchange value as the price the market pays, as if the price were the value itself. But what is a price, really? Is it the value, or is it only a rumor about the value?
