# Removing Friction

Back in the 2000s, an online retailer had a mystery on its hands. Shoppers were filling their carts, clicking checkout and vanishing. The prices were fine. The site worked. But somewhere between "buy" and "bought," a river of money was disappearing into thin air.

A researcher named Jared Spool was called in to find out why, and he did what good researchers do: he sat down next to real shoppers and watched. Within minutes, he found the leak. It was a single button on the checkout page, and it said one word: Register. Before you could spend a dollar, the site demanded that you create an account. Username, password, password again. Shoppers froze. Some took it as a commitment they didn't want. Some tried to log in, failed and gave up.

And here's the frightening part. The button wasn't broken. It worked exactly as designed. It asked for one small thing at exactly the wrong moment.

Spool's team changed almost nothing. They replaced the word Register with the word Continue, and they added one line: you don't need to create an account to shop. The account became optional, at the very end. No redesign. No sale. No new features. Purchases jumped 45 percent: $15 million in new sales in the first month, $300 million in the first year. It became known as the $300 million button, and it's one of the most expensive lessons ever learned about a single screen. One demand, at the wrong moment, had been standing between the company and a fortune. The money was always there. The friction was hiding it.

That's the subject of this chapter. Friction: the waits, the clicks, the forms, the fine print, the 17 taps to do the thing you've already decided to do. Every one of them is a small tax, and your customer pays it in the scarcest currency they own, their patience. And here's the promise. Remove the friction and you don't just make people happier. You unlock value that was already sitting there, waiting behind the hassle.

## A thousand tiny toll booths

First, a definition, because friction hides in plain sight. Friction is anything that stands between a person and the thing they want. The extra click. The line at the counter. The form that asks for your life story to deliver a pizza. The password-reset email that never arrives.

None of these feels dramatic. Each one feels small. But small is the whole trick, because friction is never one big wall. It's a thousand tiny toll booths, and every booth takes its cut, in attention, in time, in willpower. A customer who wanted your product at the first booth can be gone by the fifth.

## The value was already there

The best demonstration of this is nearly a hundred years old, and it happened in a grocery store. In 1937, an Oklahoma City grocer named Sylvan Goldman watched his customers shop, and he noticed something. People stopped buying when their arms were full. Not when their wallets were empty. When their arms were full. The basket they carried was the ceiling on the sale.

So Goldman invented the shopping cart: a folding-chair frame with wheels and two wire baskets. And then something strange happened. Nobody used it. Men thought it looked unmanly. Women thought it looked like a baby stroller. The invention sat there, ignored. So Goldman hired models, well-dressed men and women, to push the carts around his store and fill them up. Real shoppers watched the models, got curious and started copying them. Sales soared.

Goldman hadn't changed his products, his prices or his advertising. He had removed one physical limit, the carrying capacity of two arms, and the value that had been trapped behind it poured out.

That's the first law of this chapter, so write it down: the value was already there. Goldman didn't create new groceries. The $300 million button didn't create new shoppers.

> Friction doesn't destroy value; it locks it up.

Every wait, every click, every hassle is a lock on a door, and behind the door sit money, loyalty and goodwill that already belong to you, if you'd just open it. Which means removing friction is the closest thing business has to free money. You aren't building something new. You're collecting what's already yours.

## Friction has a price tag

The second law is that friction has a price tag, and it's bigger than you think. In 2006, an Amazon engineer named Greg Linden described an experiment in which the company added artificial delays to its pages, a tenth of a second at a time, and measured what happened to sales. Every 100 milliseconds of delay cost about 1 percent of sales. One percent, per tenth of a second. Around the same time, Google found that a half-second delay in loading search results caused traffic to drop 20 percent. Not five seconds. Half of one second. Your customers aren't patient people making careful trade-offs. They're busy people, and the moment a page stalls, their attention is already somewhere else.

Jeff Bezos understood this at the level of instinct. In 1999, Amazon patented one-click ordering: buy the thing with a single click, no cart, no checkout, no forms. It was so valuable that Amazon sued a competitor over it, and Apple later licensed it for its own store. Think about what that patent really is. It isn't a technology. It's the legal ownership of a removed hassle: one click instead of six.

Apple built an entire music empire on the same principle. Buying a song on iTunes took seconds, while the alternative was driving to a store or wrestling with piracy. The friction of the old way was so high that removing it created an industry.

So the physics are settled. Friction is a tax, the tax is heavy, and it never appears on any spreadsheet in the building. The company only sees the customers who made it through checkout. It never meets the ones who gave up at the password field. So it congratulates itself on a fine process, while a fortune quietly walks out the back door.

## Three rules for killing it

Here's how to find friction and get rid of it.

**Rule one: watch where people stall.** Don't ask them. Watch them. Customers will tell you on a survey that everything is fine, and then you'll watch a recording of their session and see them click the same button four times, sigh and leave. Spool found the $300 million button by sitting next to shoppers, not by polling them. Goldman found the carrying limit by watching arms, not by reading comment cards. Friction lives in behavior, not in opinions. Once a month, sit next to a real customer and watch them use what you built. You'll find a toll booth within the hour. Guaranteed.

**Rule two: delete a step before you add a feature.** Every business loves to add. Add a field to the form, add a screen to the flow, add a verification for safety. Almost nobody subtracts. But subtraction is where the money is. There's a famous story about a travel site with one optional field on its booking form, labeled Company, sitting right under the address line. Customers kept typing their company name into the address box by mistake, the credit card check failed, and the booking died. One confusing field cost $12 million a year. The fix wasn't a better field. It was no field. Every step you delete is a toll booth demolished. Before your next launch, don't ask what to add. Ask what to remove.

**Rule three: measure the wait in seconds, and make somebody own it.** What gets measured gets managed, and in most companies nobody owns the waiting. The page load time belongs to engineering, the hold music belongs to support, and the line at the counter belongs to nobody. Pick the three waits your customer feels most, put a number on each, and put one name next to each number. Take a finding like Amazon's 100 milliseconds and treat it the way it deserves: not as a slogan, but as a budget, enforced like money. Because waiting is money. Every second of hold music is a second of your customer's life you're spending without permission. Treat their time like your money, and they'll treat your product like it matters.

## Invisible to its builders

Here's the strange part. Friction is invisible to the people who built it. You know your own checkout flow because you designed it, so it feels obvious to you. It isn't obvious to a tired parent on a phone at midnight, one eye on a crying baby. The $300 million button looked perfectly reasonable to everyone inside that company, right up until someone watched a stranger try to use it.

Your business is full of toll booths you can't see, because you built them and then stopped looking. The value on the other side is already yours. Go collect it.

## The takeaways

1. **Friction doesn't destroy value; it locks it up.** The groceries, the shoppers and the money were already there. Removing a hassle is the closest thing business has to free money: you're collecting what's already yours.
2. **Friction has a price tag, and it's bigger than you think.** A tenth of a second can cost a slice of sales. Because the company never meets the customers who gave up, the tax never shows up on a spreadsheet.
3. **Friction is invisible to the people who built it.** Watch where people stall, delete a step before you add a feature and give every wait a number and an owner.

## Try this

This month, sit next to one real customer and watch them use what you built, without helping. Count every pause, every repeated click, every sigh. Then pick one of those toll booths and remove it, instead of adding anything new.

Look closely at Goldman's cart and you'll notice something else about it. He didn't conjure it out of nothing. It was a folding chair plus a basket. One-click ordering was a stored credit card plus a button. Nothing new under the sun, and everything new in the combination. That turns out to be how most innovation works, and it's a skill anyone can learn. Cut the friction, collect the value and then start combining.
