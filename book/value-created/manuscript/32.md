# Teach It

There's a story told about a carpenter in Kyoto. He takes on one apprentice every few years, and people say that when he dies, his hands will die with him.

But they're wrong. His hands live on in every apprentice, and in every apprentice of every apprentice. He has built furniture for 40 years, and that furniture will one day crumble. The hands he trained will build for a century.

That's what this last chapter is about. Not a new multiplier. The multiplier that multiplies the multipliers.

## The asset that duplicates

Leverage, networks, compounding, reputation, giving first: everything in this book multiplies value. But teaching is the final leverage, because it's the one multiplier that works on all the others at once.

When you teach someone, you don't lose what you know. Knowledge is the rare asset you can give away and keep.

That breaks the usual rules. In every other chapter, giving had a cost. Distribution costs effort. Giving first costs time. Teaching has a strange property the others don't: the asset duplicates. You teach a friend to negotiate, and you still know how to negotiate.

In fact, and this is the quiet miracle, you know it better than before. Teaching forces you to organize what you only half understood. Both sides compound.

> The student gets the knowledge. You get the mastery.

## Two bakeries instead of one

Here's what it looks like in real life. Think of a neighborhood bakery. The owner, Maria, has spent 10 years learning her craft: the slow dough, the wood-fired oven, everything.

One day a teenager from down the street asks to learn. Most people would guard it. Trade secrets, right? She teaches him everything.

Two years later, he opens a stall across town. You'd call that competition. But look at what actually happens. The neighborhood now has two beloved bakeries. Her original shop becomes the destination people drive to. And the kid puts "Trained at Maria's" on his sign, which sends customers her way.

She didn't divide her reputation. She multiplied it.

## The guru trap

There's a dark mirror here too, and it's worth naming, because this idea is easy to weaponize. There's a wrong kind of teaching: teaching to be admired instead of to multiply.

You've seen it. The endless webinar that never quite gives the answer. The coach whose students stay dependent instead of becoming independent. If your teaching keeps the student coming back forever, you built a funnel, not a student.

Real teaching has one test: can the student now teach someone else? If yes, you multiplied. If not, you performed.

## Three rules for teaching

So say you're convinced, and you want to teach something this week. What do you actually do? Three rules.

**First, teach the why before the how.** A student who understands why the dough needs 12 hours can figure out the oven temperature on their own. A student who only knows the temperature is lost the moment the oven changes. Why makes knowledge portable. How alone makes it brittle.

**Second, make them do it while you watch.** Knowledge that stays in the ears evaporates. The carpenter in Kyoto doesn't lecture about chisels; he hands the apprentice the chisel. Nobody learns to swim from a whiteboard. Teaching isn't telling. It's guided doing.

**Third, give away the whole thing.** No cliffhangers, no secrets held back for the advanced course. The whole idea, freely. That was true of giving value first, and it's even more true here. A teacher who holds back is protecting a scarcity that doesn't exist. The knowledge doesn't shrink when it's shared. And the student who gets everything remembers who gave it.
