# Distribution

In the 1970s, there was a machine that could record television beautifully. By many accounts it had the crisper picture, the better sound and the better engineering. It was called Betamax.

You probably never owned one. Not because it was worse. It wasn't. It was because the company that made Betamax built the better machine and then lost the more important race: getting it into people's living rooms.

The competitor, VHS, didn't win on quality. It won on distribution. Longer tapes, more rental stores, more machines on more shelves. Over the years that followed, the better machine became a footnote, and the better-distributed machine became the standard.

Nobody buys what they never meet. The value was real. It just never arrived. This chapter is about the arrival.

In the last chapter we picked up the longest lever in the book: leverage, the art of doing more with less. But a lever needs one more thing to matter. It needs an audience. The finest product, the smartest code, the strongest team can all sit in the dark, multiplying nothing, because nobody knows they exist. Creation is only half the job. The other half is getting what you made into the hands of the people it was made for. That half has a name: distribution.

## Value happens in the exchange

Here's the line to remember.

> Value nobody sees might as well not exist.

Think of it as physics. A tree falls in an empty forest and makes no sound for anyone. A meal cooked for an empty table feeds no one. Creation happens in the workshop. Value happens in the exchange. Distribution is the bridge between the two.

It isn't marketing gloss, and it isn't a trick to make bad things look good. It's the honest, unglamorous work of carrying created value to the people who would pay for it, use it or be changed by it.

Notice what this means. Two people can create exactly the same value, and the one with better distribution wins. Not because she's louder. Because she's reachable. Her customers don't have to climb a mountain to find her. The value comes to them.

This is why the creator who spends a year perfecting a product and a week telling people about it almost always loses to the one who spends half the time making and half the time delivering. The market can't choose what it can't see.

## A road for good books

Watch what happens when distribution moves first. In 1935, a British publisher named Allen Lane was coming home from a weekend in the country and found himself on a train platform with nothing to read.

Not because there were no good books. There were plenty, stacked in bookshops, locked in hardcovers that cost a fortune. The value existed. It just wasn't where the readers were.

Lane had an idea that sounds obvious now and was radical then. Make good books cheap and small enough to fit in a pocket, and sell them where people actually are: train stations, newsstands, chain stores. He priced the paperbacks at sixpence, about the price of a pack of cigarettes, and he called the line Penguin Books. It was an almost immediate hit.

Lane didn't write a single new book. He didn't improve the prose of a single author. He took value that already existed and built it a road. Within a few years, the paperback had done for reading what the railway did for travel: it made something precious available to everyone, simply by showing up where people already were.

That's distribution. Not better value. Better arrival.

## Build your road

So how do you build your own? Start by killing the field-of-dreams fallacy. A famous movie line is usually remembered as "If you build it, they will come." They won't. They're busy. They have 10,000 other offers competing for their attention. Building is step one. Step two is the part most creators skip, so here's a simple discipline.

**First, find where your people already gather.** Not where you wish they were. Where they actually are. The train platform, not the bookshop. The comment section, the community group, the inbox, the shelf they already browse.

**Second, make yourself easy to find there.** One reliable channel you own, like an email list or a storefront, matters more than ten scattered attempts.

**Third, spend as much time on delivery as on creation.** If you spent a month making something, spend a month getting it seen. That ratio shocks most people. It shouldn't. Half the value of your work lives in the workshop. The other half lives on the road.

And one warning. Distribution multiplies, just like leverage. Push something mediocre through a great channel and the world will discover it's mediocre faster. Make the value real first. Then build the road.

## Is it arriving?

Creation asks: is this good? Distribution asks: is this seen? You need both answers to be yes.

The value you create is a promise. Distribution is keeping the promise: showing up where the readers are, like Allen Lane on that train platform, and putting the thing within reach.

So from here on, when you finish making something, ask yourself one question. Not "Is it done?" but "Is it arriving?"

## The takeaways

1. **Value nobody sees might as well not exist.** Creation happens in the workshop, value happens in the exchange, and distribution is the bridge between them.
2. **Better arrival beats better value.** Betamax had the better machine and VHS had the better reach. Allen Lane didn't write a new book; he built a road to readers. The reachable one wins.
3. **Build the road on purpose.** Go where your people already gather, own one reliable channel, spend as much time delivering as creating, and make the value real before you multiply it.

## Try this

Take the last thing you made. Write down where the people it's for actually spend their time, then compare that with where you've put it. If the two lists don't overlap, you've found your next month of work.

And arriving unlocks something stranger. When value reaches people, sometimes those people start adding to it. Each new person who shows up makes the thing better for everyone who was already there. That's a different kind of product altogether, one that gets more valuable the more people use it.
