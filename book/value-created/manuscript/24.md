# Leverage

There's a farmer, a boulder in his field, and a morning to lose. He bends down, wraps his arms around the stone and lifts. Nothing moves. The boulder weighs 400 pounds, and he doesn't have 400 pounds in him.

So he walks to the tree line and drags back a fallen branch. He slides one end under the boulder and wedges a smaller rock under the branch, close to the boulder. Then he stands on the long end of the branch and pushes down with his own weight.

The boulder rises.

He didn't get stronger between the first attempt and the second. His muscles are exactly the same. What changed was the arrangement.

The Greek mathematician Archimedes looked at a trick like this and said: give me a place to stand, and I will move the Earth. He was describing leverage. This chapter is about the levers that let ordinary people move unreasonably large things.

In the last chapter, we watched buyers and sellers discover prices together in the market. Now we pick up the longest lever in the book. Every hour you spend creating value is precious, and leverage is how one hour does the work of ten, or a hundred, or 10,000. There are four levers worth knowing: capital, code, people and media. By the end of this chapter, you'll know which one to reach for first, and which mistake to avoid with all four.

## Multiplied, not spent

Here's the definition to hold on to. Leverage is doing more with less: arranging things so that your effort, your money or your time gets multiplied instead of spent.

Notice the word *multiplied*. The farmer with the branch multiplies his strength. The four levers do the same for everything else you have.

- **Capital.** An investor multiplies her money. The money works while she sleeps, and every dollar goes out and finds more dollars.
- **Code.** A programmer multiplies her time. She writes it once, and it runs a million times while she's nowhere near it.
- **People.** A founder multiplies her reach. A team of ten does in a week what one person could never do in a year.
- **Media.** A creator multiplies her voice. One recording is heard by 100,000 strangers, none of whom she had to visit in person.

Four levers. Same trick. The branch under the boulder, just longer.

This is why the wealthiest people you know rarely look busy.

> They aren't working harder than you. They're standing on longer branches.

## One steel box

Watch leverage work at the largest scale. In 1956, a trucking company owner named Malcolm McLean had a problem. Loading a cargo ship by hand took days. It cost almost six dollars for every ton. And along the way, a shocking share of the cargo got damaged or stolen.

He didn't hire stronger dockworkers. He didn't shout louder. He turned to a simple steel box: the shipping container. Cranes lift the box off the truck and set it on the ship, and the ship sails. Nobody touches the goods inside.

Loading costs fell from nearly six dollars a ton to 16 cents. Not 10 percent cheaper. About 36 times cheaper.

The lever wasn't bigger muscles. It was an arrangement. Make every box the same size, mechanize the lift, and suddenly every pair of hands on the dock moves vastly more cargo than before. Within 20 years, that steel box had quietly rearranged the economy of the entire planet.

That's what leverage does when it's real. It doesn't improve the work. It changes the geometry of the work.

## Finding your lever

So how do you find yours?

**Start with an audit.** Write down everything you did last week and circle the things that repeat. Anything that repeats is a lever waiting to happen. A repeated task can be automated with code, handed to people who do it better, or funded once with capital so it pays you back forever.

**Reach for the cheapest lever first.** Media costs almost nothing now. One careful essay, one honest recording, one genuinely useful guide can reach more people than a year of handshakes.

Take a concrete example. Suppose you answer the same question from customers every single week. Write the answer once, carefully, and put it where every future customer can find it. You just turned an hour of repeating yourself into an asset that answers the question forever. That's media leverage, and it cost you one afternoon.

## Respect the lever

Now the warning. Never put a lever under something broken.

Leverage multiplies whatever it touches. Multiply a good product and you get a great business. Multiply a bad product and you get a spectacular failure, faster.

And a special note on debt, the oldest lever of all. Borrowed money multiplies your gains, but it also multiplies your losses, and the bill comes due whether the bet worked or not. Respect the lever. It doesn't care which direction you point it.

Step back and every hour gives you two choices. You can spend it the way the farmer spent his first lift: muscle against stone, effort traded one for one until you're tired. Or you can arrange it the way he arranged the branch, so the same hour moves something far heavier than you could ever lift alone. Capital, code, people, media. Four branches are lying on the ground of your life right now, waiting to be picked up.

## The takeaways

1. **Leverage multiplies instead of spends.** The farmer didn't get stronger; he changed the arrangement. Capital, code, people and media are the same trick with a longer branch.
2. **Look for what repeats, and reach for the cheapest lever first.** Anything you do over and over is a lever waiting to happen, and media (writing the answer once) costs almost nothing.
3. **Leverage multiplies whatever it touches.** Never put it under something broken, and treat debt with respect: it multiplies losses as faithfully as gains.

## Try this

Find the question you answer most often, from customers, colleagues or friends. This week, write the answer once, carefully, and put it where the next person who asks can find it without you.

The question this chapter leaves you with is the one Archimedes would ask: where is your place to stand? One caution before you go looking. A lever multiplies your value, but only if someone sees it. Brilliant work can sit in the dark, multiplying nothing, simply because nobody knows it's there. Unseen value might as well not exist, and getting it seen is a craft all its own.
