# Creating Isn't Enough

Charles Goodyear had an obsession. Rubber, the tree sap the world was so excited about, melted in summer and froze in winter, and Goodyear, a hardware merchant from Connecticut, believed he could fix it. For five years he experimented: in a borrowed laboratory, then in his kitchen, then in debtors' prison, more than once. He sold his children's schoolbooks to buy chemicals. Creditors seized his furniture while his family went hungry.

Then, in 1839, came the legendary accident. He dropped a mixture of rubber and sulfur onto a hot stove, and it didn't melt. It vulcanized. Goodyear had found the process that made the modern rubber industry possible, the process behind every tire, every seal, every rubber sole on earth.

And Charles Goodyear died penniless. He left debts of $200,000, a fortune in his day, and he never saw a cent of the rubber billions. Nearly 40 years later, a tire company took his name and stamped it on factories around the world. The most famous name in rubber belonged to a man the rubber industry never paid.

So far this book has been about creating value: seeing it, finding the problems behind it, building the things that solve them. This part of the book is about the half that Goodyear missed.

## Two games, two rulebooks

Here's the sentence this chapter hangs on. Creating value and capturing value are two different games, with two different rulebooks.

Creating is making something people want: the invention, the recipe, the song, the software. Capturing is arranging the world so the money flows back to you: the contract, the brand, the distribution, the price. Goodyear won the first game completely and lost the second game completely.

And he isn't the exception. He's the pattern. The people who make things are very often not the people who get rich from them, and the reason is simple.

> The market doesn't pay you for creating value. It pays you for capturing it.

## The better product, the better deal

Nothing shows the two games more clearly than the summer of 1980. IBM was building its first personal computer, and it needed an operating system. The obvious choice was CP/M, made by a company called Digital Research and run by a brilliant programmer named Gary Kildall. CP/M ran on nearly every small computer in America.

IBM's executives flew out to meet him. Legend says Kildall went flying that day instead, his lawyer argued with IBM over a nondisclosure agreement, and IBM walked away. It took its business to a tiny software shop called Microsoft, run by a 24-year-old named Bill Gates.

Microsoft had no operating system. So Gates bought one, a rough program called QDOS, from a small Seattle company, for $50,000. Then he licensed it to IBM, with one masterstroke buried in the contract: Microsoft kept the right to sell it to anyone else. When the clone makers arrived, every machine ran Microsoft's system.

Kildall made the better product. Gates made the better deal. The best product didn't win. The best position won.

## Follow the bottleneck

Once you see the pattern, it's everywhere. Xerox's research lab pioneered the graphical interface and the mouse-driven desktop, the building blocks of the modern computer, and Xerox captured almost none of that, while Apple and Microsoft turned those ideas into fortunes. The farmer who grows your coffee captures pennies per cup. The café on the corner captures $5, for the cup, the chair and the logo. The app developer does the creating, and the app store takes a cut, often 30 percent, for owning the shelf.

None of this is a conspiracy. It's physics. Value flows to the scarcest piece of the chain. If 10,000 people can make it but only one can sell it, the seller eats. Creation is abundant. Capture is scarce. And the scarce piece sets the price.

That's the key move in this chapter: follow the bottleneck. In every chain, from raw material to customer, there's one step that's hard to replace. The patent, the brand, the distribution network, the relationship with the buyer, the one machine nobody else owns. Whoever controls the bottleneck captures the margin, and everyone else splits the scraps.

Goodyear had no bottleneck. His process could be copied, his patents were tied up in court, and he had no factory, no brand and no customers of his own. He created enormous value and captured none of it, because there was nothing about his position that the world had to pay for.

So here's the question to ask about everything you make: who captures this, and is it me? If the honest answer is someone else, you don't have a business yet. You have a donation.

## Three rules for capturing what you create

**Rule one: own the scarce piece.** Before you build anything, look at the whole chain and ask what will be hard to replace. If the answer is nothing, stop and change the plan. The best time to build a moat is before you need one: a patent filed early, a brand people remember, a customer list that's yours, a distribution channel you control.

Tesla understood this. The batteries weren't the scarce piece. The charging network was, so Tesla built its own: the Supercharger stations nobody else had. Apple understood it too. The phone wasn't the scarce piece. The app store was. Don't ask how good your product is. Ask what part of your chain the world can't route around. Then own that part.

**Rule two: get it in writing, before it works.** Goodyear spent his last years in court, fighting over patents while his creditors circled. Kildall's deal with IBM died over a nondisclosure agreement nobody would sign. Robert Kearns invented the intermittent windshield wiper, watched the car companies take it and spent decades suing them to get paid.

The pattern is always the same. The deal gets made when the value is small and everyone is friendly, or it gets fought over when the value is huge and nobody is. Amateurs negotiate after the value exists. Professionals negotiate before. Who owns what, who gets paid how much, what happens if it works: decide it in writing while it's still cheap to decide. The handshake isn't the deal. The contract is the product.

**Rule three: build the business, not just the product.** Most creators spend 90 percent of their energy on the thing and 10 percent on everything that captures its value: the pricing, the selling, the distribution, the brand. Flip the ratio. A mediocre product with great distribution beats a great product with no distribution every single time, because customers can't buy what they never see. The iPod wasn't the first MP3 player. What set it apart was everything around it: a store, a brand and an ad campaign.

So when you finish making something, your work is half done, at best. The second half is pricing it, placing it and telling the world it exists.

That's the deeper point. Creating is only half the battle. The value you create is a seed, capture is the harvest, and a seed nobody harvests feeds someone else. Goodyear vulcanized rubber and died in debt. Kildall made the better operating system and watched its era from the sidelines. The difference between them and the people who got rich was never talent, and it was never the quality of the work. It was position, paperwork and the scarce piece. So honor your creations enough to capture them.

## The takeaways

1. **Creating and capturing are two different games.** Creating is making something people want; capturing is arranging the world so the money flows back to you. Winning the first guarantees nothing in the second.
2. **Value flows to the bottleneck.** Creation is abundant and capture is scarce, so whoever owns the step that's hard to replace takes the margin. Talent and quality didn't separate Goodyear and Kildall from the winners; position, paperwork and the scarce piece did.
3. **Capture is built on purpose.** Own the scarce piece, get it in writing before it works and build the business, not just the product.

## Try this

Pick one thing you make, or want to make. Sketch its chain from raw material to customer, then circle the step that would be hardest for anyone to replace. Ask who owns that step today. If it isn't you, write down one move that would change that, and ask the chapter's question out loud: who captures this, and is it me?

Make sure the answer is you. And the first capture skill is one you'll use every single time you sell anything: the price. Most people get it exactly backwards. They charge for their effort, when the customer is buying the outcome. One way keeps you broke and the other sets you free, and the difference starts with realizing the price was never about you.
