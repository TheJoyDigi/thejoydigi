# Choosing What to Build

Consider two builders. Both were brilliant. Both worked 16-hour days for years.

One built the best fax machine accessory money could buy, in 2006, just as fax machines were on their way out. The other built a small tool that helped people send money to family across borders, slow and steady, for a decade.

Same effort. One ended up with a beautiful product nobody wanted. The other ended up wealthy. The difference was never effort. It was the problem each one chose.

## The one choice you get

That's the whole chapter in two sentences. You don't get to choose your talent, not really, and you barely get to choose your luck. But you do get to choose what you build. And that choice, made once, echoes through everything else.

Pick the right problem, and even mediocre execution carries you. Pick the wrong one, and perfect execution just gets you to the wrong destination faster.

## Taste is trained judgment

So is this about taste? That word makes a lot of people nervous. Taste sounds like something you're born with, like perfect pitch. Either you have it or you don't.

But taste isn't magic. It's judgment, trained. A chef's palate isn't a gift from the gods; it's 10,000 meals remembered. Judgment works the same way. Every problem you study, every product you take apart, every customer you watch goes into the file. Over time the file gets thick enough that your gut starts whispering before your head catches up. People call that taste. It's really just evidence, digested.

How do you train it on purpose, though? Most people don't have 10,000 meals. They have a job, a commute and an idea they're scared to say out loud.

You study problems the way a jeweler studies stones. Most people fall in love with solutions. They have a clever app idea and go looking for someone who needs it. Flip that. Go live inside a problem first. Find the people who wake up thinking about it, who would pay real money to make it disappear.

If the problem is big enough and the pain is sharp enough, the solution can be clumsy and it still works. If the pain is fake, no amount of polish saves you.

## Good problems look boring

If that reminds you of the chapter on distribution, it should. Value nobody sees might as well not exist. This is its cousin: value nobody needs. Distribution is how value gets seen. Choosing is how value gets chosen.

And here's the trap. The problems that look exciting are usually the crowded ones. Everyone wants to build the next big social app. The good problems look boring from the outside. Freight invoices. Dental scheduling. The plumbing supply chain. The best problems are the ones where the incumbents are asleep and the customers are quietly furious.

## Big, real and wrong for you

There's a darker version of this too. Plenty of people pick huge, painful problems and still flame out. They choose a real monster, and it eats them.

That's the part nobody warns you about. A problem can be big and real and still wrong for you. Some problems need capital you don't have. Some need credentials the gatekeepers will never give you. Some take 20 years, and you needed it to work in two. Choosing what to build means choosing something you can actually finish. Judgment isn't just seeing the mountain. It's knowing whether you brought the right boots.

So how do you check yourself? How do you know you've picked a problem worth your life before you spend five years on it? Ask three questions.

1. **Do people already spend money trying to solve this badly?** If they pay for a clumsy workaround, they'll pay for a good solution.
2. **Does the problem grow if you ignore it?** The best problems are rivers, not puddles. They keep flowing whether you build or not.
3. **Can you start small and still be useful?** If the only version that works costs $10 million, it isn't your problem. If a scrappy version helps 10 people tomorrow, it might be.

## A problem worth your life

"Picking problems worth your life" is a heavy phrase. It's heavy because it's true.

You get a finite number of deep efforts. Maybe five or six real, all-in bets in a working life, and each one costs you years you never get back. So the question isn't just "Is this a good business?" The question is "Is this worth three years of mornings?" If the answer is no, put it down, no matter how clever it is.

The cleverest trap in business is a brilliant idea for a problem you don't actually care about, because you'll quit at the first hard winter.

Now step back and look at the whole book. Almost everything in it has been a multiplier: ownership, trade, money, markets, leverage, networks, compounding. But there's a catch.

> A multiplier times zero is still zero.

Choosing what to build is the number you multiply everything by. Pick well, and every tool in this book works for you. Pick badly, and you just get faster at going nowhere.

## The takeaways

1. **Taste is trained judgment.** It isn't a gift you're born with. It's evidence, digested: every problem you study and every customer you watch, filed away until your gut speaks first.
2. **Judgment picks problems.** Look for real, growing pain that people already pay to patch badly, in a size you can actually finish, and that you care about enough to survive the first hard winter.
3. **Choosing is the one thing you can't hand off.** You can't outsource it, automate it or delegate it. The machines will do the building. Only you can choose what's worth building, so choose like your life depends on it. It does.

## Try this

Take the idea you're most excited about right now and run it through the three questions. Do people already pay for a clumsy workaround? Does the problem grow if nobody fixes it? Could a scrappy version help 10 people next week? Then ask the last one honestly: is it worth three years of your mornings?

That's the end of the theory. Every piece is on the table now: the multipliers, the levers, the lessons and the judgment to aim them. Pieces on a table don't do anything by themselves, though. The next step is to stop adding parts and start assembling them into one personal machine, tuned for you.
