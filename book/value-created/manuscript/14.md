# Recombination

Around 1450, in the German city of Mainz, a goldsmith named Johannes Gutenberg had an idea that changed the world. It's remembered as one of the great inventions of history. It wasn't an invention. It was a combination.

Movable type already existed; printers in Korea were casting metal type before Gutenberg was born. The screw press already existed. You could find one in the wine cellars along the Rhine, squeezing grapes into wine. Paper already existed. Ink already existed. And the goldsmith's molds for casting small metal parts? Gutenberg knew those by hand, because he was one.

So he didn't invent the printing press the way we imagine inventors inventing things, out of nothing, in a flash of genius. He rearranged. He took the winemaker's press, the idea of movable type and his own goldsmith's casting, and he put them together into a machine the world had never seen: a press that could print a thousand pages in a day. Within 50 years, more books had been printed in Europe than had been copied by hand in the previous thousand.

The machine that started the modern world wasn't invented. It was recombined. And that's the secret this chapter is built on: almost everything that matters was.

## Innovation is rearrangement

Start with what innovation really is, because it rewires how you think about new ideas. Innovation is mostly rearrangement. Researchers who study patents have found that most of them aren't new things at all. They're new combinations of old things. A new drug can be two known molecules, joined. A new dish can be two cuisines, married. A new business is usually somebody else's business model, applied to somebody else's industry. The genuinely new part is almost always the arrangement.

Once you see this, you stop staring at a blank page waiting for a bolt from the blue. You start looking at the pieces on the table and asking how they might fit together differently.

Nobody demonstrated it better than Steve Jobs with the iPhone. In January 2007, on a stage in San Francisco, Jobs announced three products. An iPod, he said. A phone. And an internet communicator. Then came the famous line: these weren't three separate devices. This was one device, and they were calling it iPhone.

Look at what was actually inside that announcement. The touchscreen existed. The cell phone existed. The web browser existed. The music player existed. Every ingredient of the iPhone was already sitting on somebody's shelf, and Apple invented almost none of it. What Apple did was combine it, into one slab of glass, with one interface, that a child could use. And the combination was worth vastly more than the sum of its parts. That's what recombination does. The parts are ordinary. The arrangement is the fortune.

## The pattern is everywhere

Once you see the pattern, you find it everywhere. A scientist at 3M named Spencer Silver invented a glue that didn't stick very well. It was a failure, and it sat on the shelf for years. Then another 3M employee, Art Fry, was singing in his church choir, and his paper bookmarks kept falling out of his hymnal. He remembered Silver's failed glue. A weak adhesive, plus a paper bookmark, plus choir practice, equals the Post-it note. Nobody invented the Post-it. They recombined it.

In 2013, a New York pastry chef named Dominique Ansel spent two months fusing a croissant with a doughnut. The result was the Cronut, and people lined up around the block at five in the morning to get one. Croissants were old. Doughnuts were old. The combination had a line around the block.

In 1971, in Kobe, Japan, a drummer named Daisuke Inoue built a box that played backing tracks so bar customers could sing along into a microphone. He never patented it. He leased a few machines to local bars. That box was karaoke, now a global industry worth billions. A microphone, a recording and a bar: three old things, one new arrangement.

## The adjacent possible

This is the best news in the whole book, and it's the heart of this chapter: you don't need to invent anything.

The scientist Stuart Kauffman gave this idea a name: the adjacent possible. At any moment, there's a set of new combinations the world is ready for, sitting right next to everything that already exists. Every time somebody makes a combination, the adjacent possible expands, and new combinations come within reach. Gutenberg's world made the printing press possible because the press, the type and the ink were all adjacent. Your world is the same. Every skill you have, every tool you've touched, every industry you've worked in is an ingredient.

And the math is on your side, because combinations grow faster than ingredients. Ten ingredients make 45 pairs. Twenty ingredients make 190. You don't need a genius idea. You need a shelf full of parts, and the habit of mixing them.

## How to recombine on purpose

How do you become a recombiner? Three rules.

**Rule one: stockpile ingredients.** Combinations can only come from parts you actually have, so collect parts on purpose. Read outside your field. Learn a skill nobody in your industry has. Talk to people who do things you don't understand. Every experience is inventory. Gutenberg could combine the press and the type because he was a goldsmith who happened to live in wine country. The parts were in his head before they were on his bench. The person with the widest shelf wins, not the person with the highest IQ. Steve Jobs said it plainly: creativity is just connecting things. But you can only connect what you've collected.

**Rule two: cross the boundaries.** The best combinations almost never come from inside one field. They come from the collision of two worlds that rarely meet. The printing press came from winemaking meeting goldsmithing meeting publishing. The iPhone came from a computer company invading the phone business, not from a phone company building a better phone. Karaoke came from a drummer hanging around bars. So if you want new combinations, skip the conference for your own industry and go to somebody else's. Read the magazine for a trade you'll never join. Have lunch with someone whose job you can't explain. Recombination happens at the borders. Tourists don't recombine; explorers do.

**Rule three: test the combination cheaply.** Most combinations fail, and that's fine, because testing a combination is cheap and inventing from scratch is expensive. Ansel tested the Cronut for two months before it ever met a customer. Gutenberg spent years tinkering in secret before the first Bible rolled off his press. The recombination mindset says: build the smallest version of the combination, put it in front of real people, and watch. If the Cronut flops, you've lost two months of pastry experiments, not two years of building a bakery. Small bets, fast feedback, keep the winners. The world rewards the person who tries 20 combinations, not the person who polishes one perfect invention in private for a decade.

## Stop inventing, start combining

Stop asking what you can invent. Start asking what you can combine. The shopping cart from the last chapter was a folding chair plus a basket. One-click ordering was a stored credit card plus a button. The printing press was a wine press plus type. The iPhone was three old gadgets in one new arrangement. The parts already exist. They're sitting on shelves, in other industries, in other centuries, waiting for somebody to put them together.

> Value isn't hiding in the undiscovered. It's hiding in the uncombined.

Go combine.

## The takeaways

1. **Innovation is mostly rearrangement.** The printing press, the iPhone, the Post-it, the Cronut and karaoke were all new arrangements of old parts. The parts are ordinary; the arrangement is the fortune.
2. **You don't need to invent anything.** The adjacent possible sits right next to everything that already exists, and combinations grow faster than ingredients: 10 make 45 pairs, 20 make 190.
3. **Become a recombiner on purpose.** Stockpile ingredients, cross the boundaries between fields and test every combination cheaply before you bet big.

## Try this

Write down 10 ingredients you already own: skills, tools, industries you've worked in, things you know from your hobbies. Pair them up and circle the two that almost never meet. Then sketch the smallest version of that combination you could put in front of a real person this month.

That's a hopeful place to close this part of the book. Creating value turns out to be a learnable craft: solve a real problem, earn attention, keep trust, remove friction, combine what already exists. But there's a hard truth these chapters haven't told you yet. Creating value and keeping it are two different games. The people who make things aren't always the people who get rich from them, and sometimes the inventor goes broke while the copier collects the fortune. Creating is only half the battle. Combine the old, and then learn to capture the new.
