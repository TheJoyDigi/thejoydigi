# Compounding

The story starts on a chessboard. A king in ancient India, delighted by the new game of chess, told its inventor to name his reward. The inventor asked for something modest: a single grain of rice on the first square, then double it on every square after, all the way to the 64th.

The king laughed. Rice was cheap.

Then the royal accountants started counting. The early squares were nothing. A few handfuls. Even the whole first half of the board was a rounding error next to what came after. But the second half emptied the granaries, then the treasury, then the kingdom. The king owed the inventor more rice than the country could ever grow.

He'd been tricked, not by the inventor but by the flat beginning of an exponential curve. The first 32 squares asked for almost nothing. The last three squares asked for almost everything.

That's this chapter in a single image. Value, doubling quietly, until it does something nobody believed was possible.

## Time, turned into a multiplier

Here's the heart of it. Compounding is time, turned into a multiplier.

Everything in this book so far has been about creating value once. Compounding is what happens when value starts creating value. Interest earns interest. A skill attracts opportunities, and every opportunity sharpens the skill. A reputation opens doors, and every open door adds to the reputation.

The growth rate matters. But the time horizon is the whole game. A good rate over a long time beats a great rate over a short one. Every time.

## The snowball

The most famous compounding story of all is Warren Buffett's, and he tells it on himself. He bought his first stock at 11 years old, and he likes to describe his life as a snowball, built up by compounding.

But here's the part people always skip. The earliest years mattered most. The snowball had decades of hill ahead of it. A dollar put to work early wasn't one dollar. It was every doubling that came after. Time wasn't a background detail of the story. Time was the story. By a widely cited estimate, about 99 percent of his fortune arrived after his 50th birthday. The decades did the heavy lifting.

Money is only the most famous snowball, though. Here's the one most people live inside without naming it.

Picture a student who reads for one hour a day, every day. At first, nothing seems to change. The hours feel invisible. Then the vocabulary compounds. Then the reading gets faster, which means more reading per hour, which compounds again. Three years later, she isn't a little sharper. She's unrecognizably sharper, because knowledge compounds exactly like money. Each new idea earns interest on every idea it connects to. The first hundred hours are the boring part. Everything after is the magic part.

## Why flat looks like nothing

So why does almost nobody live like this?

Because the early curve of compounding is flat, and flat looks like nothing. The first half of the chessboard is boring. Sixteen grains here, 32 there. The human brain sees the flat part and concludes the whole thing is small. Researchers call this exponential growth bias. We're wired to draw straight lines into the future, so a curve that stays flat and then explodes feels like a trick.

But there's a deeper reason, the one that belongs in this book. Compounding requires you to leave your winnings on the table. You have to not cash out. Every withdrawal murders a little of the future. And humans are spectacularly bad at not cashing out.

Here's the math the brain refuses to feel. Something growing at 10 percent a year doubles roughly every seven years. Seven years feels like forever, so nobody starts. But four doublings of seven years each is a sixteenfold return in 28 years.

> The rate was never the impressive part. The patience was.

## Protect the time

That's why the practical part of this chapter isn't about finding a higher rate. It's about protecting the time. Three rules.

**Rule one: start early, and if you can't start early, start now.** The first year of a snowball is worth more than the tenth, because it gets doubled the most times.

**Rule two: never interrupt it.** Every interruption resets the most powerful part of the curve. Patience isn't a virtue here. It's the strategy.

**Rule three: pick snowballs that don't melt.** Your skills compound. Your reputation compounds. Your relationships compound. Trust compounds as well. Debt compounds too, only in the wrong direction. The question isn't how hard you push. The question is what you let time multiply.

Charlie Munger, Buffett's longtime partner, put the whole thing in one sentence: the first rule of compounding is to never interrupt it unnecessarily. Notice he didn't say find the highest return. He said protect the streak. A 20 percent return that survives beats a 30 percent return that gets cashed out, spent and restarted. The interruption is always more expensive than it looks, because you don't just lose the money. You lose the doublings the money would have bought.

## What keeps paying

Let's make the question concrete, because it's the question this chapter exists to ask. Look at your own week. Somewhere in it is something you do once, and then it's gone. And somewhere in it is something you do once, and it keeps paying. The lesson you teach. The note you write down. The person you help without being asked. The habit at the margin. Find the things that keep paying, and feed them first.

Notice what this says about everything that came before it. A brand that compounds trust gets stronger every year. A network that compounds users gets harder to beat every year. A skill that compounds opens better doors every year. Every multiplier in this part of the book feeds the final one, because time multiplies all of them.

Trade multiplies your reach. Markets price your value. Leverage multiplies your effort. Networks multiply your users. But compounding multiplies all of it, because it's the one that keeps working while you sleep, year after year.

## The takeaways

1. **Time is the final multiplier.** Compounding is value creating value. The rate matters, but the time horizon is the whole game: a good rate over a long time beats a great rate over a short one.
2. **Protect the streak.** The early curve looks flat, and cashing out is tempting. Start early, never interrupt it unnecessarily, and remember that every interruption costs you the doublings that would have come after.
3. **Pick snowballs that don't melt.** Skills, reputation, relationships and trust all compound; so does debt, in the wrong direction. Find the things in your week that keep paying, and feed them first.

## Try this

Make two short lists from last week. On one, the things you did once that were gone the moment they were done. On the other, the things you did once that will keep paying: a note, a lesson, a favor, a habit. Choose one item from the second list and give it more of your time this week.

Everything you've built in this book compounds, if you let it. Build the snowball. Point it downhill. Protect the years. That's how the small becomes the enormous: one grain of rice, doubled across 64 squares. And there's one multiplier left, the strangest of all: generosity. It starts by giving value away before anyone has asked for it, and it's where the final part of this book begins.
