# Networks

In 1844, Samuel Morse tapped out his famous telegraph message from Washington to Baltimore. It was a marvel of the age: copper wire, electromagnetic clicks, words traveling faster than any horse. And for a while, that line connected exactly two places on Earth. The machine was extraordinary. The network was worth almost nothing, because there was almost nobody on it.

The fax machine tells the same story in miniature. Picture the very first one, sitting in an office like a paperweight. Then a second fax machine is sold, and the first one turns into a business.

That's the subject of this chapter: products that get more valuable the more people use them, and the strange economics underneath, where the users are the ones manufacturing the value.

## Who else is on it

Here's the principle in one line. A product's value isn't just what it does. It's who else is on it.

Every new user of a network doesn't add one unit of value. They add value to every user who was already there. The hundredth person to join makes the previous 99 richer, just by showing up.

That's why networks seem to appear out of nowhere and then feel inevitable. The value was being built by the crowd, quietly, one arrival at a time.

## The device never mattered

Go back to where it started. When Alexander Graham Bell first showed off the telephone, a common reaction from would-be investors was polite dismissal. It was a toy for the rich, they said, a curiosity for people with money to burn.

They were staring at the device, which meant they were staring at the wrong thing. The device never mattered. What mattered was that within a few decades, a telephone in your house meant you could reach anyone who also had one. Bell's company didn't really sell mouthpieces and earpieces. It sold the size of the crowd. Every new subscriber was a gift to all the existing ones.

That's the first rule of networks. Value lives in the connections, not in the nodes. A telephone with one subscriber is a sculpture. A telephone with a million subscribers is a necessity. Same machine, completely different value.

> Value lives in the connections, not in the nodes.

And here's the part most people miss. The price of the product can stay fixed while its value keeps rising, because the users are doing the manufacturing. Nobody at the phone company had to invent a new feature to make your phone more valuable. Your neighbors joining did it for free.

## When old machines wake up

The fax machine makes the mechanism visible. As the story is usually told, the company that built the first commercial fax machines nearly gave up. It sold a handful of units, and the buyers complained. Not because the machine was bad. Because there was nobody to fax.

Then a few large companies bought the machines in bulk, for use inside their own offices. Suddenly every fax machine that had ever been sold became more useful, including the ones sitting in offices that had already given up on theirs. No software update. No new feature. No price change. Just more people on the network, and the old machines woke up as if they'd been plugged in for the first time.

This is the moment network builders live for. The product you already sold gets better after the sale, and you didn't lift a finger.

Once you see it, you see it everywhere. A marketplace with ten sellers is a ghost town. A marketplace with 10,000 sellers is a destination, and every buyer who arrives makes it more attractive to the next seller, who makes it more attractive to the next buyer. Each side feeds the other. A language nobody speaks is trivia. A language a billion people speak is a career. The value was never in the dictionary. It was in the speakers.

## The dark mirror

Now here's the trap, the part the cheerleaders leave out. Networks have a dark mirror. The same mechanism that makes them valuable makes them fragile.

Every new user adds value, but only if they add signal. When the new arrivals bring noise, spam, scams or cruelty, the network gets bigger and worse at the same time. More users, less value.

A network is a commons. It rewards the builder who protects it and quietly punishes the one who just counts heads. The platforms that last are the ones that learned to defend the conversation, not just grow the crowd.

## Seed where people gather

There's a second story here, and it's about starting, because every network begins as two paperweights.

The fax company solved it by selling to big companies first, so each buyer was really buying a small network, not a single machine. The telephone companies ran their wires down the busiest streets first, where each new subscriber could immediately reach the most people. The lesson is always the same. Don't seed a network where people are scarce. Seed it where people are already gathered, so the very first users feel the value on day one and bring the second wave with them.

So here are three rules, whether you're building something or choosing something to join.

**First, build for the second user, not the first.** If your product is only useful when other people join, then your real product is the invitation: the reason to bring someone along.

**Second, seed where people gather.** The cold start is the whole game. Borrow someone else's crowd before you try to build your own.

**Third, protect the signal.** The moment the network fills with noise, the value drains out of every single connection, and no feature, no redesign and no marketing budget will put it back.

The most valuable thing you can build isn't a product people use alone. It's a place people bring each other. That's why the winners look inevitable in hindsight. They weren't inevitable. They were just the ones who understood early that value lives in the crowd, and they built accordingly.

## The takeaways

1. **A product's value includes who else is on it.** Every arrival adds value to everyone already there, because value lives in the connections, not in the nodes.
2. **Networks have a dark mirror.** When newcomers bring noise instead of signal, the network gets bigger and worse at the same time. Protect the signal, or watch the value drain out of every connection.
3. **Build for the second user, and seed where people gather.** Your real product is the invitation. Start where the crowd already is, so the first users feel the value on day one.

## Try this

Think of a product or group you use because other people are on it. Ask what made you join: who was already there, and who invited you? Then look at something you're building and ask the same question about it. What's the reason someone would bring a friend?

Every user who arrives makes a network better for everyone already there. That's the compounding engine of networks: value multiplying across people. But there's one more multiplier, and it works along a different axis. Networks multiply value across a crowd. The next idea multiplies it across time.
