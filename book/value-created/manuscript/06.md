# Every Value Starts as a Problem Solved

Three before-and-afters.

Before: you're standing in the rain, locked out of your car, keys on the driver's seat, phone at 4 percent. After: the locksmith's tool slides the lock, the door opens, and you're dry and moving again. Twenty minutes of misery, gone. You would have paid triple.

Before: a restaurant dining room sits empty on a Friday night while the owner stares at the books, rent due Monday. After: one honest review from the right critic, and there's a line out the door. Same room. Same food. The problem was invisibility, and now it's solved.

Before: a hospital nurse copies patient charts by hand, 12 hours a shift, errors creeping in when she's tired. After: a simple software tool, the charts fill themselves, and the errors vanish. Same nurse. Same patients. The problem was wasted hours, and now they're gone.

Three different worlds, one pattern. Nobody bought a slim jim, a review or a software tool. They bought the end of a problem. And that brings us to the sentence this whole book reduces to:

Every value ever created started as a problem solved.

## The whole discipline in one line

Say it as a formula. Value equals the problem solved, minus the cost of solving it. That's the entire discipline in one line, and everything else in this book is commentary on it.

> Find a real problem, solve it well, charge less than the pain it caused, and you've created value.

There is no other source. There never was.

Test it against the fortunes. The shipping container didn't invent trade. It solved the problem that loading a ship took days and broke things. The barcode didn't invent selling. It solved the problem that checkout was slow and inventory was guesswork. The search engine didn't invent curiosity. It solved the problem that finding anything took forever.

Go further back. The plow solved the problem that human backs couldn't feed a village. The printing press solved the problem that books took a year to copy by hand. The vaccine solved the problem that children died of things nobody could see. Every one of them looks like an invention from the outside. From the inside, every one is a rescue.

## The problem comes first

Notice the shape. The problem comes first, always. Nobody woke up wanting a shipping container. They woke up with cargo rotting on the dock. The needs, wants and pain from the last chapter are all just problems wearing different clothes. A need is a problem the body has. A want is a problem the heart has. Pain is a problem with a clock on it.

Which means the creator's job is not to invent. It's to notice. The locksmith didn't invent locks. The locksmith noticed that people lock themselves out every day, predictably, and that the moment of being locked out has a price attached to it. Attention to problems is the whole skill. Invention is just what happens after you stare at a problem long enough.

Here's where most people get it backwards. They start with a solution and go hunting for a problem. The app is built, the product is polished, the pitch deck is beautiful, and then comes the terrible question: who is this for? That's solution-first thinking, and it's why so many startups die with great technology and no customers. The problem was never found. Only the solution was built.

Problem-first thinking runs the other way. Live inside the problem before you touch the solution. The software that freed the nurse wasn't designed in a meeting room. Someone watched her copy those charts for days, felt how heavy the twelfth hour was, and only then asked what could lift it. The watching came first. The building came second. Always in that order.

There's a test for whether you've found a real problem, and it's brutal. Would someone pay you to make it stop, today? Not download your app. Not try your free trial. Pay, today, with real money, to end this specific misery. If the answer is yes, you have a problem. If the answer is "maybe later," or "that's interesting," or "let me think about it," you have a hobby with a logo.

## Problem times people

The size of the value tracks the size of the problem, multiplied by how many people have it. A headache pill solves a small problem for billions of people. A cancer drug solves an enormous problem for far fewer. Both are fortunes, because problem times people is the whole arithmetic of value. Small problem, huge crowd. Huge problem, small crowd. Either one works, as long as the multiplication is real.

But there's a trap inside the arithmetic, and you met it early in this book, with the first glass of water and the tenth. The tenth headache pill is worth less than the first. Problems get solved, and solved problems stop paying. The locksmith's best day is the city's worst lockout day. Once everyone has a spare key hidden somewhere, the emergency fades. Value isn't a permanent property of the solution. It's a temporary property of the unsolved problem.

That's why the great creators don't solve one problem. They build a machine for finding problems. The plumber fixes your pipe, notices your water heater is dying, then offers the maintenance plan. The software company ships the tool, watches where users still struggle, then ships the next tool. One problem is a job. A pipeline of problems is a business.

## Finding problems worth your life

So how do you find problems worth your life? Three moves.

**First, live where the problems are.** Not in the brainstorming session, but in the nurse's twelfth hour, the restaurant's empty Friday, the rain outside the locked car. Problems don't announce themselves in meetings. They leak out in complaints, workarounds and the things people apologize for.

**Second, listen for the workaround.** Every workaround is a problem wearing a disguise. The spreadsheet three people maintain by hand because the real system can't do it. The drawer full of phone chargers because the battery never lasts. The notebook where the nurse writes down what the software can't capture. Wherever people have built a clumsy bridge, there's a river that needs a real one.

**Third, price the problem before you price the solution.** Ask what the problem costs per day, per month, per year, while it stays unsolved. The empty restaurant loses a Friday. The hand-copied charts lose hours and risk lives. The locked car costs the meeting, the flight, the day. The cost of the unsolved problem is the ceiling on what your solution can charge. Know that number before you build anything.

Now a warning. Not every problem wants your solution. Some problems are load-bearing. The couple that argues about the dishes doesn't have a dish problem. The company with the toxic manager doesn't have a process problem. Solve the wrong problem brilliantly and you'll be resented, not paid. Make sure the problem you see is the problem they feel.

And some problems aren't yours to solve. The test isn't whether you can see the problem. It's whether you're the one who should fix it. The nurse's charts needed someone who had watched the twelfth hour. The locked car needed someone already nearby with the tools. Proximity is part of the solution. The best problem for you is the one you're already close to, already understand and are already equipped to end.

## The whole book in one sentence

Take stock of the climb so far. Value lives in people, not in things. It lives at the margin: this person, this moment, this one more unit. It wears two faces: what it does for you and what others will trade for it. Its price is a rumor about the exchange face, told by whoever happened to be in the room. And it springs from three wells (needs, wants and pain), and the deepest well is the one that hurts right now.

Every one of those ideas was about *seeing* value. This one is where creating it begins. Every value ever created started as a problem solved. Find the problem first. The solution is just what happens after you stare at it long enough.

So here's what to carry with you. Stop asking what you can build. Start asking what's broken. Every product you love, every business you admire, every fortune that looks like genius from the outside is a solved problem in disguise. The container, the barcode, the search box, the vaccine. Find the problem, end it well, charge less than the pain. That's the whole book in one sentence.

## The takeaways

1. **Value equals the problem solved, minus the cost of solving it.** Find a real problem, solve it well and charge less than the pain it caused. There is no other source of value.
2. **The problem comes first, always.** The creator's job is to notice, not to invent. Live inside the problem before you build, and test it with one question: would someone pay to make it stop, today?
3. **Stop asking what you can build. Start asking what's broken.** Every fortune that looks like genius from the outside is a solved problem in disguise.

## Try this

Spend one day listening for workarounds: the complaints, the clumsy spreadsheets, the things people apologize for. Write down three. For each one, ask what it costs per month while it stays unsolved, whether someone would pay to make it stop today, and whether you're close enough to it to be the one who fixes it.

Once you know that value starts as a problem solved, an honest question follows. What does the solving actually cost you? Not in money, but in what you really spend: your time, your effort and your skill. Most people budget the first two and ignore the third, which is exactly backwards, because only one of the three grows the more you spend it.
