# What Value Actually Means

Here's a riddle. A diamond is, when you get right down to it, a shiny rock. You can't eat it. You can't drink it. It won't keep you warm at night. And yet people pay fortunes for it.

Meanwhile water, the stuff you literally cannot live without, flows out of your tap for a fraction of a penny.

So which one is more valuable? Your gut says water. Obviously. It's life itself. But the market says the diamond, and the shiny rock wins by a mile. How can that be?

The question has a name. Economists call it the diamond-water paradox, and Adam Smith made it famous in 1776, in *The Wealth of Nations*. It isn't a dusty puzzle from an old textbook. How you answer it decides whether you actually understand what value is. And if you don't understand what value is, you can't create it on purpose.

By the end of this chapter, you'll never look at a price tag the same way again.

## The trap inside the riddle

The trap is assuming that value lives inside things: that a diamond has value the way it has weight, as if it were a physical property baked into the rock itself.

It feels natural to think that way. A diamond is rare, it's hard, it sparkles, and those qualities seem to be what make it valuable. Water is everywhere, it's plain, it falls from the sky, so it feels cheap. The value seems to sit in the object.

Now take that same diamond, the exact same rock, and put it in front of someone dying of thirst in a desert. Suddenly the diamond is worthless to them. Not metaphorically. Literally. They would trade it in a heartbeat for a single glass of water. And that plain, ordinary water is suddenly the most precious thing in the world.

Here's the key part. Nothing about the water changed. Nothing about the diamond changed. The molecules are identical. What changed was the person, and the situation that person was in.

So value can't be inside the thing. If it were, it wouldn't move around like that. The diamond didn't get less diamondy in the desert, and the water didn't get more watery. The only thing that changed was the human being, and what that human being needed in that moment.

That is the big idea of this chapter, and maybe of this whole book.

> Value is not in things. It is in people.

## Three ingredients

If value lives in people and not in things, the next question is obvious: what makes something valuable to a person? You can build the answer from three ingredients.

**The first is desire**, or need, if you want the stronger word. Something is valuable to you when it does something for you, when it moves you from where you are to somewhere better. A glass of water has enormous value to the thirsty person because it ends their suffering. It has almost no value to someone who just drank a full bottle, because there's nothing left for it to do. Same water. Different person, different moment, different value.

You can feel this with the most ordinary objects. Think about your phone charger. Sitting in a drawer at home, it's worth almost nothing to you. But say your phone is at 1 percent, you're in an airport, and your boarding pass is on that phone. Suddenly the same charger is the most valuable object in the terminal. The charger didn't change. You did.

**The second is scarcity.** Not how rare something is in the universe, but how hard it is for this person, right here, right now, to get it. Water in your kitchen is nearly free because it's abundant and effortless. Water in the desert is priceless because it's nearly impossible to find. The water didn't change. Its availability to you did.

Diamonds are the mirror image. A diamond is valuable in a jewelry store, and not because it's useful. It's valuable because it's scarce *and* because people desire it. Take away either one and the value collapses. If diamonds were as common as gravel, nobody would pay a fortune for them, however beautifully they sparkled. And if no human being on earth cared about shiny rocks, they'd be worthless too, however rare they were. Rarity alone isn't enough. Desire alone isn't enough. You need both, meeting in a person.

**The third is context**: the situation, the moment. An umbrella is worth a fortune in a downpour and nothing on a sunny day. A warm meal is precious to someone who is hungry and barely interesting to someone who just ate. The thing stays the same. The context moves the value.

There's your recipe. Desire: what it does for the person. Scarcity: how hard it is for them to get. Context: the situation they're in. Move any one of the three and the value moves, even though the object never changed at all.

## The first glass and the tenth

There's a sharper version of this, and you've lived it. Picture yourself lost in a desert, parched and desperate. Someone hands you a glass of water. How much is that first glass worth to you?

Everything. You'd give anything for it.

Now you've had that glass. And another. And another. By the tenth glass, how much do you care? Barely at all. You might not even finish it.

Same water, same you, same desert. But the first glass was everything and the tenth was nothing, because the first glass changed your situation dramatically. It took you from suffering to relief. The tenth glass changed almost nothing. You were already fine.

You don't need an equation to feel this. The first bite when you're starving versus the last bites when you're stuffed. The first day of a vacation versus the tenth. Each additional one matters less, because what it does for you keeps shrinking. Hold on to that feeling; the next chapter gives it a name and takes it much further. For now, the intuition is enough. Value isn't fixed. It slides around with how much you already have and how badly you still need more.

## Two objections

You might be thinking: wait a minute, diamonds are valuable because *everyone agrees* they're valuable. Isn't that value living in the thing, or at least in the market?

It's a good objection, but it actually proves the point. That agreement is just shared desire: millions of people wanting the same shiny rock at the same time. The value still lives in people, in their heads, in what they want. And shared desire can vanish. Tastes change, fashions die, and markets have collapsed because people simply stopped wanting what they once fought over. The object sat there, unchanged, while its value evaporated. The value was never in the object. It was in the wanting.

Here's another one that trips people up. If value is in people, can something free be valuable? Absolutely. Think about a stranger giving you directions when you're lost in a new city. Price: zero. Value: enormous. Or a friend talking you through a hard decision at midnight. Nobody charged you, but it changed your week.

## The change is the product

Pull the camera back and there's a thread running through everything so far, and the entire book hangs on it. If value lives in people, not in things, then what does it mean to *create* value?

It means you're not really in the business of making things. You're in the business of changing something for someone. The thing you make is just the vehicle. The change is the product.

It's worth saying twice. The thing you make is just the vehicle. The change is the product.

Nobody buys a drill because they love drills. They buy a drill because they want a hole in the wall. The drill is the vehicle. The hole, the shelf that finally goes up, the room that finally feels finished: that's the change, and that's what they were actually paying for.

Or think about a plumber at midnight. Your kitchen is flooding. You're not paying for pipes and wrenches. You're paying for the panic to end, to go from disaster back to normal. The pipes are the vehicle. The relief is the product.

Once you see it this way, a lot of confusion clears up. Why do two people pay different amounts for the same thing? Because it changes different things for each of them. Why does something valuable become worthless overnight? Because the person's situation changed, so there's nothing left for it to change.

This is also why creating value is a skill you can learn, not a lottery ticket you hope to win. If value were baked into things, creating it would mean stumbling onto the right thing, like finding a diamond in the dirt. Pure luck. But if value lives in people, creating it means understanding people. What do they want? What is scarce for them? What is their context right now? Those are questions you can investigate. Those are problems you can solve.

So here's the reframe to carry into the rest of this book. Stop asking, "What can I make?" Start asking, "What can I change for someone?" Who is thirsty, and what does their water look like?

## The takeaways

1. **Value is not in things; it is in people.** The diamond-water paradox only looks like a paradox if you believe value is a property of objects. The moment you see that value lives in the person, the paradox dissolves.
2. **Three ingredients decide what something is worth to a person.** Desire (what it does for them), scarcity (how hard it is for them to get) and context (the situation they're in). Move any one, and the value moves while the object stays the same.
3. **Creating value means changing something for someone.** The product is the vehicle. The change is what people actually pay for. If you remember one sentence from this chapter, make it that one.

## Try this

Pick one thing you paid for this week, anything from a coffee to a repair. Write down the vehicle (what you bought) and the change (what it did for you). Then ask which of the three ingredients, desire, scarcity or context, made it worth the price on that particular day.

The first glass of water is worth everything and the tenth is worth almost nothing. That gap hides a precise idea, and once you see it, you'll start spotting it everywhere: in prices, in business and in your own decisions. It's where we go next. For now, go find someone who is thirsty.
