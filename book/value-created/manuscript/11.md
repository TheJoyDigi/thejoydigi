# Attention Is an Asset

In October 1994, the first banner ad in history appeared on a website called HotWired. It was a small rectangle for the phone company AT&T, and it asked a simple question: "Have you ever clicked your mouse right here? You will." Forty-four percent of the people who saw it clicked. Nearly half.

Today, the average banner ad gets clicked by about 0.05 percent of the people who see it. Same rectangle. Same internet. So what changed?

Not the ads. Your attention got scarcer, because so much more is competing for it now.

## A poverty of attention

The attention business is older than the internet. In the 1830s, the penny press worked out that it could sell newspapers for a penny and sell its readers to advertisers for far more. Radio did it next. Television perfected it. The internet just industrialized it. And in 1971, the political scientist Herbert Simon gave the whole game its truest sentence: "A wealth of information creates a poverty of attention." He was 50 years early.

Think about what that poverty looks like today. More video is uploaded to the internet than you could watch in many lifetimes. Millions of podcasts. Billions of posts. An endless ocean of content, rising every day. And on the other side of that ocean sits a fixed number that will never change: 24 hours in a day, about 16 of them awake. That's your entire lifetime budget of attention, and every creator on earth is bidding for a slice of it.

The blunt economics make the point. In 2024, a 30-second commercial during the Super Bowl cost about $7 million. Do the division and that's more than $200,000 a second. Nobody was really buying television time. They were buying the one thing that can't be manufactured: a hundred million human minds, all pointed in the same direction at the same moment. Attention has never been cheap, and it has never been more expensive than it is right now.

When Reed Hastings, a co-founder of Netflix, was asked to name his biggest competitor, he didn't say Disney. He didn't say HBO. He said Netflix competes with sleep. Sit with that for a moment. One of the biggest entertainment companies on earth doesn't fear other entertainment companies. It fears your bedtime. Attention is finite, and every hour you give to one thing is an hour you can never give to another. Whether it knows it or not, every business is competing for your hours.

## The great inversion

This is the great inversion of the modern economy. For most of human history, the scarce thing was content. Books were rare. News traveled by horse. If you held information, you held power. Then the internet flipped the table. Information became free and infinite, and scarcity moved. It moved into the human mind. The scarce thing is no longer the message. It's the moment a mind actually receives it.

At the very start of this book, scarcity was one of the three ingredients of value. Attention is the purest scarcity in the economy today.

And wherever scarcity lives, a market forms. This one has a name: the attention economy. The business model is simple. The platform gives you the content for free. In exchange, it harvests your attention by the hour and sells it to advertisers by the slice. The users aren't the customers. The users are the inventory. There's an old saying in the tech world: if you're not paying for the product, you are the product being sold. What's actually changing hands is your attention.

## Earn it or steal it

None of this is a conspiracy. It's arithmetic. Attention is valuable, so rational businesses try to capture it. But there are two completely different ways to do that, and they lead to opposite destinations. You can earn attention, or you can steal it.

Earning it means giving someone something genuinely worth their minutes. Stealing it means hijacking their wiring: the outrage headline, the infinite scroll, the little red notification badge engineered to itch until you scratch it. Both work this quarter. Only one of them compounds.

Study the thieves first, because they teach by negative example. Clickbait promises what the article never delivers, and trains you never to come back. Outrage farming keeps you furious, because furious people don't log off. The feed learns what makes you react, not what makes you better, and serves you more of it forever. It's wildly profitable. It also burns the single most valuable asset in business, the one the next chapter is about: trust. Stolen attention always arrives with an invoice, and the invoice always comes due.

The earners play the opposite game. The teacher whose class nobody skips. The writer whose letter you actually open. Picture the local plumber with a three-week waiting list who has never run a single ad. Their customers did the marketing for them, one kept promise at a time. Different trades, same secret. They treat your attention the way a good bank treats your money. They spend it carefully, they never waste it, and they return more than they took. So you keep depositing more.

## Three rules for earning it

If attention is an asset, how do you build a position in it? Three rules.

**Rule one: earn it with value before you ask for anything.** Give first. The free sample. The genuinely useful guide. The help with no pitch attached. This is the whole philosophy behind real community building: show up useful, over and over, and attention follows on its own. Nobody owes you their focus. You rent it, and the rent is value delivered in advance.

**Rule two: respect it once you have it.** Attention is a loan, not a gift, and the interest rate on disrespect is brutal. The meeting that could have been a message. The video with 90 seconds of throat-clearing before the first real sentence. The newsletter that wastes your morning. Every wasted minute teaches your audience exactly one lesson: this person doesn't respect my time. And respect, once spent, is the most expensive thing in the world to buy back.

**Rule three: never steal it.** This is the bright line, and it's worth drawing in ink. No manufactured outrage. No bait and switch. No fake countdown timers, no dark patterns, no drama engineered to hold eyes that would rather be somewhere else. It's tempting because it works this quarter. But attention gained by theft converts into nothing that lasts. No loyalty. No trust. Nobody who stays. You rented a crowd and woke up owning an empty room.

## Your own attention

Now the deeper move, the one almost everyone misses. Your attention is an asset too, not just other people's. Where you place your focus is where your life actually goes.

Do a little math. Two hours a day of idle scrolling adds up to 730 hours a year. That's more than a full month of waking life, every single year, handed to strangers who sell it to advertisers. Guard your attention the way you guard your savings, because it buys the same things: your time, your judgment, your future.

Notice who understands this best. The people richest in attention are usually the stingiest with their own. They don't doomscroll. They decline the meeting. They protect long, unbroken stretches of focus, because they've done the math on what an hour of deep attention is actually worth. Scarcity cuts both ways. Your attention is scarce to the world, and the world's noise is infinite to you. Spend it like capital, not like pocket change.

And here's where it connects to the rest of the book. Every value starts as a problem solved, and attention follows the same law. People give their attention to whatever solves their problem, eases their pain or answers their question. The attention merchants of the last decade tried to cheat that law with tricks, and the tricks decayed. A 44 percent click rate became 0.05 percent. The law held. Give people something real, and the scarcest resource in the economy flows toward you.

So the whole chapter comes down to one fact, and that single fact reorganizes everything.

> Content is infinite. Attention is not.

## The takeaways

1. **Content is infinite; attention is not.** Information became free and endless, so scarcity moved into the human mind. Attention is now the purest scarcity in the economy, and whoever earns genuine attention can direct value.
2. **Earn attention, respect it and never steal it.** Give value before you ask, treat every minute as a loan and stay away from tricks. Stolen attention works this quarter, but whoever steals it pays for it eventually, in a currency that matters even more.
3. **Your own attention is an asset too.** Where you place your focus is where your life goes. Guard it like savings and spend it like capital, not like pocket change.

## Try this

For one day, keep a rough tally of where your waking hours go, and mark each block as earned (it gave you something real) or taken (it held your eyes and gave little back). Then look at one thing you make for other people and ask the same question of it: does it earn the minutes it asks for?

The currency that stolen attention spends, and earned attention builds, is trust. It never shows up on a balance sheet, it can't be bought, and it quietly decides whether everything else you create is worth anything at all. That's the ingredient we turn to now. Earn the attention, and keep the trust.
