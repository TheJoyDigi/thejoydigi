# Making Things People Want

In 2001, Dean Kamen unveiled the Segway, and the hype was unreal. Steve Jobs was reported to have said it could be as big a deal as the personal computer. Investors poured in a fortune, reportedly more than a hundred million dollars. The engineering was genuinely brilliant: gyroscopes, self-balancing, a machine that seemed to read your mind. By every technical measure, it was a better product than anything around it.

And then the world shrugged. After a decade, by most reports, Segway had sold only about 30,000 units in total. For the most hyped invention of its time, that was almost nothing. The Segway became a punchline, best known as the ride of choice for mall security guards. Brilliant engineering. Nobody wanted it.

The last chapter said knowledge is the raw material that weighs nothing. But raw material is useless until you aim it, and this is where most builders go wrong. They fall in love with the building and forget the wanting. The Segway team built their machine brilliantly. They just never checked whether anyone was asking for it.

## Building in a vacuum

This is the most expensive mistake in business, and it has a name: building in a vacuum. It goes like this. A founder has an idea in the shower. They spend a year building it in secret. They polish every detail. Launch day comes, with confetti and a press release, and then silence. Crickets. Not because the product is bad, but because nobody was waiting for it. They built a solution and went looking for a problem.

Now take the opposite story. In the 1970s, a man named Gary Dahl looked at pets and noticed something strange. People loved their pets, but pets were expensive, messy and demanding. So he sold them a rock. A literal rock, in a cardboard box with air holes, with a 32-page instruction manual. The Pet Rock. He sold over a million of them at about $4 each.

Was the Pet Rock a better product than the Segway? Technically, it's a rock. But it understood something the Segway team missed. People don't buy products. They buy what the product does for them. Nobody wanted a self-balancing transportation platform. But a million people wanted a funny gift, a joke to share, a little moment of delight for $4. The rock won on want. The Segway lost on want. Want is the whole game.

## The best fit wins

Here's the big idea of the chapter, the one worth tattooing on your forehead.

> The best product doesn't win. The best fit wins.

Fit means the thing you built matches a want that already exists in the world, one strong enough that people will reach for their wallets. You can't manufacture want with marketing. You can only discover it, the way a prospector discovers gold. It's already in the ground. Your job is to find where.

## Watch, then test small

So how do you find it? Here's the uncomfortable truth: not by asking people what they want. People are terrible at predicting their own behavior. Henry Ford supposedly said that if he'd asked people what they wanted, they would have said faster horses. Whether he said it or not, the point stands. What people say and what people do are two different countries.

What you do instead is watch. Watch what people already do, and where they struggle. The best product ideas come from watching someone do something the hard way and thinking, *there has to be a better way*. The founders of one famous vacation rental company got their start putting up conference visitors on air mattresses in their own apartment, because the city's hotels were sold out. They didn't invent the desire for a cheap place to sleep. They watched it happening and built the fit.

Then you build the smallest version that tests the want. Not the polished product. Not the full vision. The smallest thing that lets a stranger pay you, or sign up, or even just click. If nobody reaches for the small version, the big version won't save you. That's why smart builders charge early. A dollar of revenue tells you more about want than a thousand compliments. Compliments are free. Money is a vote.

## Who would grieve?

There's a second test, and it's brutal. If this disappeared tomorrow, who would complain? If the answer is nobody, you haven't made something people want. You've made something people tolerate. The products that win are the ones with real grief attached to their absence. Think of the app you would genuinely miss. That grief is the sound of fit.

Notice what this means for all the knowledge from the last chapter. Knowledge aimed at a real want becomes value. Knowledge aimed at nothing becomes a hobby. The Segway engineers weren't unskilled. They weren't ignorant. They were simply pointed at a want that didn't exist. Aim is everything. The arrow matters less than the target.

So stop asking, "What can I build?" Start asking, "What do people already want, and where are they settling for something worse?" Watch behavior, not opinions. Build the smallest test. Charge early. And measure everything by one question: if this vanished tomorrow, who would grieve? Build for the grievers.

## The takeaways

1. **The best product doesn't win. The best fit wins.** Fit means matching a want that already exists. You can't manufacture want with marketing; you can only discover it.
2. **Watch behavior, not opinions, then test small.** What people say and what they do are two different countries. Build the smallest version a stranger could pay for, and charge early, because money is a vote.
3. **Build for the grievers.** If it vanished tomorrow, who would miss it? Build the right thing, not just the thing right.

## Try this

Take one idea you've been sitting on and put it through the chapter's tests:

- Where have you actually *seen* people doing this the hard way, or settling for something worse?
- What is the smallest version a stranger could pay for, or sign up for, this week?
- If it vanished a month after launch, who exactly would grieve?

Fit is discovered by watching people closely. But some value can't be discovered, packaged or shipped at all. It can't be boxed or scaled. It has to be delivered by a human being, in person, with care, and that kind of value has a way of outlasting every machine built to replace it.
