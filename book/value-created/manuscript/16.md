# Pricing: Naming Your Worth

The story goes like this. In the 1950s, a market vendor sat down to lunch at a café in Paris and noticed Pablo Picasso doodling on a napkin at the next table. The man asked Picasso to draw something for him, right there, on the napkin. Picasso did. It took him maybe 30 seconds. Then he folded the napkin, slid it across the table and said, "That will be ten thousand francs."

The man laughed. "Ten thousand francs? For thirty seconds of work?"

"No," Picasso answered. "Forty years and thirty seconds."

It's the kind of tale that gets retold until nobody can say for sure how it happened. It has lasted because of what it gets right. That napkin had a price and it had a value, and the price had nothing to do with the 30 seconds. Most of us price like the man at the table. We count our costs, add a little margin and call that the price. Picasso priced like Picasso. He charged for the 40 years.

## The floor and the ceiling

Here's the idea at the heart of this chapter. Your costs decide what you *must* charge. The buyer's value decides what you *can* charge. And the distance between those two numbers is where fortunes are made or lost.

Almost every beginner prices backwards. They add up their time and their materials, tack on a polite margin and hope the customer agrees. That's called cost-plus pricing, and it's the most expensive mistake in business, because it has nothing to do with the customer. The customer never asked what it cost you. The customer is asking one question: what is this worth to me?

The answer to that question sets the ceiling on your price. Your costs set the floor. Most businesses live on the floor when they could be living near the ceiling.

## One pipe, two prices

Put the two pricing worlds side by side.

In the cost-plus world, you're a plumber who spends two hours fixing a burst pipe, so you charge for two hours plus parts, maybe $300. The homeowner, who was picturing a flooded kitchen and a ruined floor, would happily have paid $800. You just donated $500 to a stranger.

In the value-based world, you're the same plumber, and you quote the job by the problem it solves: the kitchen stays dry, the floor stays intact, dinner is on time. You charge $600, the homeowner feels relieved, and you go home with twice the money for the same two hours. Nothing about the work changed. Only the price did.

Notice the deeper truth hiding inside that story. When you charge by cost, you're telling the customer your work is a commodity, interchangeable hours. When you charge by value, you're telling the customer what they're actually buying: the outcome.

## What the buyer is really buying

This is why luxury goods break people's brains. A handbag might cost $200 to make and sell for $3,000. Critics call that a rip-off, and they're missing the point completely. The buyer isn't purchasing stitched leather. They're purchasing the feeling of arrival, the signal, the craft story, the 40 years. The bag's value lives in the buyer, the beholder, just as we saw early in this book. Cost-plus says the bag should sell for $250. Value-based says it sells for whatever the beholder's eye sees. The company that understands this keeps the margin. The company that doesn't gives it away and calls itself honest.

And here's the part nobody wants to hear. If you refuse to charge for value, the market won't reward you with gratitude. It will simply assume you're worth less.

A well-known line of experiments shows how deep this goes. People tasted the same wine under two price tags, one cheap and one expensive, and rated the "expensive" glass as better. Same wine. The price changed the taste. This is the price-quality signal: when people can't judge quality directly, they use price as the judge.

So your price isn't just a number. It's a sentence about your worth, spoken to the customer's subconscious. Price too low and you tell the world you're cheap. Price at the level of the value you deliver and you tell the world what you actually are. The market listens to that sentence more carefully than it listens to your marketing.

## Three rules for naming your worth

**Rule one: price the outcome, not the input.** Before you name a number, write down the problem you solve, in the customer's words. Then write down what that problem costs them if it stays unsolved. A consultant who saves a company a million dollars a year in wasted software shouldn't charge by the hour. She should charge a fraction of the million. A photographer who shoots a wedding shouldn't charge by the shutter click. She should charge for the only day that can never be repeated. Find the customer's pain in dollars, price against that, and your quote will look like a bargain even when it's 10 times your old rate.

**Rule two: offer tiers, and let the middle win.** Put three options on the table: a basic version, a standard version and a premium version. Most people pick the middle one, and the premium makes the middle look reasonable. Much of that is anchoring, and it isn't a trick. It's clarity. The customer doesn't know what a fair price is until you give them a frame. A single price is a guess. Three prices are a menu, and menus let people choose their own value.

Picture a freelancer who used to sell one website for $2,000. Now she sells three packages, at $1,500, $3,000 and $6,000. Same clients, same work, and most of them choose the middle. Her income nearly doubles, and the clients are happier, because they chose.

**Rule three: raise your prices until someone says no.** This is the rule everyone fears and nobody regrets. If every quote you send gets accepted, your prices are too low. That isn't success. That's a leak. The market is telling you that you left money on the table, and the only way to find the ceiling is to touch it. So raise your rates, 10 percent, 20 percent, and watch. Some clients will walk, and that's the point. The ones who stay are paying you closer to what you're worth, and you'll work fewer hours for more money.

Think of it as a courage tax that runs in reverse. It isn't paid by the people who raise their prices. It's paid by the people who never do: in exhaustion, in resentment, in the quiet knowledge that they're worth more than their invoice says.

## Stop flinching

There's one more move, the simplest in the chapter: stop apologizing for your price. The moment you discount before anyone asks, you've confessed that even you don't believe the number. State the price the way Picasso stated his, calmly, as a fact about the world. Forty years and thirty seconds. The customer can say yes or no. What they must not hear is you flinching.

> Pricing isn't arithmetic. It's autobiography.

Every number you name is you telling the world what you believe you're worth, and the world, being polite, will believe you. Name it low and you train everyone, including yourself, to expect little. Name it at the level of the value you create and something remarkable happens. The right customers appear, the wrong ones leave, and the work you were already doing finally pays what it was always worth.

## The takeaways

1. **Your costs set the floor; the buyer sets the ceiling.** Cost-plus pricing ignores the one question the customer is asking: what is this worth to me? The gap between floor and ceiling is where fortunes are made or lost.
2. **Price the outcome, not the input.** Charge for the problem you solve, not the hours it took. Your price is also a signal: people use it to judge quality they can't see for themselves.
3. **Name your worth without flinching.** Offer three tiers, raise your prices until someone says no, and never discount before you're asked. Every price is a sentence about what you believe you're worth.

## Try this

Take the job you do most often. Write down the problem it solves in your customer's own words, then write down what that problem costs them if it stays unsolved. Put that number next to what you charge today. Then sketch three tiers (basic, standard and premium) and imagine which one most people would pick.

There's a catch to all of this. You can only charge for the full value you create if nobody can swoop in tomorrow and undercut you. The moment a competitor can offer the same thing for less, your ceiling starts to fall. So what stops them? The businesses that last are the ones that are hard to copy, and what makes them hard to copy has a name.
