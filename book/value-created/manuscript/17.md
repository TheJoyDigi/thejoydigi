# Scarcity and Moats

In the late 1990s, a chef named Daniel opened a small restaurant on a quiet street in Chicago. The food was extraordinary. Within a year there was a line out the door every night, and the critics called it the best new restaurant in the city. Daniel was living the dream.

Then, 18 months later, a restaurant opened directly across the street. Same style of food. Same menu, almost dish for dish. Prices 20 percent lower. Within a year, two more copies opened on the same block. Daniel cut his prices to compete, then cut his portions, then cut his staff. Three years after the line out the door, he closed for good.

Now compare that with a bakery two neighborhoods over. Nothing fancy. But the owner had spent ten years learning the names, the birthdays and the usual orders of half the street. When a cheaper bakery opened next door, her customers didn't move. As one of them put it, "I'm not buying bread. I'm buying my morning."

Same city. Same kind of business. One had value and no defense. The other had value wrapped in something no competitor could copy overnight.

Here's the painful part of Daniel's story: he did everything right. He created enormous value. People loved his food. And it didn't matter, because nothing stopped anyone else from making the same food, one block away, for less money. Daniel built value. He never built a moat.

## Treasure and water

Here's the idea at the center of it all. Creating value is only half the game. The other half is keeping it.

Needs and pain, trust, removing friction, pricing: so much of this book so far has been about creating value. But value with no protection is a gift to your competitors. When we looked at attention, we said scarcity is value. A moat is manufactured scarcity: whatever makes you hard to replace, hard to copy or hard to leave.

The investor Warren Buffett made the idea famous. He looks for businesses with a wide moat around them, a durable competitive advantage that keeps rivals out.

Think of a castle. The castle is your business. The value you create is the treasure inside. The moat is the water around it. No moat, and anyone with a ladder walks in and takes the treasure.

And the order matters. Most founders obsess over the treasure and ignore the water. They perfect the product, launch to applause, and only then discover that applause isn't protection. The time to think about the moat is before you need it, not after the copies arrive.

## Six kinds of water

So what is the water made of? Only a few materials really work, and it helps to know them by name, because you can't build what you can't name.

**The first is secrets.** Coca-Cola has kept its formula secret for more than a century. Anyone can make brown sugar water. Nobody can make Coca-Cola, because nobody knows exactly what's in it. A secret is the cheapest moat to dig and one of the hardest to cross, as long as it stays secret.

**The second is network effects.** The first telephone was useless. The millionth telephone was priceless, because every new user made the network more valuable for everyone already on it. Marketplaces work the same way. Once all the buyers and all the sellers are in one place, a competitor has to move both sides at once, which is brutally hard. This is the widest moat in modern business, and also the hardest to dig from nothing.

**The third is switching costs.** Your bank, your accounting software, your phone number, the tool your whole team learned. Leaving would be such a hassle that you stay even when you're mildly unhappy. Every hour a customer invests in learning your product is another gallon of water in your moat.

**The fourth is brand**, which is really trust stored up over time. Brand gets its own chapter soon, because it deserves one.

**The fifth is scale.** If you make 10 million units, your cost per unit is lower than that of anyone making 10,000, and you can set prices nobody smaller can survive.

**The sixth is permission.** A license, a patent, a regulation that says only you may do this thing.

Each of these is a different kind of water. The wider the moat, the safer the treasure. And the best businesses rarely rely on just one. They layer them.

## Several shallow moats

Here's what layering looks like in the wild. Take a neighborhood coffee roaster. Anyone can buy the same beans. Anyone can buy the same roasting machine. But this owner has a roast profile he spent five years tuning, and he never wrote it down. That's a secret. Half his regulars are on a subscription, beans at the door every two weeks, and canceling would mean finding a new morning ritual. Those are switching costs. He knows 300 customers by name, and they bring their friends. Those are relationships. And the shop itself has become a local landmark with a 15-year reputation. That's brand.

Four shallow moats, layered, and together they're wider than any single deep one. A competitor with twice his money could copy any one of those layers. Copying all four at once, in the same location, with the same history, is a different job entirely.

That's the real lesson. You don't need the widest moat in the world. You need several moats that a competitor would have to cross at the same time.

## Every moat drains

Here's what nobody tells you about moats: they can be drained.

For decades, owning a taxi medallion in New York was one of the safest moats in business. The city issued only a fixed number. No medallion, no taxi. Medallions sold for more than a million dollars apiece. It was a government-built moat, wide and deep, and the owners collected rents behind it for generations.

Then a company with an app showed up and asked: what if the car isn't a taxi? Within a few years, medallion prices collapsed by about 80 percent. The moat wasn't attacked. It was made irrelevant. Technology drained it almost overnight, and no amount of lobbying filled it back up.

So the lesson isn't just to build a moat. It's to know that no moat lasts forever. Secrets leak. Patents expire. Networks get disrupted. Scale gets undercut. The castle needs its guards awake, always. A moat isn't a one-time achievement you finish and admire. It's a maintenance job. The moment you believe your moat is permanent is the moment it starts draining.

## The moat test

Moats aren't just for companies. If you're building anything (a business, a product, a career), run the moat test. It's one question: if a competitor with twice my money opened across the street tomorrow, what would still be mine?

Be brutally honest with yourself. If the answer is nothing, you're Daniel with the restaurant. You're creating value on borrowed time, and the copies are already coming.

Now rank whatever you do have:

- **Your secret recipe**, the process only you know.
- **Your relationships**, the customers who would follow you if you moved.
- **Your reputation**, the trust you built one kept promise at a time.
- **Your speed**, the ability to ship while competitors are still in meetings.
- **Your depth** in one narrow niche that looks too small for giants to bother with.

Most small builders will never have a patent or a network effect. But they can have the other moats, and the other moats are real. The bakery owner had no patent. She had mornings. Nobody could copy her mornings.

And here's the move most people miss entirely: dig the moat before you need it. While business is good, while the line is out the door, that's when you lock in the long lease, deepen the relationships, document the secrets, earn the reputation and learn the niche three levels deeper than anyone else. Daniel dug no moat while he was winning, so he had nothing to stand on when the copies arrived. Moats are built in good times and tested in bad ones. If you wait until the competitor opens across the street, you're already too late.

> Value gets you in the game. A moat keeps you in it.

Creating something people want is the entry fee. Protecting it is the whole rest of the career.

## The takeaways

1. **Value gets you in the game; a moat keeps you in it.** A moat is manufactured scarcity: whatever makes you hard to replace, hard to copy or hard to leave. Value with no protection is a gift to your competitors.
2. **Layer your moats.** Secrets, network effects, switching costs, brand, scale and permission are the materials. Several shallow moats that a rival must cross at the same time can beat one deep one.
3. **Dig before you need it, and never stop.** Moats are built in good times and tested in bad ones, and every moat can be drained. Never fall so in love with yours that you stop watching the horizon, because the water can always find a new way around.

## Try this

Once a week, ask yourself what would stop a well-funded copycat from eating what you built that week. Then widen your moat a little:

- Call a customer.
- Write down a process.
- Learn the thing one level deeper.

Small moats, dug consistently, become wide ones. Build the treasure, dig the moat, and keep digging.

Every moat in this chapter guards the treasure from the outside. But there's a quieter weakness that no rival has to exploit: a business that only works while you're standing in it. The deepest moat of all might be a business that doesn't need you there, where your effort has been turned into a process that runs on its own.
