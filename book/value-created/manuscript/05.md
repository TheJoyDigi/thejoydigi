# Needs, Wants, and Pain

Here are three moments from one ordinary life.

In the first, you're grocery shopping, a little hungry, and you buy a loaf of bread for $4. You barely think about it.

In the second, you walk past a shop window and see a leather bag: hand-stitched, beautiful, completely unnecessary. You stand there for 10 minutes doing math you'd never do for bread.

In the third, it's two in the morning, water is pouring through your kitchen ceiling, and you call an emergency plumber. The plumber names a price that would make you laugh at noon. At two in the morning, with water raining on your stove, you say yes before the sentence is finished.

Same wallet. Same person. Three completely different decisions. The bread was a reflex. The bag was a fantasy. The plumber was a rescue.

So what was different? Not the money, and not you. The difference was the well the value was drawn from. Every act of value creation in human history draws from one of three wells: needs, wants and pain.

## The first well: needs

Needs are the things you must have: food, water, shelter, sleep, medicine. Without them you suffer, and eventually you stop functioning. Needs are the oldest well. They're also the most crowded.

Needs are powerful, but they have a cruel property: they're mostly already met. In a modern economy, almost nobody is bidding for their first loaf of bread. They're choosing between 40 loaves. And when a need is met 40 ways, the sellers start competing, and competition turns the well into a commodity market.

So bread is deeply valuable and nearly worthless at the same time. Valuable because you must eat. Worthless because everyone sells it.

That's the paradox of the first well. Needs create enormous volume and thin margins. The farmer, the baker and the landlord all draw from it, and all of them fight over pennies. If you build only on needs, you'd better be the cheapest or the closest, because the customer isn't grateful. The customer is comparing.

## The second well: wants

Wants are the things you desire but don't require. The leather bag in the window. The sports car. The corner office with the view. The phone upgrade when your old phone works fine. Nobody needs these, and that's exactly why they're profitable.

Wants are where the margins live. A need has a ceiling, because the body only needs so much bread. A want has no ceiling, because desire has no ceiling. The bag isn't priced on leather and thread. It's priced on how its owner feels walking into a room with it: identity, status, beauty, belonging. You're not selling the object. You're selling the feeling of being the person who owns it.

But wants have their own cruel property, and marginal utility is knocking again. Desire fades at the margin. The sixth slice of pizza thrilled you less than the first, and the second designer bag thrills you less than the first one did. Wants are fashion, and fashion changes. This year's must-have is next year's embarrassment. Build on wants and you live on a treadmill, always inventing the next desire before the old one expires.

## The deepest well: pain

That brings us to the third well, pain: the things that hurt right now, and that you'd pay almost anything to make stop. The burst pipe. The toothache at two in the morning. The laptop with your whole business on it, frozen the night before the big presentation. The ransomware note on the screen.

Pain is the deepest well, and here's why. A need can wait until morning. A want can wait until payday. Pain can't wait. Pain has a clock, and the clock says now.

The person with water raining through the ceiling isn't comparing plumbers. They aren't reading reviews. They aren't negotiating. They're buying the fastest rescue available, at whatever price rescue costs.

Notice what happened to price sensitivity. At noon, that plumber's quote is outrageous. At two in the morning it's a bargain, because the price is no longer being compared with other plumbers. It's being compared with the cost of doing nothing: a flooded kitchen, a ruined floor, a night of no sleep. Against that, almost any number looks small.

This is the big idea of the chapter. Value tracks the intensity of feeling, and nothing a human feels is more intense than pain. We seem to be wired this way. Psychologists have studied it for decades, and one of their best-known findings is that a loss tends to feel roughly twice as heavy as a gain of the same size. The fear of loss usually beats the hope of gain.

Put the three wells in one line.

> Needs keep you alive. Wants make life sweet. Pain makes you act now.

Alive is negotiable, sweet is optional, but now is now. That's why the person selling relief from pain names the price, while the person selling bread takes whatever the market gives.

Look at the great fortunes and you'll see the third well everywhere. The locksmith at midnight isn't selling a $20 lock. The locksmith is selling the end of standing outside your own door in the cold. The dentist isn't selling 30 minutes of drilling, but the end of the worst night of your month. The cybersecurity company isn't selling software. It's selling the end of the nightmare where the business dies overnight.

And the deepest veins of pain are the ones that come back. A burst pipe happens once. Payroll comes every two weeks. Taxes come every year. Backups, security, accounting, insurance: recurring pain is why the subscription exists. Solve a pain that returns on schedule, and the customer doesn't buy once. They pay you, month after month, never to feel it again.

## Listening for pain

So how do you find the wells? Stop brainstorming products. Start listening for pain, with three questions.

**First: whose pain is this?** Name a real person, not a market. Markets don't feel pain; people do. If you can't name the person and describe their worst Tuesday, you haven't found a well. You've found a guess.

**Second: what does the pain cost them for every day it continues?** Not in feelings, in numbers. The flooded kitchen costs a floor. The frozen laptop costs the contract. A slow checkout page costs a store a percentage of every single day's sales, forever. Price your solution against the cost of the pain, not the cost of your effort. The plumber's wrench turns for 20 minutes. The flooded kitchen would have cost thousands.

**Third: would they pay to make it stop today, not someday?** Someday is where wants live. Today is where pain lives. If the answer is today, you've found the deepest well. If the answer is someday, you're selling a want. That's fine, but price it like a want, and don't be surprised when the customer sleeps on it.

## Real pain, not invented

A warning before you go digging. There are two ways to work the third well. Relieve real pain, and you're a creator of value. Manufacture fake pain, and you're something else.

You've seen the fake kind. The countdown timer that resets when you refresh the page. The warning that your computer has a virus, sold by the people who put it there. The rumor of scarcity, invented to force your hand. The last chapter taught you to hear rumors. Invented urgency is a rumor too, and customers learn to hear it.

Real pain is discovered. Fake pain is manufactured. The test is simple: if you stopped selling tomorrow, would the pain still be there? The burst pipe doesn't care about your marketing. The toothache doesn't need your funnel. Build on pain that exists without you, and the well never runs dry.

## The takeaways

1. **Value springs from three wells: needs, wants and pain.** Needs are crowded, so buyers compare. Wants are fickle, so desire fades and fashion moves on.
2. **Pain is the deepest well.** It's urgent and personal, and it's priced against the cost of doing nothing rather than against the competition. Pain that comes back on schedule runs deepest of all.
3. **Find the pain, and you've found the value.** Every business you admire, every product you can't live without, every fortune that looks like luck from the outside is a rescue operation in disguise. Someone found a real pain and ended it.

## Try this

Pick one problem you've noticed around you and run it through the three questions in writing. Name the person, and describe their worst Tuesday. Put a number on what each day of the pain costs them. Then ask whether they'd pay to make it stop today, or only someday.

Look back at the ground we've covered: value lives in people, it lives at the margin, it wears two faces, its price is only a rumor, and it springs from three wells, the deepest of which hurts right now. Put all of that together and you arrive at a single sentence that explains every product, every business and every fortune. Trace any of them back far enough, and you find one person, one pain and one problem, solved.
